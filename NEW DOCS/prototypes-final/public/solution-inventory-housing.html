SOLUTION INVENTORY · DOMAIN: HOUSING STATUS: PUBLISHED v0.4 · UPDATED APR 23

What's been tried for the affordable-housing problem, where, and what actually happened?

Before proposing anything new, this inventory maps what has already been tried — across jurisdictions, with conditions, results, and exportability. Most policy debate happens without this map; the assembly is the contribution.
SYMPTOM-LEVEL INTERVENTIONS · MANAGING DOWNSTREAM EFFECTS

First-time buyer down-payment subsidies

JURISDICTIONS: ON · BC · QC · 6 US STATES2010 – PRESENTSYMPTOM LEVEL
Conditions
Income caps; first-residence requirements; matched contributions or interest-free loans up to specified amount.
Results
Measurable increase in first-time-buyer transactions; price effect ranges from neutral to mildly inflationary in supply-constrained markets. Net wealth transfer to existing sellers.
Why it didn't shrink the problem
Subsidy increases purchasing power without changing supply curve; in inelastic markets, capitalization into prices captures most of the subsidy.
Exportability
Necessary conditions for non-inflationary effect: elastic supply curve. Without that, the subsidy simply transfers to sellers.
PROPONENTSHelps families enter the market who otherwise couldn't; reduces wealth-inequality transmission in early adulthood.
CRITICSPrice-inflationary; transfers public funds to existing homeowners; addresses no structural cause.
REAL DISAGREEMENT
Whether housing-affordability policy should target individual buyers (helping families enter) or market structure (changing what housing is). The disagreement is about scope of intervention, not about whether the program works on its own terms.
MECHANISM-LEVEL INTERVENTIONS · TARGETING PROXIMATE CAUSES

Vacancy and foreign-buyer taxes

JURISDICTIONS: BC · ON · NEW ZEALAND · AUSTRALIA · UK2016 – PRESENTMECHANISM LEVEL
Conditions
Annual tax (1–2%) on properties left vacant or owned by foreign buyers. Self-declared with audit; declarations enforced by penalty.
Results
BC: measurable reduction in foreign-buyer transactions, marginal price effect on luxury segment, no significant effect on affordability. Vancouver vacancy rate reduced from 1.0% to 0.7%. NZ ban: similar pattern.
Why it partially worked
Reduces a marginal demand source (foreign capital). The reduction is real but marginal because foreign buyers were never the dominant demand source.
Exportability
Works as targeted intervention. Should not be expected to solve affordability; effect-size matches the demand-share it removes.
PROPONENTSRemoves speculative demand; signals that housing is for residents.
CRITICSMarginal effect; can be politically inflammatory; addresses symptom not cause.
REAL DISAGREEMENT
Whether the political signal value of these taxes is worth the analytical-distraction cost — they consume policy attention that could be directed at structural causes. Both sides agree the direct effect is small.

Zoning reform / "missing middle" upzoning

JURISDICTIONS: AUCKLAND · MINNEAPOLIS · TORONTO · VANCOUVER · CALIFORNIA2016 – PRESENTMECHANISM LEVEL
Conditions
Eliminate single-family-only zoning; allow duplex/triplex/fourplex by-right; reduce parking minimums; streamline approvals.
Results
Auckland (most-studied case): substantial supply increase across upzoned areas; rent growth 12–28% lower than control areas. Minneapolis: mixed; supply increase but limited price effect. Vancouver: minimal price effect to date.
Why it worked in Auckland but not Vancouver
Auckland's reform was paired with infrastructure investment and absorbed available capital; Vancouver's reform competes with extant investment-demand pressures the reform doesn't address.
Exportability
Necessary condition: supply elasticity is the binding constraint in the target market. Sufficient only when investor-demand component is small.
PROPONENTSAuckland evidence is strong; reforms work where supply is the constraint.
CRITICSAuckland's success doesn't generalize where investor demand dominates; risks transferring affordability to peripheral markets.
REAL DISAGREEMENT
An empirical question — what fraction of Canadian urban demand is investor-driven versus shelter-driven — that has not been adequately measured. Without that measurement, both proponents and critics are arguing from priors.
STRUCTURAL-LEVEL INTERVENTIONS · ADDRESSING ROOT CONDITIONS

Land-value taxation

JURISDICTIONS: ESTONIA · SINGAPORE (PARTIAL) · DENMARK · PA (US, PARTIAL)1916 – PRESENTSTRUCTURAL LEVEL
Conditions
Tax on unimproved land value rather than property value. Improvements (buildings, renovations) untaxed or lightly taxed.
Results
Pittsburgh (1913–2001): demonstrably reduced speculation, encouraged dense development. Estonia: similar pattern, with substantial new construction in formerly-vacant urban land. Reduction in speculative landholding patterns where implemented.
Why it works
Removes the speculative-asset character of land. Land cannot be hidden, moved, or hoarded productively. Burden falls on landholders rather than producers.
Exportability
Requires assessor capacity (separating land value from improvement value). Politically difficult in jurisdictions where landholders are concentrated and politically organized.
PROPONENTSTheoretically sound (Henry George, Stiglitz, multiple Nobel economists). Empirical record where implemented is consistent with predictions.
CRITICSImplementation challenges; political resistance; risk of over-taxing land-rich-cash-poor households (e.g., elderly homeowners on fixed incomes).
REAL DISAGREEMENT
Whether the political and administrative obstacles can be overcome in a Canadian context. The economics is broadly accepted; the political economy of implementation is the contested question.

Reform of mortgage securitization architecture

JURISDICTIONS: ICELAND (POST-2008) · IRELAND (PARTIAL)2008 – PRESENTSTRUCTURAL LEVEL
Conditions
Restructure the relationship between bank balance sheets, public mortgage insurance (CMHC equivalent), and securitization markets. Iceland's post-2008 housing reset is the closest live case.
Results
Iceland post-crisis reset reduced household debt-to-income substantially and stabilized housing prices at lower-relative levels. Achieved through a one-time crisis intervention, not normal-times reform.
Why few jurisdictions have tried this
In normal times, the political coalition for restructuring is absent. In crisis, the technical complexity exceeds capacity of reactive policy-making. Iceland succeeded because the crisis was severe enough to overcome both obstacles.
Exportability
Conditions for non-crisis implementation are largely unknown. Most relevant precedents come from post-crisis contexts that we hopefully don't replicate.
PROPONENTSAddresses the actual structural cause (credit-creation architecture) rather than its symptoms.
CRITICSPolitically nearly impossible outside crisis. Risks introducing new instabilities. Existing system, while flawed, is at least stable.
REAL DISAGREEMENT
Whether structural reform can be undertaken proactively (before crisis forces it) or whether crisis is the only forcing function strong enough to overcome the political coalition for the status quo. If the latter, the policy-design question is what reforms should be staged so they can be enacted when crisis arrives.

Capital-gains tax on primary residences

JURISDICTIONS: NONE FULL · UK (PARTIAL) · MOST OECD: NONEHISTORICAL · CONTEMPLATEDSTRUCTURAL LEVEL
Conditions
Tax capital gains on primary residence sale, eliminating the current Canadian principal-residence exemption. Could be partial (above threshold) or full.
Results
No major jurisdiction has fully implemented this on primary residences. UK partial-CGT: limited effect because thresholds remain well above typical home values.
Why none have tried
Politically toxic. Existing homeowner wealth in Canada is concentrated in primary residences; political class is property-wealthy; institutional resistance is total. No major party has proposed it.
Exportability
Currently unknown. The intervention exists in policy literature but not in implementation.
PROPONENTSRemoves the most distortionary tax preference in the Canadian system. Reduces housing's investment character at the source.
CRITICSPunishes ordinary households that have planned around the existing system. Implementation would require generation-long phase-in. Politically untenable.
REAL DISAGREEMENT
Whether the policy goal should be making housing not-an-asset (in which case capital-gains taxation is necessary), or preserving housing-as-asset while addressing affordability through other means (in which case capital-gains taxation is counter-productive). This is a Layer 5 values dispute, not a Layer 4 empirical one.
