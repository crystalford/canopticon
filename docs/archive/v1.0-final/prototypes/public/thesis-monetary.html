The architecture of quiet power: how Canadian monetary design shapes political outcomes.

The housing crisis, the bank oligopoly, the conflicts of interest at the top of Canadian financial leadership are not separate stories. They are instances of a single structural condition: monetary system design that concentrates credit creation in a small number of institutions whose interests then shape the political system supposed to regulate them.

Three apparently-separate Canadian problems — housing-affordability collapse, the Big Five bank oligopoly, and the revolving-door pattern at senior monetary-policy roles — have a single structural source. The 1974 Bank Act amendments and 2002 securitization changes redesigned credit-creation architecture in ways that concentrate creation in five institutions while making housing the dominant asset class for that creation. Every actor downstream — from the political class to the median household — now has material interest in preserving that architecture. The system's design is its policy.

Layer decomposition · 4 layers

FACTUAL · 24 CLAIMS · 19 GRADE-A 7 LINCHPINS

The factual layer assembles documented events: the 1974 Bank Act amendments, the 1992 amendments, the 2002 securitization-treatment changes, the personnel-transition record between Big Five banks and the BoC/Finance Canada/PMO across 28 documented moves, and the contract-allocation patterns that follow each transition. All A-grade or B-grade where primary documents exist; nothing inferred.

Anchoring claim: 1974 Bank Act amendments · 2002 securitization changes · 28 documented personnel transitions

FRAMING · 8 CLAIMS · MIXED GRADE 2 LINCHPINS

The dominant framing of these issues in Canadian discourse treats each as a separate story with separate causes. The framing layer documents how this fragmentation gets produced — through the propagation of the supply-side housing framing (see genealogy), the framing of bank concentration as efficiency, and the framing of revolving-door appointments as expertise-utilization. Each framing has institutional sponsors and is reproducible across media.

ARCHITECTURAL · 11 CLAIMS · 7 GRADE-A 5 LINCHPINS

The architectural layer maps the structural pattern: credit creation concentrated in five institutions; mortgage origination as the dominant credit-creation activity; CMHC's securitization role removing the constraint on bank balance sheets; senior personnel rotating through the regulator, the central bank, and the regulated banks. Together these constitute a tightly-coupled system. Most policy debate does not engage at this level.

DISTRIBUTIVE · 4 CLAIMS · 3 GRADE-A 1 LINCHPIN

The distributive layer documents who pays and who receives. Net result of the architecture: wealth transfer from non-property-owning Canadians to property-owning Canadians; concentration of housing-asset gains in older cohorts; reduction of housing-as-shelter to housing-as-investment-class for the median Canadian household. The distributional pattern is monotonic across the period and well-documented.
