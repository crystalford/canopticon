This method turns the theory of [the mechanism chain](/theories/the-mechanism-chain) into a procedure. Use knowledge, what a thing does and how to operate it, feels like knowing how it works. This method makes the mechanism into the form people already hold well: an ordered list.

## What it rests on

- **The overestimate shows only when people explain.** People rate their understanding of zippers, toilets and locks well above what they can write out step by step, and the rating falls after they try. The illusion is much weaker for procedures, which are already ordered steps.
- **A mechanism is parts and actions in order.** The standard definition in the philosophy of science describes a mechanism as entities and activities organised from a starting condition to a finish, and people work out how a machine moves by following its chain one link at a time.
- **Parts and order teach.** Diagrams of a system's parts and the sequence of events improved conceptual recall and problem solving for learners new to a topic, and learning the parts first helped further.
- **Rebuilding from memory makes it last and shows the gaps.** Recalling a passage beat rereading it after two days and after a week.

The studies, their numbers and what was read of each are on the [theory's page](/theories/the-mechanism-chain).

## Steps

1. **Name the result and the starting condition.** What the thing does, and what sets it going.
2. **List the parts that act.** Leave out the ones that only hold things in place.
3. **Give each part its action, in order.** From the starting condition to the result. Check the order against a reliable source.
4. **Compress each link to one cue word.** A part or an action, whichever brings the link back.
5. **Learn the parts and their actions first**, before any detail.
6. **Rebuild from the cue words alone.** Out loud or on paper, without the source.
7. **Mark the break point and fix that link.** Reread only the link that failed, then rebuild the whole chain again on a later day.

## The instruction

Paste this, followed by the thing you want to understand (and a source on it if you have one), into a conversation with a language model, or download it as a skill below.

```text
Build a mechanism chain: hold how something works as a short chain of parts and actions in causal order, one cue word per link, so it can be rebuilt from the words.

1. Name the result (what the thing does) and the starting condition (what sets it going).

2. List the parts that act. Leave out parts that only hold other parts in place.

3. Give each part its action, in order, from the starting condition to the result. One link is one part doing one thing. If the mechanism loops (a part that switches off what started it), say which link feeds back to which.

4. If a source was given, check the order against it and quote the sentence each link rests on. If none was given, say which links you are least sure of and what would confirm them. Do not invent parts.

5. Compress each link to one cue word: the part or the action, whichever would bring the link back. Show the chain as the cue words joined by " · ".

6. Write the explanation rebuilt from the chain, one short sentence per link, in order.

7. Then offer a rebuild test: ask the person to explain it back from the cue words alone, without looking. When they answer, mark the first link that is missing or wrong (the break point), explain only that link, and suggest trying the whole chain again later.

Keep it to the mechanism. Leave out history, brands and safety advice unless asked.
```

## Worked example

**A flush toilet.** Asked how a toilet works, the everyday answer is a few words in no order: water pressure, tank, flush, fill. Built as a chain:

1. Pushing the handle pulls a chain that **lifts** the flapper.
2. The tank's water **drops** into the bowl in about three seconds.
3. It fills the **siphon**, which pulls the bowl's contents down the drain.
4. The flapper **reseats** over the opening.
5. As the water level falls, the **float** falls with it and opens the fill valve.
6. The tank and bowl **refill**.
7. The rising float **shuts** the valve off. The last link feeds back to the fifth.

The chain: **lift · drop · siphon · reseat · float · refill · shut**. The everyday answer has the result and one part. It is missing the siphon, the flapper and the float, and those are the links where a rebuild usually breaks.

More worked examples, a zipper and a bicycle, are on the [theory's page](/theories/the-mechanism-chain).

## Where it fails

- **The chain can become its own illusion.** Knowing five cue words can feel like knowing the mechanism. The chain helps only when it is rebuilt from memory and checked against a source.
- **A chain can be learned wrong.** Experts in bicycles still made errors drawing one. Check the order against a reliable source, and treat a model's chain as a draft until you have.
- **Not everything needs it.** Coarse understanding is usually enough, and leaning on people who know is often the efficient choice. Use it where you want to understand, explain or fix the thing.
- **The procedure comparison may have other causes.** Procedures may be judged accurately for reasons besides being ordered lists, in which case a chain will help less than expected.
