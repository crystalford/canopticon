## McLuhan meant it literally

From: It is the ratio among our senses which is violently disturbed by media technology.
Caveat: The 1960 quotation has been checked against a secondary source, not yet against the original report.

In 1960, in a report to the US National Association of Educational Broadcasters, Marshall McLuhan wrote: "It is the ratio among our senses which is violently disturbed by media technology." He meant that as a claim about the nervous system. He grounded it in Hans Selye's stress physiology, and his critics attacked it on exactly those terms, as bad neurology.

Later he tied it to the theory of left and right brain hemispheres. When that theory failed, the claim was reclassified as metaphor. It was set aside more than it was refuted.

What has changed since is the measurement. Research on how the brain combines the senses shows that when two of them are combined, the weight each one gets depends on how reliable it is. That is a ratio in the literal sense. The series starts there, by taking McLuhan at his word.

## Every extension is also an amputation

From: McLuhan's pairing of extension and amputation is not a figure of speech here.
Caveat: The GPS finding is an association measured over three years in habitual users, not proof that GPS causes the decline.

McLuhan paired every extension with an amputation: whatever a medium strengthens, something else weakens. Across the six cases in this series, that pairing is the most consistent pattern in the evidence.

Habitual GPS users show weaker spatial memory over time. People remember less of what they photograph and let a device keep. Action video games sharpen visual timing, while slow, sustained reading goes unpractised. A drug restores a baseline that the drug itself shifted. Saving something frees memory for the next thing, and lets the saved thing go.

None of these cases is pure gain or pure loss. Whether a tool is good or bad is too coarse a question to answer. What it strengthens, and what it lets go, can be answered case by case, and the answers are specific.

## Small effects, whole environments

From: It is what turns a small shift into an environment.
Caveat: The claim is about scale and persistence, not about dramatic effects on any one person.

The effects in this research are small. Measured per person, they typically run a tenth to a third of a standard deviation. The links between technology and well-being account for at most a fraction of a percent of the variance. Read one study at a time, that looks like nothing.

But these effects do not happen to one person once. They apply to whole populations, daily, for years, and they accumulate. Spatial memory declines over three years of GPS use. Reduced social media use persists after the payment for reducing it ends. A hedonic baseline outlasts the drug that shifted it.

Of the five dimensions in the framework, persistence appears to be the decisive one. A small change that goes away is an event. A small change that stays becomes the environment, and the environment is the part people stop noticing.

## In addiction, the baseline is the medium

From: What becomes invisible is the shifted baseline: the medium, with the drug as its content.
Caveat: This concerns dependence-type addiction. It is a reading of the allostatic model, not clinical advice.

McLuhan built his account of media on stress physiology, on Hans Selye. The same tradition, through the concept of allostasis, produced the most influential modern model of addiction: Koob and Le Moal's account of a persistent shift in the brain's hedonic set point.

Put the two together and something specific follows. In dependence, the drug never becomes invisible. It stays vivid. What becomes invisible is the shifted baseline itself, which is felt as how the world is rather than as a state someone is in. That baseline is the medium. The drug is its content.

The paper on addiction describes two routes back. One turns reward gain down, as GLP-1 drugs and naltrexone appear to do. The other makes the ground briefly visible again.

## Recovery runs through other people

From: the configuration seems to re-temper best in company.
Caveat: The drug and recovery findings come from rats and mice. The listener argument is interpretation, not measurement.

The pattern I did not expect concerns how a configuration changes back.

Rats given a choice between social contact and heroin choose the contact, and crave less afterwards. In mice, psychedelics reopen a window for learning from social reward. Thinking aloud to a listener who responds runs Vygotsky's path in reverse: inner speech goes back to being social speech. In ADHD, inner speech is present but loosely coupled to action, and an outside listener may supply some of that coupling.

These are very different kinds of evidence, and none of them settles anything alone. They point the same way. The configuration seems to re-temper best in company. The ancient Greek word *anakrasis* meant mixing with others, and it turns out to describe recovery.
