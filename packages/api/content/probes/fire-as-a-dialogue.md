## The seed

I read a thing by McLuhan about fire being a kind of dialogue in itself. A scene or chapter for one of my scripts could explore that idea, and literally have it as part of the scene. There are two ways to put people by a fire:

1. They talk by a fire because that is a setting you could choose, sort of choose-your-own-adventure storytelling.
2. The scene deliberately explores a theme, and the theme, the setting, the dialogue and the meaning are all purposeful.

The fire is only one example. The real question is how to construct a narrative that explores actual themes this way, and how to actually do it.

## The question

In most stories the theme is what the story is about: the plot carries it, and sometimes a character says it. The setting is the background. In McLuhan's terms it is ground, and the characters and their talk are the figure.

The aim here is to turn the ground into a figure: to take a theme and build it into every layer of a scene at once, so that the setting, the objects, the structure, what people do with their hands and how they talk all carry it. The meaning is then in how the scene is made, as well as in what anyone says.

The nearest existing name is T. S. Eliot's objective correlative: "a set of objects, a situation, a chain of events which shall be the formula of that particular emotion" [2]. Eliot meant one emotion. This is a whole theme, carried on every layer of a scene, and worked out by writing the scene, so the story becomes a way of thinking the theme through.

## One theme through every layer

The theme here is dialogue, and the scene is people talking by a fire.

- **Setting: the fire changes what is said.** Polly Wiessner recorded conversations among the Ju/'hoansi of southern Africa by day and by firelight. By day, 34% of the talk was complaint and criticism and 31% was economic matters. At night, 81% of the long conversations involving many people were stories, mostly about people they knew [1]. The same people, with the same concerns, talked differently once the fire was lit. What the characters cannot say by day becomes sayable by it.
- **Structure: the fire's life is the scene's clock.** Kindling, blaze, embers, ash. Small talk while it catches, the argument at the blaze, stories and confessions in the embers, silence in the ash. The scene ends when the fire does, unless someone feeds it.
- **Action: someone has to tend it.** The character who keeps getting up to add wood is keeping the talk going. The one who lets it burn down wants it over. Neither has to say so.
- **A third voice: the fire answers.** Fed, it flares, spits and settles. It sets the pauses, and it gives people somewhere to look, so hard things can be said to the fire instead of to a face.
- **Thought: the fire as a mirror.** Gaston Bachelard wrote of the daydreaming fire induces: people watching flames lose themselves in them, thinking with no practical aim [3]. A character staring into the fire is half talking to themselves.

## From the fire to the screen

Wiessner found that firelight stories carried news of people who were not there, and built a sense of community beyond the people present [1]. Television took the hearth's place in the home; Cecelia Tichi tells that history in *Electronic Hearth* [4]. Around a fire people sit in a circle with the light in the middle. In front of a television they sit in a row, facing the light and away from each other. With a phone, each person has a light of their own.

Social media is one place firelight talk went: stories about people who are not there, told to people who are not there. The same layers apply to it, and each one changes:

- **Setting:** the light is in every hand at once, and the circle is scattered across a city.
- **Structure:** a feed has no embers. A fire burns down and ends the scene; a feed is fed by everyone and refills as it is used, so nothing tells the talk it is over.
- **Action:** around a fire, a person tends it. On a feed, everyone adds to it, and the platform decides what stays lit.
- **The third voice:** the fire answers whoever feeds it. The feed answers too, with likes, replies and what it shows next.

One caution for the script. The popular claim that a phone lying on the table makes a face-to-face conversation worse comes from a 2013 study [5], and a 2021 replication with 356 people did not find the effect [6]. The scene is stronger asking how the shape of the talk changes than repeating that screens ruin it.

## A sketch to argue with

```screenplay
EXT. LAKESHORE - NIGHT

A fire, just catching. NOOR and SAM sit on one log, a little apart. Sam feeds it twigs.

SAM
You got the boat running?

NOOR
Mostly.

The kindling flares. They watch it instead of each other.

LATER

Embers. Noor's face, lit from below.

NOOR
(to the fire)
I never told you why I left.

Sam reaches for a log. Stops. Leaves it.

INT. APARTMENT - NIGHT

Noor and Sam on a couch, a foot apart, each lit by a phone. Noor types, deletes, types.

In a group chat of twelve: "never told anyone why I left."

Sam's phone buzzes.
```

The same confession twice. By the fire it is said to the fire, and Sam chooses not to break the quiet. On the couch it goes to twelve people at once, and Sam reads it.

## Doing it

1. **Name the theme as a question.** Here: what happens to what people say, depending on what they say it around?
2. **List the layers of the scene:** setting, structure, objects, actions, the form the talk takes, light and sound.
3. **Ask how each layer could carry the theme.** Write down every answer, including the strained ones.
4. **Keep what the scene cannot do without,** and cut what is forced.
5. **Notice the questions the layering raises that you did not plan.** What does it mean when someone lets the fire die? What is the feed's version of ash? That is where the story starts finding things out.
