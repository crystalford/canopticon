# Why Temperature

*McLuhan's hot and cool as a theory of regulation. Working paper, second draft.*

**Thesis.** McLuhan's hot and cool is a theory of regulation. A medium can supply what a person would otherwise produce, and it can raise their arousal. Both press on the same balance a body keeps with heat. Kept warm all the time, people lose the capacity to produce their own; brief, regular excursions into the cool rebuild it.

### The claims, stated formally

**Research question.** Why did McLuhan describe media as hot and cool? What, if anything, does the distinction let us explain or do?

**Answer.** Hot and cool describe how a medium loads the regulation of the person using it: how much of the output it supplies, how much it arouses them, and whether they predict before the answer arrives. The distinction explains why people retain little of what is supplied to them. It identifies when cool media help and when they do not. It gives a practical rule for using hot media, AI included, without losing the capacity they replace.

**Contributions.**

- An answer to why McLuhan's distinction is one of temperature, which his readers have left open since 1964.
- A mechanism, prediction before the answer, that links McLuhan to research on active learning, pretesting and problem-solving first.
- Two measures, completion share and order, that apply to any medium for a given task and receiver.
- A boundary condition: cool media help only receivers with enough prior knowledge to predict.
- A named condition, cognitive ectothermy, and a protocol of cool excursions for countering it.

**Definitions.**

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| Hot (for a task and receiver) | The medium supplies most of the output in the part of the task being learned, before the receiver has predicted it |
| Cool | The receiver produces much of that output, or predicts it before the answer arrives |
| Completion share | The proportion of the output, in the part of the task being learned, that the receiver produces |
| Order | Whether the receiver predicts or attempts before the answer arrives |
| Arousal | The medium's effect on the receiver's overall gain, the energy with which everything is processed |
| Overheating | High supply and high arousal at once |
| Cognitive ectothermy | Relying on the environment to supply understanding, as an ectotherm relies on outside heat |
| Cognitive endothermy | The trained capacity to produce one's own predictions, recall and explanations |
| Cool excursion | A brief, deliberate period of producing before receiving, within a mostly warm environment |

**Propositions.**

|  | Proposition | Section | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| P1 | Hot and cool describe a relation between a medium, a task and a receiver, not a fixed property of the medium. | 3, 7 | Moderate: alliesthesia; the same model built hot and cool (Bastani) |
| P2 | A medium heats a receiver in two separable ways: supply and arousal. | 4 | Proposed; each half rests on its own literature |
| P3 | What is learned depends on the receiver producing part of the output, and most of all on predicting before the answer arrives. | 5, 6 | Strong: active learning, pretesting and problem-solving first; nine of thirteen paired comparisons |
| P4 | Cool media help only receivers with enough prior knowledge to predict; beginners do better starting hot. | 6 | Strong: expertise reversal; younger learners in the productive-failure data |
| P5 | A medium high in both supply and arousal leaves the least room for reflective control, and its content spreads fastest. | 4 | Moderate: separate findings (Brady; Metcalfe & Mischel) connected here, not tested together |
| P6 | The capacity to produce one's own understanding is trained by regular cool excursions and left untrained by constant supply. | 8 | Proposed: by analogy with cold acclimation; consistent with the removal studies |

## Abstract

McLuhan divided media into hot and cool. Hot media are "well filled with data" and ask little of the audience; cool media ask the audience to complete them. He never said why the distinction is one of temperature, and critics have treated it as his least useful idea.

This paper reads it as a theory of regulation. Temperature is the oldest language people have for regulation, in medicine, emotion and self-control. A medium can heat a person in two ways: by supplying the output they would otherwise produce, and by raising their arousal. A body warmed from outside stops making its own heat. A mind handed a finished answer makes no prediction of its own, and experiments show that predicting first is what makes learning last.

Scored blind, thirteen paired comparisons from the learning research fit this reading in nine cases, show no difference in three, and reverse in one. In the reversal, novices do better with the hot form, as a body needs fuel before it can make heat. Cold-acclimation research supplies the last step. The capacity to produce one's own heat is trained by excursions into the cold and left untrained by constant comfort, and the information environment now keeps most people constantly warm.

## 1. When the phone dies

You have driven to a friend's house a dozen times with your phone giving directions. One day the phone dies on the way, and you realise you do not know the route. You have been there often, but you have never found your way there.

London taxi drivers are the opposite case. To qualify, they spend three to four years learning the city well enough to plan any route themselves. Woollett and Maguire (2011) followed trainees through that training. The ones who qualified gained grey matter in the posterior hippocampus; those who did not qualify, and a control group, did not. Same city, same task. In one case a device produces the route, and in the other the driver does.

In 1964 Marshall McLuhan drew a distinction that bears on exactly this. In *Understanding Media* he wrote that "high definition is the state of being well filled with data," and:

"Hot media are, therefore, low in participation, and cool media are high in participation or completion by the audience."

His examples: radio is hot and the telephone cool; a photograph is hot and a cartoon cool; a lecture is hot and a seminar cool. "The hot form excludes, and the cool one includes."

He never explained why the distinction is one of temperature. His readers mostly treat the words as provocation. A *Maclean's* critic wrote that the distinction "has never served as a useful addition to any media critic's toolbox." The most serious attempt to take McLuhan's temperatures literally, a 2014 journal section edited by Dylan Mulvin and Jonathan Sterne, followed them into data centres, thermal imaging, weather maps and the calorie. It did not follow them into the body of the person receiving the medium.

This paper does. Its answer is that temperature is how people have always talked about regulation. McLuhan's hot and cool describes how a medium loads the regulation of the person using it. That account explains why the route never stuck, and it suggests what to do about it.

## 2. Temperature is the oldest language of regulation

Whenever people have needed words for a living system keeping its balance against what surrounds it, they have reached for hot and cold. McLuhan's pair belongs to a long family.

**Medicine.** Greek medicine described health as a well-tempered mixture, *krasis*, of hot, cold, wet and dry. Latin translated *krasis* as *temperamentum*. As the physician and etymologist Jeffrey Aronson notes, "temperamentum meant the same as κρᾶσις," and the Greek word could also mean the temperature of the air. *Temperament* and *temperature* both come from the Latin *temperare*, to bring to the right strength by mixing. Until the seventeenth century the four temperaments could also be called the four temperatures. The Sensory Syncrasis series took its name from the same root.

**Folk medicine.** The anthropologist George Foster described an "equilibrium model" of illness found across many cultures: health as balance, illness as imbalance. In Latin American folk medicine, foods, drinks and conditions are classed as hot or cold, and mixing them carelessly, such as a cold drink after a hot bath, is said to cause illness. Chinese and Ayurvedic medicine also class foods and conditions as heating or cooling.

**Emotion.** Anger is described as heat in many languages: boiling over, a hothead, losing your cool. Linguists such as Zoltán Kövecses have traced how widespread the pattern is. The link has a physical side. Craig Anderson's review of heat and violence concludes that "excessive heat appears to cause increases in aggression in many settings."

**Self-control.** Psychology's best-known model of willpower uses the same two words. Metcalfe and Mischel (1999) proposed "a cool, cognitive 'know' system and a hot, emotional 'go' system." The hot system is "impulsive and reflexive"; the cool one is "contemplative" and "flexible." The balance between them is "determined by stress, developmental level" and other factors. The strategies that help children wait are, in their words, "cooling strategies."

**Slang.** *Cool* spread from African American speech and jazz from the 1930s and came to mean composure: staying "calm, even unimpressed." A cool person shows little and leaves others to read them. Hot is the opposite: everything shown at once.

In each case, hot and cold do not name an amount of energy. They name how a system stands toward what is pressing on it: whether the pressure comes in and takes over, or whether the system holds its own balance. That is the sense in which McLuhan's distinction becomes useful.

## 3. What a body does with heat

Take McLuhan's metaphor as body physiology, the way the Sensory Syncrasis series took his sense ratio. It matches his definitions at four points, two of them strongly.

| Strength | The body | McLuhan on media |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Strong | Where heat comes from. In heat, warmth arrives from outside and the body sheds it: it sweats and moves less. In cold, the body must make its own: it shivers, raises its metabolism and moves more. | Hot media "do not leave so much to be filled in or completed by the audience"; cool media are "high in participation or completion." |
| Strong | The receiver's state. Cabanac (1971): "A given stimulus can induce a pleasant or unpleasant sensation depending on the subject's internal state." He called this alliesthesia. A hot bath is welcome when you are chilled and unbearable when you are overheated. | "The hot radio medium used in cool or nonliterate cultures has a violent effect." |
| Moderate | Gathering and spreading. Many mammals huddle in the cold and spread apart in heat. | "The hot form excludes, and the cool one includes." |
| Suggestive | Reversal at the extreme. Above about 45°C, cold receptors fire, so very hot water can briefly feel cold. | The next chapter of Understanding Media is "Reversal of the Overheated Medium." |

The first row carries the argument. On this reading, McLuhan's *participation* is the receiver producing something, as a cold body produces its own heat. A hot medium arrives finished and leaves the receiver nothing to produce. A cool medium arrives incomplete and leaves the receiver to complete it.

Whether McLuhan intended this is a separate question. He may have taken the words from the slang of his time without thinking about bodies at all. The claim here is that the metaphor carries a physiology that fits his definitions, which would explain why it has lasted despite never being explained.

## 4. Two ways a medium heats a person

The family of meanings in section 2 shows that "hot" carries two senses, and McLuhan's media can be hot in either one.

**Supply.** A medium can deliver the finished output: the answer, the route, the conclusion, the fully lit image. This is McLuhan's own definition, and it is the sense the rest of this paper tests. In the series' vocabulary it concerns the precision of the signal, meaning how little is left for the receiver to infer.

**Arousal.** A medium can also raise the receiver's arousal. This is how the media historian Brenton Malin reads McLuhan's heat: as "the idea that emotions were bodily temperatures being drawn out by technologies." In the series' vocabulary it concerns gain, the overall amplification that the series found to be the best-measured dimension across its cases.

The two are different, and they can come apart. A tense phone call is high in arousal and still cool: you have to work out what the other person means. A calm, polished lecture is low in arousal and still hot: everything is supplied.

Supply and arousal vary separately. The learning evidence in section 6 compares the two lower corners. The overheated corner is the feed described next.

**When both are high, a medium overheats.** A feed that hands over a conclusion and raises arousal at the same time leaves the least room for the cool system. Metcalfe and Mischel's account predicts the result. Stress shifts the balance toward the hot "go" system, which is fast and reflexive. In a study of 563,312 tweets, Brady and colleagues (2017) found that each moral-emotional word in a message increased its spread "by a factor of 20% for each additional word." The content that travels furthest is content that comes finished and arrives hot. It is shared on reflex, with nothing produced in between.

McLuhan's next chapter title, "Reversal of the Overheated Medium," has a plain reading in these terms. A person kept at high supply and high arousal stops regulating and starts reacting. The rest of this paper concentrates on supply, because supply is where the evidence on learning is strongest. Arousal is the second half of the account, and section 9 returns to it.

## 5. The mechanism: who predicts first

If participation means producing, the next question is what producing does. The answer from three lines of research is that it gives the receiver a prediction for the answer to correct.

**Guessing before the answer helps, even when the guess is wrong.** Richland, Kornell and Kao (2009) had students attempt questions before reading a science essay. Pretest accuracy was 5–22%. Yet in all five experiments the students who had attempted the questions later outscored students who spent the time reading, for example 75% against 56%. Students who tried to answer the questions also beat students who only memorised them, 90% against 78%. The attempt did the work.

**Working on a problem before instruction helps.** Sinha and Kapur (2021) meta-analysed 53 studies and 166 comparisons. Solving first, then being taught, beat being taught first (Hedges' g = 0.36, 95% CI 0.20–0.51), and more strongly when the design was followed closely. A second meta-analysis of 33 articles found no clear difference between solving first and other preparatory activities such as guided sensemaking. All of those also have the learner produce something before the explanation.

**The brain learns by correcting its own predictions.** In the predictive-processing account the series has used throughout, perception runs on predictions, and learning happens when incoming signals correct them. How far a signal can override a prediction depends on its precision. A high-definition signal settles the matter at once. A low-definition one leaves gaps that the brain fills from its own predictions, which is what McLuhan called completion.

Put together, the difference between hot and cool comes down to order:

**A hot medium answers before the receiver has predicted.** There is nothing of the receiver's own for the answer to correct, so little changes.

**A cool medium leaves the receiver to predict first.** The answer then corrects something the receiver made, and that correction is what is learned.

The physiology and the psychology describe the same arrangement. A body in the cold produces its own heat, and a brain with a low-definition signal produces its own predictions.

## 6. The evidence, scored

Thirteen comparisons in the learning research set a hotter condition against a cooler one. Scored blind by a second coder, nine fit the reading, three show no reliable difference, and one reverses. None shows the cooler condition doing worse, except for complete beginners, and that exception marks a boundary.

Each comparison was scored on two questions. Does the person produce more of the output in the cooler condition? Do they attempt or predict before the answer arrives?

| Comparison | Hotter | Cooler | Outcome | Fits? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Freeman et al. (2014), meta-analysis of 225 studies | Lecture | Active learning | Exams +0.47 SD; failure 33.8% against 21.8% | Yes |
| Sinha & Kapur (2021), meta-analysis of 53 studies | Instruction first | Problem first | g = 0.36 for problem first | Yes |
| Richland et al. (2009), 5 experiments | Extra reading | Pretest first | e.g. 75% against 56% | Yes |
| Deslauriers et al. (2019), 149 students | Polished lecture | Active session | Scores +0.46 SD; felt learning −0.56 SD | Yes |
| Bastani et al. (2025), about 1,000 students | AI gives answers | AI gives hints | Without AI: answers group 17% worse than controls; hints group the same | Yes |
| Ishikawa et al. (2008), 66 people | GPS | Paper map | GPS users stopped more and drew worse route maps | Yes (device caveat) |
| James & Engelhardt (2012), 15 children | Typing letters | Printing letters | Only printing recruited the reading network | Yes (small) |
| Kosmyna et al. (2025), preprint | LLM writes | Writing unaided | Lower connectivity when former LLM users wrote alone | Yes (preprint) |
| Henkel (2014) | Photographing | Looking | Photographed objects remembered worse | Yes |
| Calculator meta-analyses (1986, 2003) | Calculator | Paper and pencil | No harm; the student still sets up the problem | No difference |
| Laptop against handwritten notes (2014; replication 2021) | Typing | Handwriting | No significant difference on replication | No difference |
| Google effect on memory (Sparrow et al., 2011) | Expecting the computer to save it | Expecting it erased | Failed to replicate (Camerer et al., 2018) | No difference |
| Worked examples (Kalyuga et al., 2003) | Studying a worked solution | Solving the problem | Novices do better with the worked solution; experts with solving | Reverses for novices |

**The reversal marks a boundary.** Novices learn more from the fully worked solution, which is the hotter form. Kalyuga's summary: "instructional guidance, which may be essential for novices, may have negative consequences for more experienced learners." The productive-failure data show the same boundary from the other side: children in grades 2–5 did better with instruction first.

The physiology predicts this. A body produces heat in the cold only if it has fuel, and a cold battery delivers less than a warm one. A learner who knows nothing yet has nothing to predict with, so a cool medium leaves them cold. Whether hot or cool helps depends on the state of the receiver, which is alliesthesia applied to learning.

**The nulls.** Within the notes research, where production did differ, it went the expected way: notes that copied the lecture word for word went with lower scores. The Google-effect study failed to replicate, so it says little either way. The calculator result is taken up below.

**A blind second scoring.** A second coder scored all thirteen pairs from neutral descriptions of what each condition asked of the learner. The conditions were labelled A and B in mixed order and no outcomes were given. The coder was told not to use any remembered results.

The coder agreed on which condition was cooler in all thirteen pairs, with high confidence in nine. Matching those judgements against the outcomes gives a harder result than a first, unblinded count:

- **Nine pairs fit:** the cooler condition produced more learning, or less loss once the tool was removed.
- **Three show no reliable difference:** calculators, laptop against handwritten notes, and the Google effect.
- **One reverses:** worked examples for novices.

The first count had scored calculators as a fit, on the grounds that the student still sets up the problem. The blind coder treated computation as the skill being learned and rated the calculator condition much hotter, 1 against 4 on a five-point scale. On that reading, calculators are a null that cuts against the paper.

There is a possible explanation in the paper's own terms. Fewer than 20% of the calculator studies covered grade 4 or below, where arithmetic facts are learned, so most introduced calculators after the skill had formed. Handing a task to a tool after a skill is built may cost less, and more slowly, than handing it over before. That explanation was found after seeing the result, so it is offered as an open question.

This remains a count of directions, not a meta-analysis. The second coder was a separate AI model working from descriptions. It may have recognised well-known studies despite the instruction, and it did not read the original papers.

## 7. Temperature is a relation: a chatbot is both

A conversation with a chatbot shows why hot and cool cannot be fixed labels on devices. The person writes the question, which is cool: they decide what to ask, push back and steer. The answer arrives complete, which is hot. The same exchange holds both.

Heat behaves the same way. It flows, and its direction depends on the difference between the object and the body. The same cup of tea warms cold hands and cools a feverish mouth. Alliesthesia makes the same point for pleasure. Temperature belongs to the relation between a medium, a task and a receiver.

McLuhan sometimes wrote this way himself. He described "the hotting-up of the medium of writing to repeatable print intensity": a medium raised in temperature by a change in how it was produced and used.

Bastani's study shows the relation directly. The same model, GPT-4, was used both times. Set up to give answers, it was hot, and students were 17% worse once it was removed. Set up to give hints, it was cool, and left no measurable cost. The design decided which it was.

That turns the binary into two questions anyone can ask of an exchange:

**Share:** how much of the output, in the part of the task I am trying to get better at, do I produce?

**Order:** do I predict before the answer arrives, or after?

The same medium can be hot for one task and cool for another. It can help a novice by being hot and hold back an expert for the same reason. This also accounts for the feature of McLuhan's scheme readers have complained about most, that his classifications shift. Commentators on McLuhan describe radio as cooling once television took over its role. A study of social platforms in 2026 found that "media temperature" behaves as a continuum shaped by use. That is how a relation behaves.

**A worked example.** Someone wants to understand compound interest and turns to a chatbot. Here are three ways the same exchange can go.

| Exchange | Share: what the person produces | Order | Temperature |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| They type "Explain compound interest" and read the answer. | The question only. The explanation, the example and the formula all come from the machine. | Answer first | Hot |
| They write: "I think it means interest on interest, so $100 at 10% becomes $121 after two years. Is that right, and where does my picture break?" | Their own model, an example and a calculation. The machine corrects them. | Prediction first | Cool |
| They read the plain answer, close the window, and explain it to a friend that evening. | The explanation, later, in their own words. | Answer first, production after | Cooled after the fact |

The model, the topic and the time spent are the same in all three. What changes is the share the person produces and the order in which they produce it. On the evidence in section 6, the second exchange is the one that leaves the most behind. The third recovers some of the loss, because explaining afterwards is a form of retrieval.

## 8. Capacity: acclimation and the always-warm environment

So far temperature has been a state: hot now, cool now. The physiology also describes a capacity, and this is where the reading stops being a description and becomes a diagnosis.

**Cold trains the capacity to make heat.** In a ten-day cold-acclimation study, van der Lans and colleagues (2013) found that people recruited brown fat and produced more heat without shivering. They also "felt more comfortable in the cold, and reported less shivering." The ability to produce one's own heat grew with use, and the cold came to feel better.

**Constant comfort leaves it untrained.** Most people now spend their days in a narrow, controlled indoor climate. Van Marken Lichtenbelt and colleagues (2017) argue that "comfort and health may be related but are not synonyms," and that "prolonged temporal excursions outside the thermal comfort zone result in acclimatization." They recommend excursions outside the comfort zone.

**The information environment is kept warm.** Search, feeds, explainers and now chatbots supply an answer before most questions are fully formed. By the reading in sections 5 to 7, that is a climate in which the receiver rarely has to predict, recall or explain, so the capacity to do those things is rarely trained. The dead phone in section 1 is what that looks like: someone acclimatised to warmth, suddenly asked to produce their own heat.

**Cognitive ectothermy.** Biology divides animals by where their heat comes from. Endotherms, such as mammals and birds, produce their own. Ectotherms, such as lizards, take it from the environment: they bask, and when the sun goes in they slow down. This paper uses the same pair for understanding. *Cognitive ectothermy* is relying on the environment to supply understanding: answers, routes and explanations arrive from outside, and when the supply stops, so does the person's ability to produce them. *Cognitive endothermy* is the trained capacity to produce one's own predictions, recall and explanations. Unlike the animal kinds, these are not fixed. People move between them according to how they spend their time, which is why acclimation matters.

(Biologists use "endothermic brain" for a different idea, about how warm-bloodedness shaped animal cognition. The terms here are metaphors for a person's relation to information.)

**The feeling of effort is not fixed.** Deslauriers' students read the effort of active learning "to signify poorer learning," and rated the hot lecture higher. The acclimation data suggest that feeling can change. People who trained in the cold found it more comfortable. The discomfort of a cool medium may partly be a sign of being out of practice, which practice would lower.

The rise of cold showers and cold plunges shows that many people now look for deliberate cold exposure for the body. The equivalent for attention and understanding is rarely deliberate. That is the gap the rest of this paper addresses.

## 9. What follows: regulation rather than content

Most public argument about media is about content: what is true, what is harmful, what should be removed. McLuhan's lasting point was that the form of a medium matters more than what it carries, but he left "form" hard to act on. Read as regulation, his hot and cool gives form three things that can be noticed and managed: how much a medium supplies, how much it arouses, and how much time a person spends in the cool.

**For a person.** The practice that follows is a cooling protocol, a set of small excursions:

- predict before you ask
- recall before you look something up
- explain an idea in your own words after you watch it explained

Regularity matters more than intensity, as with cold exposure. Fuel matters too. Someone new to a subject does better starting hot, with worked examples, then cooling by degrees. The worked-example research calls this fading: steps of the solution are removed one at a time as the learner improves. In these terms, fading is acclimation.

**When a medium overheats.** For feeds that are hot on both counts, the relevant tools are Metcalfe and Mischel's cooling strategies: delay, distance, attention to the abstract rather than the arousing features. Cooling here means getting the reflective system back in charge before reacting or sharing.

**For AI.** A chatbot that answers on request is hot by default. Bastani's study shows the same model built cool, giving hints before answers, left students no worse off. Cool modes ask for the user's guess first, give hints before answers, and ask the user to explain the answer back. They can be built now.

**For platforms.** Ranking by how satisfied people feel selects for hot forms. For anything meant to inform or teach, Deslauriers found the feeling of learning strongly anticorrelated with learning.

**For conversation.** Talking with another person, with nothing to look up, is cool. It is where gaps show, and it is also where they close. *Krasis* found that "the routes back run through other people"; company is the oldest cool environment there is.

**For a research record.** A record that only supplies finished papers is a hot medium for its own author. Built cool, it would ask for the author's explanation before showing the paper. It would keep successive versions of that explanation, so the author can see their understanding grow, and it would offer spoken pieces to be given from memory.

## 10. Objections

**"This is active learning with a new name."** The learning research is older than this paper and does the empirical work. The paper adds four things that research does not supply:

- An answer to why McLuhan chose temperature.
- A single mechanism, prediction before the answer, linking active learning, pretesting and problem-solving first.
- A dependence on the receiver's state, which accounts for the novice reversal.
- Two measures, share and order, that apply to any medium, AI included.

It also adds the capacity argument of section 8, which classroom research does not address.

**"It is only a metaphor."** It is a metaphor, and the paper's claim is about why it works. Temperature is the language people have used for regulation in medicine, emotion and self-control. McLuhan's version lines up with measured physiology at two strong points. A metaphor that lasts sixty years without an explanation may be carrying something real. The work here was to find out what.

**"The fit was found after the fact."** Partly true, which is why section 3 ranks the correspondences and section 6 includes the studies that cut against the reading. The two strongest correspondences match McLuhan's two defining claims, completion by the audience and dependence on the receiving culture. Both were written in 1964, before the learning studies. A blind second coder, in section 6, agreed on which condition was cooler in all thirteen pairs.

**"Television was cool only because 1960s screens were blurry."** Probably true, and it supports the reading. Definition belongs to a signal at a given time and to the person receiving it. A sharper screen is a hotter medium, which is what temperature as a relation predicts.

**"McLuhan called print hot, yet reading a novel asks a great deal of the reader."** It does, and the answer shows how the measures work. Print supplies the words in full, and the reader cannot change them. It leaves the faces, the voices, the scenes and the links in an argument for the reader to produce. Measured on the words, a book is hot. Measured on imagining and understanding, it is cool, as long as the reader does that work rather than skimming for the gist. Temperature belongs to the part of the task, which is why the same book can be both.

## 11. Limits

**This reads a metaphor; it does not recover McLuhan's intent.** He may have taken the words from slang without thinking about bodies.

**The correspondences are qualitative.** No study measures media against thermoregulation directly.

**The scoring in section 6 rests on two coders, one of them blind, and the blind coder was an AI model.** It counts directions and is not a meta-analysis.

**Several learning studies are small or recent.** James & Engelhardt had 15 children; Kosmyna is a preprint that has drawn a published critique; Ishikawa's GPS result partly reflects the device.

**The capacity argument is an analogy.** Cold acclimation is measured in bodies. That understanding acclimates the same way is supported by the learning evidence, but no study follows information "excursions" over weeks the way van der Lans followed cold.

**The arousal half of the account is less developed.** The link from hot media to Mischel's hot system rests on separate findings that this paper connects without testing.

**Folk-medicine claims are summarised from secondary sources.** The Chinese and Ayurvedic hot-cold classifications in particular are not examined here.

**Several McLuhan quotations come from reproductions.** They should be checked against the 1964 edition.
