# Does Habitual Defensiveness Block Change? What the Behavioural Science Says
Research document · Canopticon · received 2026-10-01 · standalone · personal · kind: personal-pattern inquiry
---

**Yes: the evidence fairly consistently shows that face-saving and self-justification reduce how much people take in from mistakes and feedback, and so how much they change.** The main mechanism is not some deep character flaw. Each defensive move lowers the discomfort that would otherwise push you to update, pulls your attention away from the information, and rehearses the story that nothing needs to change.

## TL;DR

- **Your intuition is broadly right.** In Eskreis-Winkler & Fishbach's 2019 studies (5 studies, N = 1,674), people learned less from their own failures than their successes, but just as much from *other people's* failures. Feedback that pulls attention onto the self can make performance worse (Kluger & DeNisi 1996). In Motivational Interviewing research, voicing reasons *not* to change ("sustain talk") predicts worse outcomes.
- **The "I was going to do it that way, but…" habit is a known pattern.** Sociologists call these lines "accounts" and "disclaimers": face-saving moves that protect your image. That makes them changeable like any other cue → response habit.
- **The fix is a system, not willpower.** Lower the threat before the moment (a values reminder, self-compassion). Install an if-then rule at the moment ("If I feel the urge to explain, I say 'fair' and stop"). Then log what actually happens.

## Key Findings

### 1. How defensiveness dampens change

**(a) It discharges the discomfort that powers change.** Festinger's cognitive dissonance theory (1957); Aronson's refinement; Tavris & Aronson's "pyramid of choice." A justification like "I was going to do it that way anyway" is a dissonance-reducer. It makes the uncomfortable gap disappear *without* any change in behaviour.

**(b) It diverts attention away from the information.** Eskreis-Winkler & Fishbach (2019, *Psychological Science*): "Failure is ego threatening, which causes people to tune out." Kluger & DeNisi (1996), 607 effect sizes: feedback improved performance on average (d = .41), but **over one-third of feedback interventions decreased performance**.

**(c) It rehearses the "no change needed" story.** Kunda's motivated reasoning (1990). Miller & Rollnick's "righting reflex" and "sustain talk." Magill et al. (2018): sustain talk associated with worse outcomes (r = .19). Amrhein et al. (2003): strength of commitment language predicted drug-use outcomes a year later.

**(d) Self-serving attributions keep the model of yourself from updating.** Each such attribution means the failure is filed as "not about me."

"Full change" has three steps: accept the information, update the model of yourself, change the action. A defensive line typically cuts the chain at step 1 or 2.

### 2. The honest counterweight: self-protection has a real job

The psychological immune system (Gilbert, Wilson). Positive illusions (Taylor & Brown 1988; Colvin, Block & Funder 1995 on the social costs). Self-affirmation theory (Steele 1988; Cohen & Sherman 2014: "Events that threaten self-integrity arouse stress and self-protective defenses that can hamper performance and growth"). Pushing back is appropriate when criticism is wrong or in bad faith. The cost shows up when the defence is *automatic and indiscriminate*.

### 3. Your specific micro-behaviour: "I was going to do it that way, but…"

Face-work (Goffman 1955/1967). Accounts and disclaimers (Scott & Lyman 1968; Hewitt & Stokes 1975). Self-presentation; sociometer theory (Leary). Excuse-making (Snyder) and self-handicapping (Jones & Berglas 1978). Hindsight bias (Fischhoff): "I was going to do it that way" may *feel* true even when it isn't. Contingent self-esteem (Crocker & Wolfe 2001; Crocker & Park 2004: the pursuit of self-esteem "interferes with learning and mastery"). Shame vs guilt (Tangney; Tangney, Stuewig & Martinez 2014, 476 inmates: shame-proneness predicted reoffending through externalisation of blame). Reactance (Brehm 1966) and autonomy (Deci & Ryan). Possible ADHD-related sensitivity (Shaw et al. 2014: emotion dysregulation in 34–70% of adults with ADHD in clinic studies; "rejection sensitive dysphoria" is not in DSM-5-TR or ICD-11). It's a habit, mechanically: cue → response → reward (brief relief).

### 4. Ranked: the most likely drivers

1. Self-image protection / competence-contingent self-worth. 2. An automatic verbal habit. 3. Shame-aversion. 4. Autonomy / reactance. 5. Wanting to be seen as competent by specific people. 6. Environmental cues from settings where mistakes were punished.

## Recommendations: build it as a system

**Before the moment:** values affirmation (Epton et al. 2015, 144 tests: message acceptance d = .17, intentions d = .14, behaviour d = .32; Creswell et al. 2005 on cortisol; caveats: small, must precede the threat, school replications failed). Self-compassion (Breines & Chen 2012; Leary et al. 2007: "leads people to acknowledge their role in negative events without feeling overwhelmed with negative emotions"). Growth mindset (Moser et al. 2011 on error positivity; a 2021 *PLOS ONE* replication got mixed results). Intellectual humility (Porter & Schumann 2018).

**At the moment:** one-breath pause plus affect labeling (Lieberman et al. 2007). Defusion (ACT). Mindful presence (Niemiec et al. 2010). An if-then plan (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006, 94 tests, d = .65; Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer 2024: "across 642 independent tests" effective, .27 ≤ d ≤ .66): **"If someone corrects me and I feel the urge to explain what I was going to do, then I say 'Fair' (or 'Good catch') and stop."** Swap scripts; replace "but" with "and."

**The feedback:** people overestimate the social cost (Brooks, Gino & Schweitzer 2015 — askers rated *more* competent; Bruk, Scholl & Bless 2018 "beautiful mess effect"; the pratfall effect, Aronson, Willerman & Floyd 1966; Gibson, Harari & Marr 2018 on the status penalty for higher-status disclosers; note Gino's other retractions). Low-stakes exposure. Psychological safety (Edmondson 1999). Seek feedback on purpose.

## A 2-Week Experiment

Setup: if-then plan and two ownership scripts on the lock screen; 5-minute values write-up. Trigger: any correction. Action: breath → label → script → stop. Feedback: log trigger, defended Y/N, what happened next, what changed. Weekly review on day 7 and 14. Success: a falling defend-rate and a few entries where a default changed.

## Link Back to Your Earlier Patterns

The pallet-wrap "hard way" habit: owning it is what turns a one-off correction into a changed default. Time-estimation calibration: log the miss without commentary. The bad-mouthing habit: each justification makes the next instance easier (Tavris & Aronson); guilt framing over shame (Tangney).

## Caveats on the Evidence

Lab-heavy and student-heavy. A 2024 multi-lab registered replication (Vaidis et al.) failed to replicate the classic choice effect in induced-compliance dissonance. A November 2025 *Psychological Science* commentary (Spiller) argued the Eskreis-Winkler & Fishbach Script Task "provides a confounded test"; the authors replied. Correlation vs causation for shame/guilt, mindfulness, MI language. The strongest pieces: implementation intentions, the feedback-intervention meta-analysis, and convergence on one conclusion — *threat to the self reduces openness, and lowering threat increases it.* Not a diagnosis.
