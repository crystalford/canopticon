## The Architecture of Carney's Voice: Who Builds the Language of Canada's Technocrat-Prime Minister

Mark Carney's public language — pastoral in cadence, technocratic in register, soft in the way it frames coercion as guidance and policy as care — is not the product of a single speechwriter or a hired Madison-Avenue firm. It is, on the documented evidence, an unusually personal idiolect that Carney has been workshopping in front of expert audiences for nearly two decades, supported in his political phase by a tight in-house team drawn substantially from Justin Trudeau's old circle and from Gerald Butts's orbit at Eurasia Group. The "Carney sound" — what critics have variously described as central-bank pastoral, technocratic euphemism, or "values-realism" — predates his entry into politics. It was first developed inside the Bank of Canada and the Bank of England, codified in his 2021 book *Value(s)*, and is now being extended, almost literally as a brand, into prime-ministerial communications products like the YouTube vlog series *Forward Guidance*.

What follows traces, as concretely as the public record allows, who is behind each layer of that apparatus: the speeches, the slogans, the operational PMO communications team, the family and political-strategy circle around him, the central-bank rhetorical tradition he inherits, and the journalistic and academic analysis — increasingly critical — of the gap between his soothing register and his actual policy choices.

## 1. The Speechwriting Question: Carney as His Own Drafter, Inside an Inherited Communications Shop

The most striking finding of any close reading of the documentary record is that the major set-piece Carney speeches appear to be written, or at least heavily drafted, by Carney himself. The Wikipedia entry on his January 20, 2026 "Principled and Pragmatic: Canada's Path" address at Davos states explicitly that "Carney's speech, which he wrote himself, was compared and contrasted favourably against Trump's speech at the same venue the following day." Aaron Wherry's CBC analysis and Sabrina Maddeaux's *Orbit Policy* substack independently note the same point — Maddeaux writing that "the speech was actually written by Carney himself rather than a speechwriter," and arguing that this is "particularly illuminating about how he views the world." Paul Wells, in his *Substack* analysis "The Carney doctrine," treats the speech as a coherent personal statement of worldview rather than as a staffer-confected product. John Zimmer's rhetorical breakdown on *Manner of Speaking* identifies a signature Carney toolkit — tricolons and asyndetons ("we joined its institutions, we praised its principles, we benefited from its predictability"), anaphora ("to stop pretending, to name reality, to build our strength at home, and to act together"), and the extended Václav Havel "greengrocer" metaphor — that closely mirrors the rhetorical fingerprints already visible in his 2015 Lloyd's of London speech and in *Value(s)*.

The Prime Minister's Office does, however, employ professional speechwriters who handle the daily volume of remarks, press statements, announcements and bilingual material. According to the Hill Times' multi-part PMO breakdown by Laura Ryckewaert, a key documented speechwriter is **Hatim Jamali**, who was a speechwriter for the Liberals during the 2025 campaign, had a brief stint as a speechwriter in Trudeau's PMO at the tail end of that government, and previously did communications work for Pablo Rodriguez and Marie-Claude Bibeau. The communications operation itself, as of late 2025, is headed by **Alex Wellstead** as Director of Communications — a Trudeau-era spokesperson retained by Carney — with **Audrey Champoux** serving as deputy communications director for media relations. The broader political-communications spine of the operation reflects continuity rather than reinvention:

- **Marc-André Blanchard** — Chief of Staff since July 2025, a former Canadian Ambassador to the United Nations and most recently EVP at Caisse de dépôt et placement du Québec Global. Blanchard is described as "a master diplomat" by colleagues and is reported to navigate "policy, power, and perception."
- **David Lametti** — Principal Secretary, a former Liberal Justice Minister, Oxford contemporary of Carney's, and (according to *The Globe and Mail*) handler of "much of the prime minister's political and policy agenda."
- **Marco Mendicino** — Interim Chief of Staff from March through July 2025.
- **Tom Pitfield** — interim Principal Secretary before Lametti; previously executive campaign director for the 2025 Liberal campaign, founder of Data Sciences, and co-founder of Canada 2020.
- **Braeden Caley** — Deputy Chief of Staff and co-campaign director in 2025; a "past senior communications director for the Liberal party" and former president/CEO of Canada 2020. Caley is the most senior person on Carney's team whose career is principally in political communications.
- **Andrée-Lyne Hallé** — Deputy Chief of Staff and co-campaign director; an ex-Trudeau PMO staffer and former deputy chief of staff to Chrystia Freeland.
- **Tim Krupa** — Director of Policy (ex-Goldman Sachs and Bridgewater), who handled policy during Carney's leadership campaign and the 2025 election.
- **Kaitlin Power** — Executive Assistant to the PM and his campaign "body person."

Notably absent from this list is any single, name-brand chief speechwriter of the kind that George W. Bush had in Michael Gerson, or Barack Obama in Jon Favreau. There is no documented evidence of an outside PR firm or strategic-communications agency on retainer to write Carney's policy speeches — though, as discussed below, the political strategy and platform architecture is widely reported to involve Gerald Butts of Eurasia Group on an informal advisory basis. The operational arrangement is best characterized as Carney as principal author of his major speeches, supported by a campaign-and-PMO communications shop that polishes, translates, distributes and amplifies — not one that invents his voice.

## 2. The Lexicon: Tracing the Signature Phrases

The phrases that have come to define Carney's public lexicon are, with one important exception, traceable to him personally — often to dated speeches or to his book — rather than to consultants. The cumulative effect, however, is a brand identity that systematically renders power as guidance, decisions as information, and policy as care.

**"Forward Guidance."** This is the foundational case. The term is a textbook monetary-policy concept describing a central bank's communication of the likely path of future interest rates; in its modern form it is associated with Alan Blinder's 1990s work and with Ben Bernanke's Fed. Carney's personal claim to the phrase comes from April 2009, when as Bank of Canada Governor he was among the first central bankers anywhere to use "Odyssean" (commitment-style) forward guidance, promising to hold the policy rate at 0.25 per cent until mid-2010 contingent on inflation. As Governor of the Bank of England (2013–2020) he doubled down on the practice — at one point being labelled an "unreliable boyfriend" by Labour MP Pat McFadden for the way successive forward-guidance signals were reinterpreted. The deliberate brand extension came on April 19, 2026, when Carney's PMO launched a YouTube vlog called *Forward Guidance*, opening with Carney explicitly drawing the line from his central-bank past to his prime-ministerial present: "During my time as Governor of the Bank of Canada, I developed a practice to assure people that no matter how challenging the situation seemed, we had a plan." *Policy Magazine* (Lisa Van Dusen) wrote that "the title for Mr. Carney's video, *Forward Guidance*, comes from a central banker's term for previewing monetary policy plans for the future"; Aaron Wherry of CBC, the editorial board of *The Globe and Mail*, and the *Hill Times* all read the format as a deliberate, FDR-fireside-chat-style adaptation of central-bank communication discipline to mass political messaging. The Globe editorial — "Less forward guidance, Mr. Carney, and more accountability" — is sharp on the bait-and-switch: the video, it argued, was "almost exclusively material cut and pasted from speeches he has delivered before," repurposing the technical authority of "forward guidance" to package recycled political content.

**"Value(s)." ** The parenthetical "s" is Carney's own conceit, formalized in his 2021 Public Affairs/Signal book *Value(s): Building a Better World for All*. As multiple reviewers explain (Hachette publicity, the *Thinking Faith* Jesuit review, *Open Canada*, the PMC academic review, *Canadian Dimension*'s hostile read by Jeremy Appel), the parenthesis is meant to signal the slippage between economic value (singular: price) and moral values (plural: solidarity, fairness, resilience, sustainability, responsibility, humility, dynamism). The device is consistent with Carney's broader move — turning a numerical concept into a moral one while retaining the patina of quantitative authority. Crucially, the device is used as a live rhetorical hinge in the Davos speech: "we are no longer relying on just the strength of our values, but also on the value of our strength." There is no evidence that this construction was workshopped by a consultant; it is his own signature.

**"Tragedy of the horizon."** This is, as widely documented, Carney's own coinage, in his September 29, 2015 Lloyd's of London speech as Governor of the Bank of England. The phrase deliberately rhymes with Garrett Hardin's "tragedy of the commons" — Carney explicitly invokes the parallel in the speech — and frames climate change as a time-horizon mismatch problem between the planning horizons of monetary policymakers, financial regulators and elected politicians, and the much longer arc of climate damage. The phrase has been canonized inside the climate-finance world (TCFD, NGFS, GFANZ, Carbon Tracker) and was used to mark its tenth anniversary by Environmental Defence Canada and others. Importantly, the speech is also the rhetorical template for Carney's entire later style: framing political problems as information problems ("by managing what gets measured, we can break the Tragedy of the Horizon"), and presenting governance as the orderly disclosure of risk.

**"Rupture, not a transition."** This was first used by Carney in domestic political contexts — Policy Magazine quotes him before Davos describing Trump's impact as "not a transition, it's a rupture" — and then anchored the Davos speech itself ("a rupture in the world order, the end of a pleasant fiction"). The framing is itself a familiar Carney move: it disclaims agency. A rupture is something that happens *to* the system; it absolves the speaker from having helped build the prior order. The Carnegie Endowment and the *Tyee* both noted the rhetorical power of the framing; Nesrine Malik in *The Guardian* and the *Orbit Policy* substack both criticized it for its convenient timing — Carney only "named reality" once the costs had reached Canada's door.

**"Elbows up."** This is the one case where Carney's lexicon was not authored by him. The phrase is a hockey reference popularly attributed to Gordie Howe, and was already in circulation as a Canadian anti-Trump rallying cry by early 2025. *Saturday Night Live* helped it go viral when Mike Myers tapped his elbow and mouthed "elbows up" during a February 2025 episode. The Liberal campaign formally annexed the phrase on March 22, 2025 with a one-minute YouTube/X ad shot inside a hockey rink, in which Carney quizzes Myers (wearing a "Never 51" Team Canada jersey) on Canadiana before both men raise their elbows. The Liberal Party of Canada itself published, posted and distributed the ad on Carney's official accounts the day before he called the snap election; no outside agency-of-record has been publicly named for the spot. Industry/branding analysis by Grant Gordon (Key Gordon agency) and *Fast Company*'s Mark Wilson described the larger visual identity — the cropped maple-leaf wordmark, the bilingual "Canada Strong / Canada Fort" lockup — as deliberately patriotic, simple, and "visually boring" in a way that matched the moment. The slogan's authorship is therefore best described as crowdsourced: a Gordie Howe trope, amplified by Myers on SNL, picked up by the Liberal digital team, and integrated into the campaign by Caley, Pitfield and Hallé. The footnote anecdote — that a Maple Ridge B.C. volunteer named **Justine Sones** beaded the "Elbows Up" bracelet that Carney has been seen wearing at events since the Calgary Stampede — underscores how organic, rather than consultant-confected, this particular piece of branding actually was.

**"Build Canada Homes" / "Canada Strong" / "Building Canada Strong" / "Powering Canada Strong."** These are platform-level Liberal Party brand units, rolled out by the campaign in March–April 2025. The Liberal Party's official site uses "Building Canada Strong" as the umbrella name for the housing platform, with "Build Canada Homes (BCH)" as the new federal home-building agency announced on the campaign trail. The naming convention — a participle ("Building/Powering"), a national identifier ("Canada"), and a moral adjective ("Strong") — is consistent with the Liberal campaign's broader visual and verbal identity ("Canada Strong / Canada Fort"). Industry analyst Grant Gordon, quoted in *Yellopolitics*, found the slogan "derivative" and said the team "could have done better"; nevertheless, the agency-of-record for the Liberal Party's 2025 branding has not been publicly named, with most accounts attributing the rollout to the in-house campaign team led by Caley and Pitfield, with Butts's strategic input.

**"From reliance to resilience."** This phrase appears repeatedly in Carney speeches from late summer 2025 onward — most prominently at the Global Progress Action Summit in Toronto in September 2025 ("We need to build new trade relationships in order to move from reliance to resilience"). *Policy Magazine* used it as the title of a major editorial reading of his foreign policy ("From Reliance to Resilience: Mark Carney's Foreign Policy"). The phrase is a stylistic cousin of "values-based realism" and "the strength of our values, the value of our strength" — alliterative, antithetical, and built on a noun-to-noun pivot. There is no evidence of consultant authorship; the construction is consistent with Carney's own signature rhetorical patterns and with the broader "resilience" vocabulary running through *Value(s)*.

## 3. Diana Fox Carney's Role: Shared Milieu, Not Documented Messaging Role

Diana Fox Carney is an Oxford-trained economist (with master's degrees in PPE and agricultural economics) who has worked in international development, at think tanks in the UK, and — as of the period leading up to her husband's premiership — as a senior advisor at Eurasia Group in New York, focused on global climate and energy policy. She also has documented relationships with the Pictet Group, the Shell Foundation Council, and a number of climate-finance and sustainable-investment organizations.

There is, however, no documented evidence that she drafts speeches, manages messaging, or shapes the verbal brand of her husband's prime ministership. The most that the record shows is:

- During the 2025 campaign she introduced Carney at a Winnipeg rally and at the Liberal headquarters on election night, where she described him in distinctive Carney-style language as "consistent, compassionate, loyal and driven by an exceptionally strong set of values" — a phrasing that closely echoes the moral vocabulary of *Value(s)*. This may indicate shared sensibility rather than messaging influence.
- She campaigned door-to-door as a surrogate in Nepean, telling the *Ottawa Lookout* that she was acting as Carney's "eyes and ears" in his own riding. *The Walrus* characterized this as part-surrogate, part-character-witness deployment, "engineered" by the campaign rather than freelance.
- In her *Alright, Now What?* interview with Mitzie Hunter for the Canadian Women's Foundation, Fox Carney explicitly defines her role as professionally distinct from her husband's: "the jobs that I do are the same jobs that I did two years ago, and I'm the same person."
- The most significant point of operational adjacency is institutional, not editorial: she works at Eurasia Group, where Gerald Butts is Vice Chairman; Ian Bremmer's firm has been reported (by *Western Standard* and others) to hold federal contracts that pre-date and post-date Carney's premiership, a fact that has fuelled significant conflict-of-interest commentary in conservative-aligned media but does not, on the available evidence, translate into documented speech or messaging input.

In short: Fox Carney's professional vocabulary — climate finance, sustainability, resilience, values-driven investment — overlaps almost entirely with Mark Carney's. They share an idiolect because they share a milieu (Oxford, Bank of England, GFANZ, Eurasia Group, the global climate-finance circuit). But there is no documented instance of her involvement as a speechwriter, message designer or brand architect for the Prime Minister.

## 4. The Central Bank Communication Tradition: From Blinder to Greenspan to Carney

The pastoral-technocratic register that defines Carney's voice is not unique to him; it is the inherited dialect of late-twentieth and early-twenty-first century central banking. Carney did not invent this register. He is its most fluent contemporary practitioner — and the first to attempt its full transposition into electoral politics.

The architecture of "central bank speak" as a deliberate policy tool was built principally by **Alan Blinder**, Princeton economist and former Vice Chair of the Federal Reserve. His 2008 *Journal of Economic Literature* survey paper, co-authored with Michael Ehrmann, Marcel Fratzscher, Jakob de Haan and David-Jan Jansen ("Central Bank Communication and Monetary Policy: A Survey of Theory and Evidence," NBER Working Paper 13932), is the standard academic statement of the position that communication itself is a policy instrument — one with measurable effects on financial markets and on the predictability of monetary-policy decisions. Blinder's earlier 1996 argument that central banks should communicate more (against the prevailing Federal Reserve culture of deliberate obfuscation summarized in the famous remark attributed to Montagu Norman, "never explain, never excuse") was the intellectual hinge. Blinder's 2022 follow-up paper ("Central Bank Communication with the General Public: Promise or False Hope?", co-authored with Ehrmann, de Haan and Jansen, Princeton GCEPS Working Paper 291) is more cautious about whether the technique can be scaled from market communication to mass-public communication — which is exactly the experiment that Carney is now running with his *Forward Guidance* vlog.

The institutional shift the Blinder literature describes is dramatic. Alan Greenspan, the Fed chair who once said he had "learned to mumble with great incoherence," became by 2003 an explicit "forward guidance" practitioner, telling markets the funds rate would stay low "for a considerable period." The FOMC began announcing decisions in 1994; published its "bias" assessments from 1999; began issuing dot-plot projections under Bernanke. The Bank of International Settlements (BIS) literature treats central bank communication as both a transparency commitment and a market-management tool. The aesthetic of this communication — calm, technocratic, parsed, hedge-laden, prone to bureaucratic abstractions like "data-dependent" and "well-anchored expectations" — is what Carney brought intact into his Bank of Canada (2008–2013) and Bank of England (2013–2020) tenures.

Carney's specific contributions to this tradition are well-documented in two senses. First, on the operational side: he was an early adopter of commitment-style forward guidance in April 2009 at the Bank of Canada, and he installed forward guidance, plus the "Tragedy of the Horizon" climate-finance framework, at the Bank of England. Tony Yates, his former senior BoE advisor, wrote a retrospective (in *Medium* and the *New Statesman*) describing Carney's tenure as having "modernized" Bank communication while leaving mixed substantive results. Second, on the rhetorical side: Carney pushed central bank speech into more explicitly *moral* territory — climate, inequality, "purpose," "values" — than any of his peers. His 2015 Lloyd's speech, his Reith Lectures, and *Value(s)* extend the register from monetary technocracy to a comprehensive moral economics. The book reviews collected by the *PMC* (a 2022 academic review) and *Open Canada* (Taylor Owen) noted that *Value(s)* is essentially a manifesto-as-policy-paper, written in the calm cadence of a Bank of England Inflation Report.

What does this inheritance produce, stylistically? Several closely related features:

- **Decisions framed as information.** "Forward guidance" is the paradigm: the central bank does not *do* something to you; it *informs* you of what it expects to do, so that you can plan. Power is exercised by setting expectations, not by direct action.
- **Policy framed as risk management.** Climate is a "physical, liability, transition" risk-disclosure problem; trade is "vulnerability" and "resilience"; sovereignty is "diversification."
- **Authority framed as guidance.** The speaker is a steward of public information, a calmer of markets, a manager of expectations — not a wielder of coercive power.
- **Care vocabulary in technical clothing.** Words like "stability," "sustainability," "resilience," "solidarity," "fairness" — moral language from outside the discipline — are bolted onto the technocratic syntax.

Carney's distinctive political-communications move is to take this entire register and apply it to electoral politics, where it sounds — to admirers — adult, principled and serious, and — to critics — euphemistic, evasive and curiously decoupled from accountability.

## 5. The Political-Communications Apparatus: Eurasia Group, Gerald Butts, and the Liberal Continuity

If there is a "PR firm" or external strategic-communications hand on Carney's political operation, it is not a Madison-Avenue agency — it is **Eurasia Group**, the political-risk consultancy founded by Ian Bremmer, where Gerald Butts is Vice Chairman and Diana Fox Carney is a Senior Advisor.

**Gerald Butts.** According to Butts's own Eurasia Group biography, he "played a leadership role in the design of the strategies for the Liberal Party's 2015, 2019, and 2025 national election campaigns — as well as Justin Trudeau's bid for the party's leadership in 2013, and Mark Carney's in 2025." The 2025 Carney leadership and general-election campaigns are explicitly listed as among his strategic-design responsibilities. *The Globe and Mail* confirmed in early 2025, and Warren Kinsella publicly noted on January 12, 2025, that Butts was "advising Mr. Carney on the campaign trail." Butts also leads New Climate Group, a private consultancy. He is not a documented speechwriter for Carney, but he is the single most consistent strategic intelligence behind the Liberal Party's communications architecture across three election cycles, and he is the operational link between the Trudeau-era PMO and the Carney-era PMO. Penny Collenette and Alex Marland, the political-communication scholar at Memorial/Acadia, are both quoted observing that the Carney PMO staffing pattern is one of "operational continuity" with the Trudeau apparatus.

**The Mike Myers "Elbows Up" ad.** The ad — posted on Carney's official YouTube and X accounts on March 22, 2025, the day before he called the snap election — has not been publicly attributed to any external production company in the reporting reviewed (CTV News, *Hollywood Reporter*, *Neil Young Archives*, *Yellopolitics*, *Fast Company*). It is described consistently as a Liberal Party campaign ad shot at a hockey rink, with Myers volunteering his time. The implied production chain is internal-Liberal: campaign co-directors Caley and Hallé, executive campaign director Pitfield, with creative input from the in-house digital team and the kind of strategic framing for which Butts is known. No outside PR or strategic-communications firm has been publicly named as the producer of the spot.

**The 2025 leadership/election campaign team.** Beyond Butts, the documented senior figures running Carney's political communications were:
- **Tom Pitfield** — executive campaign director.
- **Braeden Caley** — co-campaign director, former senior communications director for the Liberal Party, former CEO of Canada 2020.
- **Andrée-Lyne Hallé** — co-campaign director; ex-Trudeau PMO and Freeland office.
- **Mark Wiseman** — former Blackrock manager, Century Initiative co-founder, appointed by Carney as an outside advisor.
- **Marco Mendicino** — interim Chief of Staff, March–July 2025.

**External political advisors with documented influence.** The *Niagara Independent* and other outlets note Carney's documented links to John Podesta (the Obama- and Biden-era climate-policy operator) — a link mediated by Butts. There is no documented external PR firm or branding agency on the Liberal Party of Canada's books for the 2025 campaign; the *Yello* and *Fast Company* analyses both characterize the brand identity as having been built in-house.

The picture, then, is this: the Carney political-communications operation is essentially a continuation of the Trudeau-Butts apparatus, with some new senior leadership (Blanchard, Lametti, Krupa) drawn from Carney's own pre-political networks (UN, CDPQ, Oxford, Goldman/Bridgewater). The strategic intelligence is Butts and the in-house team; the moral-technocratic voice is Carney's own.

## 6. Academic and Journalistic Analysis of Carney's Rhetoric

A small but growing body of rhetorical and journalistic analysis has begun to dissect Carney's prose. The most systematic readings, both supportive and critical, converge on the same diagnosis: Carney's effect comes from antithesis, tricolon, anaphora and a small set of carefully designed phrase-pairs, deployed in a calm technical register that disguises the political audacity of what is being said.

**John Zimmer's "Manner of Speaking" analysis (January 27, 2026).** Zimmer's close reading of the Davos speech catalogues Carney's rhetorical devices in detail: tricolons and asyndetons ("we joined its institutions, we praised its principles, we benefited from its predictability"; "a country that cannot feed itself, fuel itself or defend itself has few options"; "engaging broadly, strategically, with open eyes"); antithesis ("the strength of our values, the value of our strength"); anaphora ("to stop pretending, to name reality, to build our strength at home, and to act together"); and the extended Václav Havel "greengrocer" metaphor as the spine of the speech. Zimmer admires the structural tightness and the moral clarity but flags two flaws: occasional overcomplication (the unexplained jargon "variable geometry") and a contested central metaphor — Antti Mustakallio, the Finnish rhetoric specialist Zimmer cites, argues that the Havel greengrocer image inappropriately equates the voluntary rules-based international order with a coercive communist system "built on fear and violence."

**Paul Wells, "The Carney doctrine" (Substack, January 2026).** Wells treats the Davos speech as a genuine articulation of personal worldview and Canada's foreign-policy turn — the eight-year "feminist foreign policy" branding under Trudeau, Wells notes, was quietly retired in November and replaced at Davos with "values-based realism." Wells's reading is closer to admiring than critical.

**The Tyee's "To Grasp the Power of Carney's Davos Speech" (January 26, 2026).** This walks through the speech in detail, identifying Carney's deliberate use of Larry Fink's Gretzky "skate where the puck is going" introduction, his "hypnotic" anaphora in the close, and his calculated avoidance of Trump's name while making Trump the entire subtext.

**Andrew Coyne (*Globe and Mail*, January 24, 2026).** Coyne explicitly says the speech "has been, to be sure, wildly overpraised. The rhetoric is not particularly eloquent, the insights not especially scintillating. Its main recommendation, that middle powers should lessen their exposure to the great powers by trading more with each other, is essentially warmed-over friendshoring." Coyne credits the speech's "candour, its clarity, and, perhaps most of all, its timing," but argues that the central Havel metaphor is "inapt" because the rules-based international order is not a totalitarian regime but a voluntary association. Coyne has also, in *National Newswatch*, separately attacked "Canada's New Government" as an "irritating phrase" and called the proposed Canadian sovereign wealth fund undefined at the level of basic meaning.

**Orbit Policy / Brendan Frank, "The Performance of Ritual: The Hypocrisy of Carney's Davos Speech" (January 21, 2026).** This is the sharpest analytic reading. Frank argues that the speech "is also a stunning example of hypocrisy and of words being out of step with actions." He notes that Carney's invocation of Havel reverses the original meaning: in Havel's essay, taking down the sign is a *bottom-up* act of dissent by ordinary people; in Carney's speech it is a *top-down* call addressed to "companies and statesmen, not the people." Frank: "Actual people are notably missing from Carney's framing... This is the most revealing line of all in the speech." He further notes the irony of a leader who "was quick to crack down on strikes and on workers' collective power" speaking of collective action.

**CBC News reality-check (Brian Stewart, three-month follow-up).** Reviews Carney's response to the U.S.–Israel war on Iran against the Davos rhetoric, concluding that Carney's silence about the UN Charter when Washington violated it three months after Davos "felt different" — a phrase quoted from Liberal MP Will Greaves. CBC's review explicitly tests "values-based realism" against subsequent events and finds significant gaps.

**Nesrine Malik (*The Guardian*).** Argues the speech only acknowledged the collapse of the rules-based order "once the rot reached his own door," noting Carney's failure to acknowledge the victims of the "hypocrisies" of that order — Iraq, Gaza, the Global South.

**Tyler Cowen (*Marginal Revolution*).** Argues, drily, that Carney can "talk back" precisely because "he knows America will defend Canada, including against Russia, no matter what."

**Sabrina Maddeaux / Orbit Policy and David Coletto.** Both noted the strategic intent of *Forward Guidance* as a "politics of reassurance" — using the calm technical authority of central bank communication to address political uncertainty. Coletto sees this approvingly; Maddeaux is more skeptical, identifying it as recycled campaign content packaged as a new transparency exercise.

The pattern across these analyses is consistent: Carney's rhetoric is widely identified as effective precisely because it is calm, structured, technically authoritative and morally framed — and several of the most analytic readings (Frank, Coyne, Malik, the Tyee in places, Wherry) explicitly identify the gap between the rhetorical posture and the political substance as the central problem with the style.

## 7. The Orwell Question: The Gap Between Carney's Language and His Record

The most direct Orwell-frame critique published in mainstream Canadian commentary comes from the **Fraser Institute** (Jake Fuss and Tegan Hill, "Carney engaging in Orwellian doublethink with federal budget rhetoric"). The piece is built around Carney's own phrase, in advance of the October 2025 budget, that the budget would focus on "both austerity and investments." The authors write: "In George Orwell's classic 1984, he describes a dystopian world dominated by 'doublethink' — instances whereby people hold two contradictory beliefs simultaneously while accepting them both. In recent comments about the upcoming October federal budget, Prime Minister Carney unfortunately offered a prime example of doublethink in action." They argue that the actual fiscal plan involves *more* borrowing than Trudeau planned — $93.4 billion more — and is therefore "not austerity — even if Prime Minister Carney truly believes it to be."

**Jen Gerson (*The Line*).** Gerson's columns are among the sharpest critiques of the gap between Carney's campaign rhetoric and his governing posture. On the "Elbows Up" promise, she writes: "the actual government we're getting on the Canada–U.S. file is about as close to the campaign advertisement as a drop-shipped Temu Labubu. Or perhaps the Liberals correctly intuited what I did not: that this country's ruling class doesn't really want a fight, but merely a kayfabe to keep up the appearance of virtue in the face of tyranny. A show. A rebellion on principle, but one without grit, blood, or teeth." Gerson explicitly calls the "Elbows Up" framing "always pretty lame" — "the best our national brain trust could come up with was dragging a has-been comic who's long lived in the U.S. ... to pull out a tortured hockey metaphor to the delight of Boomers."

**Aaron Wherry (CBC).** Wherry's analysis of Carney's September 2025 decision to scrap most retaliatory tariffs is a careful documentation of the elbows-up/elbows-down rhetorical reversal: "Carney once spoke of Canada putting its elbows 'up,' now those elbows might be said to be down." Wherry concedes the "Elbows Up" phrase was "undoubtedly a brilliant, nearly Churchillian, bit of political rhetoric" — but notes that "as a strategy for continental trade negotiations, it was perhaps always of limited use."

**The Globe and Mail editorial board ("An elbows-down strategy in the pipeline").** "It's been nearly six months since election day, a federal campaign the Liberals won in large part on Mark Carney's vow to take an aggressive approach... Elbows up was the slogan and offence was the play. Ever since the Liberal win, however, those elbows have been drooping lower and lower." The editorial documents the May 2025 White House visit ("you're a transformational president"), the June 2025 scrapping of the digital services tax, and the September 2025 retaliatory-tariff retreat as the three principal walk-backs from the campaign language.

**The Globe and Mail editorial board on Forward Guidance ("Less forward guidance, Mr. Carney, and more accountability").** A direct attack on the rhetorical packaging of substantively recycled content: "Mr. Carney's 'forward guidance' video was almost exclusively material cut and pasted from speeches he has delivered before... All of the 'forward guidance' was repetition of past promises."

**William Watson (*Financial Post*).** Watson's column — referenced in the *Canadaland* podcast bibliography — characterizes Carney's "forward guidance" as "mainly 'backward spinning,'" a direct critique of the central technique.

**Tammy Nemeth (EnergyNow).** From a different ideological direction, Nemeth criticizes Carney's "lofty rhetoric" around European integration as masking a "deeply nostalgic, almost romantic view of Europe" while "selling out Canadian sovereignty for a mountain of red tape and romantic symbolism." She notes Carney's repeated invocation of "nostalgia isn't a strategy" while practicing what she calls deeply nostalgic European-elite politics.

**Michelle Rempel Garner (Substack).** A direct attack on the "Tragedy of the Horizon" framing as having "normalized a frenzy of rapid and dogmatic change to global energy policy" while failing to account for energy security — written from the Conservative side of the debate.

**Canadaland (Jesse Brown).** Treats *Forward Guidance* as a "tightly scripted, 'intimate' video designed to project calm authority while walking Canadians through an increasingly shaky relationship with the United States. It's part history lesson, part recycled campaign slogans, and highly reminiscent of FDR's fireside chats." Asks whether Carney is "just shifting the blame."

**The Hill Times (Stuart Benson).** Quotes Conservative MPs accusing Carney of "conditioning" Canadians for "rockier roads to come" through the *Forward Guidance* format.

The collective force of this critical literature is to identify a recurring pattern. Carney's language consistently:

1. Frames decisions as guidance ("forward guidance") rather than as exercises of power.
2. Frames policy reversals as evolutions ("from reliance to resilience," "values-based realism," "rupture, not a transition") rather than as choices with losers.
3. Uses moralized abstraction ("values," "principles," "solidarity," "stability") in places where political opponents demand concrete commitments.
4. Borrows the authority of central-bank technocracy — the calm, the parsed, the data-dependent — to insulate political decisions from political accountability.
5. Builds slogans of populist defiance ("Elbows Up," "Canada Strong") whose actual policy expression turns out to be markedly softer than the rhetorical posture.

This pattern is what the most analytic critics — Frank at Orbit Policy, Coyne at the Globe, Gerson at The Line, the Fraser Institute, the Globe's editorial board, Wherry at CBC, Malik at *The Guardian* — are pointing to when they describe Carney's language as euphemistic, evasive, or doublethink-adjacent. None of them quite call it Newspeak, but the substance of the critique is recognizably Orwellian: a sustained gap between the warmth of the words and the coldness of the underlying decisions.

It is worth noting one important caveat the academic literature itself supplies. Alan Blinder's own work — particularly the 2022 paper — is openly skeptical that the central-bank communication model can be successfully extended to the general public, predicting that "attempts to communicate with the general public, though laudable, will fail." That is the wager Carney's entire prime-ministerial communication strategy is making, and it is not yet clear that he is winning it.

## Conclusion

Pulling the threads together produces a relatively clear answer to the question with which this report began. The distinctive Carney voice — the soft, pastoral, technocratic register that frames power as guidance, decisions as information, and policy as care — is principally:

**1. Carney's own sensibility, formed inside a specific institutional tradition.** It is the voice of the global central-bank communication revolution that Alan Blinder helped theorize, that Alan Greenspan and Ben Bernanke pioneered at the Fed, and that Carney himself extended at the Bank of Canada (forward guidance, 2009) and the Bank of England (Tragedy of the Horizon, 2015; the climate-finance disclosure agenda, 2015–2020). The book *Value(s)* (2021) is the manifesto form of that register. The major speeches — including the 2026 Davos address that critics most consistently flag as exceptional — are written by Carney himself.

**2. Polished and operationalized by an in-house team rather than an external PR firm.** The PMO communications operation is led by Director of Communications Alex Wellstead (retained from Trudeau), Deputy Director Audrey Champoux, deputy chiefs of staff Braeden Caley and Andrée-Lyne Hallé, Principal Secretary David Lametti, Chief of Staff Marc-André Blanchard, and speechwriter Hatim Jamali. There is no documented external speechwriting firm, agency-of-record, or named single-voice ghostwriter behind the major speeches.

**3. Strategically scaffolded by Gerald Butts and the Trudeau-era Liberal apparatus.** Butts has openly claimed strategic-design responsibility for the 2025 Carney leadership and general-election campaigns; Eurasia Group is the institutional setting where the political-strategy intelligence around Carney is consolidated. The "Elbows Up" campaign ad with Mike Myers, the "Canada Strong" / "Building Canada Strong" / "Build Canada Homes" platform-branding family, and the broader campaign visual identity were produced in-house by the Liberal Party campaign team, with no external production company publicly named.

**4. Not, on the available evidence, shaped editorially by Diana Fox Carney.** Despite her overlapping professional vocabulary (climate finance, sustainability, resilience) and her institutional adjacency through Eurasia Group, no documented record places her in a speechwriting, messaging or branding role for her husband's communications.

**5. Increasingly subject to a coherent critical literature that identifies the language as Orwell-adjacent doublespeak.** The Fraser Institute frames it explicitly as "doublethink"; *The Globe and Mail*'s editorial board, Andrew Coyne, Jen Gerson, Aaron Wherry, William Watson, Nesrine Malik, Brendan Frank at *Orbit Policy*, Tammy Nemeth, and the Canadaland podcast have all pointed — from different ideological angles — to the same diagnostic gap: between the warmth of the rhetoric and the coldness of the actual decisions (scrapped tariffs, repealed carbon tax, dropped digital services tax, softened stance on Iran, revived Keystone XL talk, elbows-down trade posture). The rhetorical-analysis literature (Zimmer at *Manner of Speaking*, the *Tyee*) treats Carney as a structurally accomplished orator whose effects are produced by classical devices — tricolon, anaphora, antithesis, extended metaphor — deployed in a calm technical register inherited from the central bank.

The architecture, in other words, is largely Carney's own — built over twenty years inside central banking, codified in *Value(s)*, and now extended into mass political communication via *Forward Guidance* and the Davos doctrine. What he has added since entering politics is not a new voice but a new audience: a deliberate attempt to take the pastoral-technocratic register of the central banker, which had previously been addressed to financial markets and elite policy audiences, and turn it on the Canadian electorate. Whether that experiment succeeds is, as Alan Blinder warned in 2022, an open question — and the increasing critical literature suggests it may already be encountering its limits.
