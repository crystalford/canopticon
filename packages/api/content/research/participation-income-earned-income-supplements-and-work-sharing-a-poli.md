# Participation Income, Earned-Income Supplements, and Work-Sharing: A Policy Framework for Canada

## TL;DR
- **Participation income is the stronger bet for Canada.** A conditional, earnings-linked supplement built on the existing Canada Workers Benefit (CWB) architecture is far cheaper, more politically durable, and better targeted than universal basic income (UBI). The Parliamentary Budget Officer's February 19, 2025 report puts a national guaranteed basic income at $107 billion gross under the nuclear-family definition, "roughly cut in half to $53 billion" under the economic-family definition — whereas the CWB already delivers targeted top-ups automatically through the tax system at a fraction of that cost.
- **The Speenhamland/wage-suppression trap is real but solvable.** Rothstein (2010) finds employers capture a large share of wage subsidies — "$1 in EITC spending increases after-tax incomes by $0.73," and in his simulation low-skill single mothers "keep only $0.70 of every dollar" while employers capture about $0.72. The fix is a binding minimum-wage floor paired with the supplement, which makes the two policies complements rather than substitutes.
- **Work-sharing is a companion, not a substitute.** Germany's Kurzarbeit demonstrably saved jobs in downturns; France's 35-hour week is a cautionary tale that blanket hours mandates do not reliably create jobs. Canada should expand its badly underused EI Work-Sharing program (0.2% take-up vs. a 21% international peak) and normalize Dutch-style pro-rata part-time work rather than legislate a shorter week.

## Key Findings

1. **Participation income (PI)** — Anthony Atkinson's 1996 proposal in *The Political Quarterly* — pays a benefit conditional not on paid work alone but on "social contribution" broadly defined: employment, self-employment, job search, education/training, caring for dependents, disability, or approved voluntary work. It preserves the reciprocity norm that UBI abandons, which is its central political advantage. Its central weakness is administrative: verifying participation for an entire population is costly, and Atkinson estimated only ~1% would fail to qualify.

2. **Canada already has a working PI-style instrument.** The Canada Workers Benefit is a refundable tax credit that phases in at 27% of earnings above $3,000, reaches a 2025 maximum of $1,633 (singles) / $2,813 (families), then phases out at 15% above $26,855 (singles) / $30,639 (families). It is delivered automatically through the tax system with advance quarterly payments (ACWB), and self-employment/gig income counts as working income on the same terms as wages.

3. **The Speenhamland problem is empirically contested but the design lesson holds.** Revisionist history suggests the 1834 Royal Commission exaggerated Speenhamland's wage-suppressing effects, but modern EITC incidence research (Rothstein 2010) finds employers do capture a substantial share of wage subsidies absent a wage floor.

4. **A binding minimum wage is the antidote to capture — but its complementarity is asymmetric.** Neumark & Wascher (2011) and Lee & Saez (2012) find a minimum wage and an earnings subsidy are complements when paired, though the pairing helps single mothers while potentially harming childless workers.

5. **Work-sharing works in downturns, hour mandates don't reliably create jobs.** Kurzarbeit is the "gold standard" short-time work scheme; France's 35-hour week largely failed to raise aggregate employment.

## Details

### 1. Theoretical frameworks: participation income vs. UBI

Anthony Atkinson introduced participation income in "The Case for a Participation Income" (*The Political Quarterly*, 1996). In his own words, the benefit would be "paid conditional on participation… not limited to labour market participation," qualifying activities including employment, self-employment, absence due to sickness/disability, unemployment while available for work, approved education or training, caring for young/elderly/disabled dependents, and approved voluntary work. The condition "involves neither payment nor work; it is a wider definition of social contribution."

The philosophical divide: Philippe Van Parijs (author of *Real Freedom for All* and *Basic Income: A Radical Proposal*) argues for a genuinely unconditional basic income with no means test and no work test, contending that "the more broadly this condition is to be interpreted, the less of a difference there is with a basic income." Critics De Wispelaere and Stirton ("The Public Administration Case Against Participation Income," *Social Service Review*, 2007) argue PI would be an administrative nightmare and that its political trade-offs are understated. Heikki Hiilamo (University of Helsinki), in his 2022 book *Participation Income*, argues PI can outperform UBI for poverty reduction in the digital age and proposes a "targeted participation model" limited to low-income people currently reliant on social assistance — precisely the design most transferable to Canada.

Thomas Piketty's position is instructive for Canada: he is skeptical of a full UBI, warning it can be "social justice on the cheap," and in a January 2017 *Le Monde* op-ed co-signed with Saez, Bozio, Landais and others argued for delivering income support essentially as refundable tax credits on payslips of the employed rather than as an unconditional universal grant — effectively an earned-income supplement model.

### 2. Canada Workers Benefit: structure, mechanics, effectiveness

The CWB replaced the Working Income Tax Benefit in 2019. For the 2025 tax year:
- **Phase-in:** begins at $3,000 of working income, at 27% (some calculators cite 30%) of each additional dollar.
- **Maximum:** $1,633 (singles) / $2,813 (families); disability supplement up to $843.
- **Phase-out:** 15% of adjusted family net income above $26,855 (singles) / $30,639 (families), reaching zero at roughly $37,474 (singles) / $49,391 (families).
- **Secondary-earner exemption:** ~$16,386 for 2025, excluding part of the lower-earning spouse's income to protect dual-earner couples.
- **Advance payments (ACWB):** up to 50% of entitlement in three installments (single worker ~$272/installment; family ~$469).
- **Self-employment:** net self-employment income counts as working income; gig workers qualify on the same terms.

**Take-up gap:** Statistics Canada (Zheng, catalogue 75f0002m2025004) finds that among eligible T4 wage earners "the T1 filing rate was around 91%," with non-filing rates of "8.4% in 2019, 9.0% in 2020 and 9.2% in 2021" — i.e., roughly 9% of eligible earners are non-filers and therefore miss the benefit, with far higher rates (59.6%) among lapsed filers. Because the CWB is claimed through the tax return, non-filers are systematically excluded — a critical design flaw for a poverty-reduction tool. (Note: some tax-preparation sources assert a higher 25–30% non-claim figure; the Statistics Canada filing-behaviour data is the more authoritative estimate.)

### 3. UK Working Tax Credit: history and outcomes

The UK tax credit system began in 1999 (Working Families' Tax Credit replacing Family Credit), with Working Tax Credit (WTC) and Child Tax Credit introduced in 2003. Key lessons for Canada:
- **Cost overruns from low wages:** "Low wages force Britain to spend £900m more on tax credits than planned" (*The Independent*, December 2014) — direct evidence that supplements can balloon in cost when wages stagnate.
- **Overpayment/clawback problems:** the annual reconciliation model produced large overpayments (Labour "overpaid poor families by £2 billion," per *Prospect*).
- **Take-up issues** and eventual replacement by Universal Credit, which withdraws at 55p per £1 of net earnings above a work allowance.
- WTC required self-employed claimants to register with HMRC and, for those below the equivalent of 24 hours/week at minimum wage, to prove work was "genuine and effective" — an income-verification model directly relevant to gig work.

### 4. Denmark's flexicurity model

Denmark's "golden triangle" combines: (1) low employment protection (easy hire/fire); (2) generous unemployment benefits (up to two years of dagpenge via A-kasse funds); and (3) extensive active labour market programs (ALMPs) — on which Denmark spends more than any OECD country. Notably, Denmark has **no legal minimum wage**; wages are set through collective bargaining covering ~67% of workers. The AEA's 2022 assessment (Andersen, *Journal of Economic Perspectives*) concludes flexicurity alone is insufficient — "flexicurity in itself… is insufficient for successful outcomes" and requires compulsory-participation ALMPs. Marginal part-time work (<15 hours/week) has more than doubled since 2000, raising concerns about precarity.

### 5. The Speenhamland problem and wage suppression

The Speenhamland system (1795–1834) topped up agricultural labourers' wages to a bread-price-indexed subsistence level from parish rates. The 1834 Royal Commission condemned it for depressing wages and encouraging idleness; Karl Polanyi's *The Great Transformation* cemented this narrative. Revisionist scholarship (e.g., Fred Block and Margaret Somers, "In the Shadow of Speenhamland") argues "few of the indictments of Speenhamland hold up against the evidence," calling the Royal Commission report empirically fragile and noting wage-price indexing dated back to the 1349–51 Ordinance of Labourers and 1563 Statute of Artificers. The modern consensus: the standard wage-suppression story is overstated, but the *mechanism* — employers capturing subsidies absent a wage floor — is real.

**Modern EITC incidence evidence:** Jesse Rothstein (2010, *AEJ: Economic Policy*, "Is the EITC as Good as an NIT?") simulated that "a large portion of low-income single mothers' EITC payments is captured by employers through reduced wages," finding that "$1 in EITC spending increases after-tax incomes by $0.73, while $1 spent on the NIT yields $1.39." In his working-paper simulation ("Tax Incidence and the EITC"), low-skill single mothers "keep only $0.70 of every dollar they receive [and] employers of low-skill labor capture $0.72, $0.30 from single mothers plus $0.43 from ineligible workers" whose wages also fall. The NBER review (Nichols & Rothstein 2015) confirms "employers capture some of the program benefits through lower wages."

**The design solution — pair the supplement with a binding minimum wage.** Neumark & Wascher (2011, *ILR Review*, "Does a Higher Minimum Wage Enhance the Effectiveness of the Earned Income Tax Credit?") found that coupling the EITC with a higher minimum wage enhances the positive employment and earnings effect for single women with children — but the combination can *reduce* employment and earnings for childless workers and less-skilled minority men, an important asymmetry. Lee & Saez (2012, *Journal of Public Economics*) show that under efficient rationing and optimal taxes, the optimal policy combines an EITC with a binding minimum wage; as EPI (Zipperer & Rothstein 2019) summarizes, "Lee and Saez 2012… finds that, under reasonable assumptions, the optimal policy combines an EITC and a minimum wage." EPI concludes the two policies "complement one another" and "the minimum wage is an effective complement that limits any dilution." A minimum wage prevents the supplement from leaking to employers by setting a floor below which wages cannot fall.

### 6. Work-sharing and entry-level job design

**Germany's Kurzarbeit** is widely considered the "gold standard" short-time work program. Employers reduce hours instead of laying off workers; the state replaces 60–70% of lost income (via social security), for up to 12–24 months, and refunds social security contributions on reduced hours. In the 2008–09 crisis it stabilized the labour market ("German labour market miracle"). During COVID-19, the German government initially estimated 2.35 million workers (~5.5% of the workforce) would use it at an added cost of "€10 billion (US $11 billion), including €6 billion in social security contribution refunds and €4 billion in short-work money" (Tax Foundation, citing the German government) — though actual take-up peaked far higher, at roughly 6 million workers (over 20% of social-insurance employment) in April 2020 per IZA/Federal Employment Agency data. Caveat: CEPR research warns that in normal times Kurzarbeit can reduce labour reallocation and productivity by keeping workers in dying jobs (labour hoarding).

**France's 35-hour week** (Aubry laws, 1998–2002) is the cautionary tale. The government's 2002 evaluation credited it with ~300,000 jobs, but rigorous studies (Estevão & Sá, IMF Working Paper WP/06/251, "Are the French Happy with the 35-Hour Workweek?") concluded the law "failed to raise aggregate employment and increased job turnover," "increased dual job holdings by 3.3 percentage points," and increased transitions from large to small firms by ~1.2 points. The lesson: blanket legislated hours reductions do not reliably create jobs and can reduce employment for affected workers.

**Netherlands' part-time normalization** is the constructive model. Under the Flexible Working Act (Wet Flexibel Werken, 2016), employees at firms with 10+ workers have a legal right to request reduced hours after six months, refusable only for "substantial business reasons." The pro-rata temporis principle is strictly applied: part-time workers accrue holiday, pension, and benefits proportional to hours. The Netherlands has among the highest part-time employment rates and shortest average work years in the world, with full-time often defined at 36–38 hours.

**North American work-sharing.** The US and Canada both run short-time compensation (STC) programs, but take-up is minuscule. Canada's EI Work-Sharing Program allows units to cut hours 10–60% for 6–26 weeks (extendable to 38), with EI covering lost hours. A 2024 ESDC evaluation found Canada's take-up was just 0.2% of the labour force at the May 2020 pandemic peak, versus a 21% average across comparable countries — and only ~19,000 employees/year participated 2013–2020 (≈1 per 1,000 workers), largely due to low program awareness. The Brookings Hamilton Project (Abraham & Houseman) proposes mandating state work-sharing programs within the UI system. Canada expanded Work-Sharing in 2025 as a tariff-response measure.

### 7. Self-employment and gig work integration

The CWB already treats net self-employment income as working income, a model for PI. Verification is the challenge: the UK required proof that self-employed work was "genuine and effective." **Portugal's Decent Work Agenda (Law 13/2023, in force May 1, 2023)** created a rebuttable presumption of employment for digital platform workers (Article 12-A): if some of six criteria are met (platform sets pay, controls presentation/conduct, supervises via algorithm, restricts autonomy, etc.), the worker is presumed an employee and the burden shifts to the platform to disprove it. Portugal was the first legal system with such a broad presumption, covering both online and offline crowdwork. Economically dependent workers (earning ≥50% of income from one entity) gain collective-bargaining and anti-discrimination rights. The EU Platform Work Directive builds on this model. Income smoothing for variable earnings is handled through annual reconciliation (as in the CWB and UK WTC) and reference-period averaging (as in Dutch pro-rata calculations using a 13-week lookback).

### 8. Fiscal and implementation data

**UBI/GBI costs (PBO):**
- 2018 report: national GBI on Ontario pilot parameters (guarantee of $16,989 single / $24,027 couple, 50% phase-out) = $76 billion gross, ~$43 billion net after replacing $32.9 billion of existing federal supports; ~7.5 million beneficiaries.
- 2021 report: gross cost rising from ~$85 billion toward higher levels depending on phase-out rate (a 15% phase-out drives gross cost to ~$180+ billion); poverty cut nearly in half; labour-supply reduction small (~1–1.5% of hours).
- **2025 update (PBO Yves Giroux, Feb 19, 2025):** gross static cost "$107 billion in 2025 under the nuclear family unit definition… roughly cut in half to $53 billion when using the broader economic family unit"; poverty reduction of 34% (nuclear) vs. 40% (economic) on the Market Basket Measure; behavioural cost $3.6–5 billion; net cost as low as $3.6 billion after eliminating offsetting tax credits.

The Dauphin, Manitoba "Mincome" experiment (1974–79) and the Hum & Simpson review found small work-hours reductions (never exceeding ~9% for men; larger for women); a 2017 re-analysis (Calnitsky & Latner) found an 11.3 percentage-point reduction in labour-market participation, ~30% attributable to community effects, driven disproportionately by young and single-headed households.

**Administrative trade-off:** UBI's core advantage is simplicity (one payment to all). PI's cost is verification complexity. But Canada's counter is that the CWB already administers a PI-style benefit automatically through the tax system at negligible marginal cost — capturing most of PI's targeting benefits without building new verification bureaucracy. The main leak is the non-filer take-up gap (~9% of eligible earners).

### 9. Canadian context: institutions, pilots, immigration

**Think tanks:**
- **C.D. Howe Institute** documents the "welfare wall" and high marginal effective tax rates (METRs): benefit clawbacks push METRs above 50% for families with two children on employment income roughly $45,000–$65,000 in most provinces. At very low incomes ($3,000–$13,000) the CWB produces a *negative* METR (rewarding work) — "The CWB's main purpose is to counter the 'welfare wall.'" The Fraser Institute (Bazel 2024) similarly finds many working families "face METRs as high as 40% and 50%, an effective rate many would assume is reserved only for those among the highest earners." This is the core case for gentle phase-out curves.
- **Broadbent Institute** frames basic income from a social-democratic perspective, cautioning it should not replace public services and that EI "should not… be folded into a universal basic income."
- **CCPA** (Himelfarb & Hennessy, eds., *Basic Income: Rethinking Social Policy*, 2016) warns basic income "can be an excuse for governments to eliminate critically important public services and simply replace them with a cheque," and emphasizes public services over cash.

**Provincial pilots and federal bills:**
- **Quebec's Basic Income Program** (Programme de revenu de base, launched Jan 1, 2023) pays people with severe employment limitations a basic benefit of $1,336/month ($16,032/year) plus adjustments (single-person adjustment $371/month), with a generous earnings exemption (~$16,032/year) and asset allowances; eligibility requires severe health-related limitations for at least 66 of the previous 72 months. It reached ~86,289 beneficiaries in 2024-25 (~25% of the social-assistance caseload), the vast majority single.
- **PEI** proposed a Guaranteed Basic Income of 85% of the poverty line ($19,252 single / $27,227 for two adults, 50% phase-out, ages 18–64, gross first-year cost ~$189M) but implementation stalled on federal funding; a smaller operating Targeted Basic Income Guarantee (T-BIG) pilot tops up ~700 people to 85% of the market-basket measure at ~$1.7M/year, fully provincially funded.
- **Newfoundland and Labrador** launched (from 2024) a targeted basic income for adults aged 60–64 receiving income support, paying "the equivalent of the federal seniors benefits"; ~200 individuals were receiving monthly benefits as of mid-2025.
- **Federal bills:** S-233 (Sen. Kim Pate) reached the Senate National Finance Committee in 2023 but died with the 44th Parliament; C-223 (NDP MP Leah Gazan) was defeated at second reading Sept 25, 2024. Successor bills S-206 (introduced May 28, 2025) and C-253 remain in early stages. None would actually implement a basic income — they only require the finance minister to develop a "national framework."

**Immigration and labour supply:** The CWB and its provincial variants interact with immigration because eligibility requires Canadian residency throughout the tax year. Arguments that high immigration-driven labour supply suppresses low-end wages strengthen the case for pairing any earned-income supplement with a firm minimum-wage floor to prevent the supplement from subsidizing low-wage employers.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 (0–12 months): Fix the instrument Canada already has.**
1. Close the CWB take-up gap by moving toward automatic ("no-touch") enrolment for non-filers, using CRA data and pre-filled returns — recovering the ~9% of eligible earners (and far higher shares of lapsed filers) who currently miss the benefit. This captures most of PI's benefit at near-zero marginal administrative cost.
2. Raise and index the federal/provincial minimum wage floor *before or alongside* any CWB enhancement, to prevent employer capture. Benchmark: the supplement should never allow the effective wage floor to fall.

**Stage 2 (1–3 years): Reshape the CWB into an explicit participation income.**
3. Broaden qualifying "working income" to Atkinson-style participation: count approved education/training, certified caregiving, and structured volunteering toward eligibility — piloting Hiilamo's targeted model on the existing social-assistance caseload to contain verification costs.
4. Flatten the phase-out curve to keep METRs below ~50% across the $30,000–$60,000 range, coordinating federal and provincial clawbacks (the C.D. Howe agenda). Extend the secondary-earner exemption.
5. Treat gig/self-employment income identically to wages (already CWB policy) and add income-smoothing via rolling reconciliation to handle volatility.

**Stage 3 (3–5 years): Add work-sharing and part-time normalization as companions.**
6. Make EI Work-Sharing the default counter-cyclical tool: mandate awareness, simplify application, and pre-authorize rapid activation — targeting take-up rates closer to the 21% international peak than Canada's 0.2%.
7. Legislate a Dutch-style right to request reduced hours with strict pro-rata benefit protection, rather than a French-style blanket hours mandate. Do *not* legislate a halving of entry-level hours by fiat; instead subsidize voluntary hours-sharing at the entry level through the EI system.
8. Consider a Portugal-style rebuttable employment presumption for platform work to ensure gig workers accrue the pro-rata protections that make part-time work viable.

**Thresholds that would change these recommendations:** If a CWB enhancement is introduced *without* a binding wage floor and real wages for low-earners stagnate (the UK "£900m overspend" signal), shift toward stronger minimum-wage enforcement. If verification costs of broadened participation criteria exceed ~10–15% of benefits paid, retreat to the narrower earnings-based CWB. If work-sharing take-up remains below ~5% in the next downturn, adopt the Brookings mandate model.

## Caveats
- **The Speenhamland evidence is genuinely contested.** The revisionist view (Block & Somers) that wage suppression was exaggerated is scholarly but not unanimous; the modern EITC incidence findings rest on simulations with assumed elasticities, not clean natural experiments, and Rothstein himself notes the evidence is "quite limited and dated."
- **Minimum-wage complementarity is asymmetric.** Neumark & Wascher find the min-wage/EITC pairing helps single mothers but can *harm* childless workers and minority men — so a uniform prescription risks unintended distributional effects.
- **PBO cost figures are gross and sensitive to definitions.** The 2025 GBI cost swings from $53B to $107B purely on family definition; net costs depend on politically contentious assumptions about which existing programs are eliminated.
- **Kurzarbeit's success is downturn-specific.** Its job-saving benefits do not translate to permanent hours reduction; using it in normal times risks productivity losses through labour hoarding.
- **Several figures are advocacy-sourced or projections.** UBI Works cost framings, some 2026 benefit projections, and think-tank poverty-cost estimates ($73–86B) should be treated as advocacy inputs, not settled facts.
- **The CWB phase-in rate is reported inconsistently** across sources (27% vs. 30%) and thresholds vary by province (Quebec, Alberta, Nunavut have distinct configurations); verify against CRA before drafting legislation.
