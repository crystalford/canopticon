# When "Healed" Is the Treatment Working: The Stopping Mechanism in Maintenance-Dependent Conditions

The pattern you describe is well documented in pieces but has no single name. Health psychology calls its belief component the "acute model" of a chronic condition, most sharply named in asthma as the "no symptoms, no asthma" belief. Adherence research calls the behavior "intentional non-adherence" ("I stop when I feel better"). Behavior analysis explains the motivational engine: symptom relief negatively reinforces the routine, and relief then removes the routine's own motivating condition. What is missing from the literature is a general, cross-domain account of the full loop (symptom → maintenance → remission misread as cure → stopping → relapse caused by an unchanged structural cause). Nothing has been written about the two concrete examples here: a toe web space and an open dental contact. That gap is a legitimate original contribution for a CANOPTICON piece.

## TL;DR

- **It is named, but only in pieces.** Leventhal's Common-Sense Model (the acute-vs-chronic "timeline" belief), Meyer, Leventhal & Gutmann's 1985 hypertension study (patients who saw hypertension as acute dropped out of treatment), Halm et al.'s 2006 "no symptoms, no asthma" belief (held by 53% of hospitalized asthma patients), and the "intentional non-adherence" literature ("when I feel better I stop") each cover part of the loop. No source names the whole cycle across conditions, and none treats structural anatomy as the cause that never goes away.
- **Why you stop: success removes the signal and the motive.** The symptom is both the reminder and the reinforcer. When the routine works, the cue disappears and the behavior loses its reinforcement. People then judge whether to continue by what they currently observe (Rothman's maintenance theory). Rules they were told but never experienced are fragile (the rule-governed vs contingency-shaped behavior literature). Dermatology and periodontology both have explicit "maintenance after clearance" models (proactive eczema therapy; supportive periodontal therapy). Their outcome data show relapse when maintenance stops.
- **Practical test:** if the cause is anatomical, mechanical, or genetic and still present, the condition is "maintenance forever" until the structure changes. Treat symptom-free periods as evidence the routine is working, not as evidence you no longer need it.

## Key Findings

1. **The belief error has a name in health psychology: the acute (or "acute episodic") model of a chronic condition.** Leventhal's Common-Sense Model says people represent threats by identity (label and symptoms), cause, consequences, timeline, and control/cure. The timeline dimension is acute, chronic, or cyclical. Meyer, Leventhal & Gutmann (1985, *Health Psychology*) interviewed 230 hypertension patients. Patients new to treatment "were likely to drop out of treatment if: they had reported symptoms to the practitioner at the first treatment session, or they construed the disease and treatment to be acute." Even though hypertension is largely symptomless, 92% of patients in continuing treatment and 94% of re-entry patients (those who had quit and come back) said they could tell when their blood pressure was up. In other words, patients judge the condition by symptoms they feel. Halm, Mora & Leventhal (2006, *Chest*) found that 53% of 198 inner-city adults hospitalized for asthma "believed they only had asthma when they were having symptoms." That belief was tied to one-third lower odds of taking inhaled steroids while symptom-free, and it was "positively associated with expecting to be cured." This is your pattern exactly, measured.

2. **The behavior has a name in adherence science: intentional (or symptom-driven) non-adherence.** Standard adherence scales (the Morisky scale) include the item "When I feel better I sometimes stop taking my medicine." In a pilot study of intentional versus unintentional non-adherence to cancer-pain analgesics, which used the Morisky scale, stopping when feeling better was the most common non-adherence behavior, reported by 3 out of 4 patients. The WHO/WMH surveys (Kazdin et al., 2024, *Psychological Medicine*; 13 countries) found that "the main patient-reported reason for discontinuation was feeling better (46.6%)" among people stopping antidepressants. Demyttenaere et al. (2001) reported that 53% of primary-care patients had stopped antidepressants by endpoint, and "the most common reason given was 'feeling better.'" In a 2023 ambulatory-care survey, "because they felt better" (77%) was the main reason patients stopped antibiotics early. That was a conference abstract about an acute condition, and stopping early may sometimes be appropriate for antibiotics.

3. **Stopping is common even in "silent" chronic conditions.** Vrijens et al. (2008, *BMJ*; 4,783 patients with electronically monitored dosing) found that "about half of the patients who were prescribed an antihypertensive drug had stopped taking it within one year." That paper records *that* people stop, not *why*.

4. **The belief that predicts continuing: "necessity."** Horne et al.'s 2013 meta-analysis of the Necessity-Concerns Framework (94 studies, 25,072 patients, 24 long-term conditions) found that higher adherence went with stronger beliefs that the treatment is personally necessary (OR 1.742). Once symptoms clear, the felt necessity of a maintenance routine collapses, which is the mechanism in your cycle.

5. **Both of your clinical examples have established "maintenance after clearance" paradigms, and their data show relapse on stopping.** In eczema, proactive therapy (continued intermittent treatment on previously affected skin after it clears) beats reactive, flare-driven treatment. In periodontology, supportive periodontal therapy is explicitly lifelong. Patients who do not comply lose more teeth.

6. **The gap:** no peer-reviewed source found treats "remission misread as cure when the cause is structural" as a general phenomenon across domains. None names the self-extinguishing reinforcement loop as such, or links it to anatomical causes like toe spacing or open dental contacts.

## Details

### A. What exists in the literature, and the right terminology

| Concept | Source / field | What it captures | What it misses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Common-Sense Model; "timeline" and "cure/control" beliefs | Leventhal and colleagues; Meyer et al. 1985 | People treat chronic conditions as acute and stop when symptoms go | Mostly medication; not structural causes |
| "No symptoms, no asthma" / acute episodic disease belief | Halm, Mora & Leventhal 2006 | Belief that the condition exists only when symptomatic; expecting a cure | Asthma-specific |
| Intentional non-adherence | Adherence literature; Morisky scale; NICE framing | Deliberate stopping "when I feel better" | Treats stopping as a decision, not a reinforcement loop |
| Necessity-Concerns Framework | Horne et al. 2013 | Adherence tracks perceived personal need | Doesn't model how need perception decays with success |
| Initiation vs maintenance | Rothman 2000, *Health Psychology* | Starting depends on expected outcomes; continuing depends on "perceived satisfaction with received outcomes" | Assumes satisfaction sustains behavior. In your case, satisfaction (healing) ends it |
| Proactive vs reactive therapy | Atopic dermatitis literature | Formal "get control, keep control" model | Framed clinically, not psychologically |
| Supportive periodontal therapy | Periodontology | "Life-long supportive maintenance phase" | Framed as attendance compliance |
| Prevention/preparedness paradox | Public health, risk management | Successful prevention makes itself look unnecessary | Applied to organizations and populations, not personal self-care |
| "Inside every chronic patient is an acute patient wondering what happened" | F. Marcus Brown III, *J Clin Psychol* 2002;58(11):1443–1449 | The psychological gap between acute expectations and chronic reality | Commentary, not empirical |

Two terms need care. Geoffrey Rose's "prevention paradox" is a population-epidemiology idea: most cases come from the many people at low risk. It is *not* the same as "success makes prevention look unnecessary." The latter is better called the preparedness paradox or "invisible success." Harvey Fineberg's 2013 JAMA commentary, "The Paradox of Disease Prevention: Celebrated in Principle, Resisted in Practice," lists as an obstacle that "the success of prevention is invisible, lacks drama, often requires persistent behavior change, and may be long delayed." That is the closest published phrasing to your insight. Kleinman's explanatory models (a patient's own account of cause, course, and treatment) overlap with the Common-Sense Model conceptually. I found no Kleinman-derived work addressing this specific loop.

### B. Mechanisms: why stopping happens and why "healed" is misread

**1. Success removes the reinforcer (behavioral account).** In operant terms, your routine is maintained by *negative reinforcement*: moisturizing or flossing ends an unpleasant state (cracked skin, jammed food, sore gums). Behavior analysts note that when the aversive state behind a negatively reinforced behavior is gone, the person "would no longer be motivated to engage in the behavior." The discomfort is the *motivating operation*. Without it, the behavior no longer pays off in any way you can feel, and it fades much as an unreinforced behavior does. The literature on "automatic negative reinforcement" in clinical problems (e.g., compulsive buying maintained by relief from bad moods) states this logic explicitly. I found **no paper that applies it to self-care maintenance and calls it a self-defeating loop**. Here the routine's success removes the very condition that sustains it. That framing is original. It follows directly from established principles but is not itself an established finding.

**2. Success removes the cue (memory account).** The symptom is also the reminder. Habit research shows that routines tied to a stable context cue become automatic; a routine cued by a *symptom* loses its trigger when the symptom goes. That is out-of-sight, out-of-mind at the level of prospective memory. This is an inference from general habit and prospective-memory principles. I did not find a study of symptom-cued self-care decaying after remission.

**3. Satisfaction-based maintenance goes wrong (decision account).** Rothman (2000) proposes that people decide whether to keep a behavior going based on "perceived satisfaction with received outcomes." For a structural condition this produces a perverse result: the outcome (healed skin) is judged a complete success, so the effort feels finished. The model predicts continuation when outcomes satisfy. Your case shows that satisfaction can instead signal "done," because the outcome is read as a final state rather than a state the routine keeps up.

**4. The acute template (belief account).** Most everyday experience with illness is acute: cuts, colds, sprains heal and stay healed. The Common-Sense Model predicts people borrow that template. Meyer et al. and Halm et al. show the borrowing is common and predicts dropout. The misreading is not stupidity. It is a reasonable generalization from most of one's experience, applied to the minority of conditions where it fails.

**5. Absent counterfactual (attribution account).** While the routine runs, you never see what would happen without it, so the healthy state gets credited to "my body healed" rather than "my routine is holding it." Only stopping shows the counterfactual, which is why the lesson comes by trial and error. The prevention/preparedness-paradox commentary makes this point for institutions; applying it to personal self-care is interpretation.

**6. Told rules vs experienced contingencies (your secondary thread).** Skinner distinguished *contingency-shaped* behavior (learned from direct consequences) from *rule-governed* behavior (following verbal instructions). Hayes and colleagues showed experimentally that rule-governed behavior is "relatively insensitive to consequences." Rules speed up learning, but once the real contingencies change, behavior under a rule adjusts more slowly. Behavior analysts also distinguish *pliance* (following a rule for social approval, e.g., because parents said so) from *tracking* (following a rule because it accurately describes how the world works). A child told "floss every day" without the mechanism learns pliance. Once parental enforcement is gone, the behavior has neither a social nor an experienced basis, so it falls apart. The trial-and-error learner eventually gains the contingency-shaped version, which is robust, but only after paying for it. Self-determination theory supports the same conclusion from a different direction. A meta-analysis of 184 data sets (Ng et al., 2012) found autonomous (internalized) motivation and autonomy-supportive practitioners related to better health outcomes. "Because I understand why" beats "because I was told." On whether some people internalize the "why" more readily: SDT links internalization to autonomy-supportive (vs controlling) environments. A cross-sectional study of adults with eczema at a tertiary outpatient clinic found that "lower adherence to emollients was independently associated with lower conscientiousness, higher extraversion, and concomitant systemic or active topical therapy." I found no study linking need for cognition or an "empirical learner" style specifically to maintenance persistence. That remains a gap.

### C. Clinical background: the toe example

*Informational, not medical advice. Persistent web-space changes deserve an in-person diagnosis.*

- **The site is anatomically predisposed.** The web space between the fourth and fifth toes is the classic location for interdigital problems. Interdigital tinea pedis (athlete's foot) is described as most common "typically in the web space between the fourth and fifth toes." Merck notes that moisture from foot sweating facilitates fungal growth. Toes pressed tightly together trap moisture and friction (maceration). Where they are not, the skin can dry and split (fissures). Either way the geometry of the space is a standing cause. I found **no study directly measuring toe-spacing anatomy as a predictor of recurrent interdigital xerosis.** That link is plausible but not established in the literature I found. Recurring "dryness" between the toes is sometimes dry-type tinea, erythrasma, or dermatitis rather than simple xerosis, which is a reason to get it checked once.
- **Moisturizers work only while applied.** In a split-face study of stopping long-term moisturizer use, dryness increased significantly "within 1 day after discontinuation" and "reverted to baseline levels after 6 days" by objective assessment (10 days subjectively). Cosmetic-science "regression" study designs exist precisely because benefits fade once application stops, as the outer skin layer containing the moisturizer sheds.
- **Dermatology already has the "maintenance forever" model: proactive therapy.** In atopic dermatitis, proactive therapy means continuing intermittent anti-inflammatory treatment on previously affected skin after clearance, plus daily emollients. A systematic review and meta-analysis (Schmitt et al., 2011) found proactive fluticasone (RR 0.46) and tacrolimus (RR 0.78) reduced flare risk compared with vehicle. A 2026 study of patients stopping dupilumab found cumulative flare rates of 39.6% with proactive tacrolimus vs 61.6% with reactive, flare-driven treatment at 24 weeks. That was a retrospective, weighted observational comparison, not a randomized trial. The underlying rationale: follow initial control ("get control") with maintenance ("keep control").
- **Emollient adherence is poor.** In a Korean survey, at least 56.6% of AD patients did not use emollients as often as recommended. In a tertiary-clinic study, mean adherence was 65% for emollients vs 95% for systemic therapy. Relevant caveat: eczema is an immune and barrier disease, not a structural-spacing problem, so it is an analogy for your toe case, not the same condition.

### D. Clinical background: the teeth example

- **Open contacts are a recognized cause of food impaction and local periodontal damage.** Jernberg, Bakdash & Keenan (1983, *J Periodontol*) compared open contacts with the same person's closed contacts on the other side in 104 adults. Open contacts showed more probing depth (+0.27 mm) and attachment loss (+0.48 mm), and differences in food impaction between sides were linked to both. The differences were small but statistically significant. Clinical reviews describe the chain: open contact → vertical food impaction → plaque → pocket formation → more impaction. The durable fix is restoring a tight contact (restorations, crowns, orthodontics). Hygiene only manages it.
- **Why gums "heal" so quickly, and why that's misleading.** In the classic experimental gingivitis study (Löe, Theilade & Jensen, 1965), healthy subjects who stopped all oral hygiene developed gingivitis within about 9–21 days. The gums returned to health about a week after hygiene resumed. That quick reversibility is exactly what teaches the wrong lesson: the tissue *looks* cured within days, but only while cleaning continues. Later work identified "fast," "slow," and "low" responder phenotypes. That supports your intuition that people differ biologically in how much cleaning they need. Repeated cycles at a site with food impaction can progress beyond reversible gingivitis to attachment loss.
- **Interdental cleaning evidence is modest.** The Cochrane review (Worthington et al., 2019) concluded that floss or interdental brushes in addition to toothbrushing "may reduce gingivitis or plaque," with low-certainty evidence and no evidence on periodontitis or interproximal caries. There is **no trial evidence on after-every-meal cleaning for impaction sites**. That recommendation rests on mechanism and clinical judgment, which is appropriate for an anatomical outlier but is a real gap.
- **Stopping maintenance means losing teeth.** Supportive periodontal therapy is described as a "life-long supportive maintenance phase." Meta-analyses found non-compliers lose more teeth than compliers: Lee, Huang, Sun & Karimbux (2015, *J Dent Res*; 8 studies) reported a pooled tooth-loss risk ratio of 0.56 for regular versus erratic compliers, and Campos et al. (2021; 13 retrospective and 1 prospective study) found "non-compliers in SPT have an increased risk of tooth loss when compared with compliers."

### E. The broader frame: remission is not cure

The same structure appears wherever an ongoing input holds a condition in a good state: blood pressure controlled by medication (about half stop within a year), asthma controlled by inhaled steroids, depression in remission on antidepressants, back pain controlled by exercise, eczema held by emollients, periodontal stability held by maintenance visits. The shared rule is that "controlled" describes the state *with* the input, not the state of the underlying system. The acute template ("give your body time to heal") works when the cause is transient: an injury, an infection that clears. It misfires when the cause persists. In your cases the cause is geometry, and geometry doesn't heal. I found no published research on people learning this meta-lesson late in life. It is an obvious qualitative research opportunity.

### F. Gaps: where a CANOPTICON piece would be original

1. **No cross-domain name or model** for the loop. The Common-Sense Model covers beliefs, adherence research covers the stopping, and behavior analysis covers reinforcement. No one has joined them.
2. **No framing of "success removes the motive" for self-care**, even though it follows directly from motivating-operation theory.
3. **No work on structural or anatomical causes** as the defining feature of "maintenance forever" conditions, as opposed to disease processes.
4. **No study of toe-web geometry and recurrent xerosis**, and no trial of per-meal interdental cleaning at impaction sites.
5. **No research on learning the general lesson** ("some things are permanent maintenance") versus relearning narrow lessons one condition at a time, and nothing on age at insight.
6. **Little on the told-vs-understood childhood pathway** (pliance vs tracking) as a predictor of adult maintenance persistence.

### G. A suggested name

**"Self-extinguishing maintenance."** Definition: a maintenance behavior that, by succeeding, removes the symptom that cues and reinforces it, so that the successful state is misread as cure and the behavior stops, while an unchanged underlying cause guarantees relapse. A companion term for the cognitive half: **"remission-as-cure error"** (reading a maintained state as a resolved one). "Self-extinguishing" is chosen deliberately: in behavioral terms the behavior undergoes something like extinction, and it is the behavior's own success that triggers it. Flag this as a coinage, not established terminology.

### H. Interpretive note (not sourced literature): McLuhan's figure/ground

*This is interpretation, not a finding.* In McLuhan's terms the symptom is **figure**: vivid, attention-grabbing, demanding a response. Once the routine works, the routine turns into **ground**, the unnoticed environment that makes the healthy figure possible. Ground is invisible by nature, so the more completely the routine succeeds, the more thoroughly it hides its own necessity. Stopping is effectively a "probe" that flips ground back into figure. The relapse makes the hidden structure visible. That matches the trial-and-error learning you describe. The CANOPTICON move would be to make the ground visible on purpose: write down the routine, its cause, and its "remove-the-cause" exit condition, so it doesn't need a relapse to be seen.

## Recommendations: telling "maintenance forever" from "heal and done"

1. **Ask what the cause is and whether it's still there.** Transient causes (injury, infection, one-off exposure) → heal and done. Persistent causes (anatomy, spacing, genetics, chronic physiology) → maintenance until the structure changes.
2. **Use the relapse count as data.** Two recurrences at the same site with the same trigger is strong evidence of a standing cause. Stop treating each episode as new.
3. **Reframe the symptom-free state.** Write it as "controlled by X," not "healed." Borrow dermatology's language: "get control, keep control."
4. **Take the cue off the symptom.** Tie the routine to a fixed context: after the shower for the toes, after each meal for impaction sites. Then the cue doesn't disappear when the routine succeeds.
5. **Define the exit.** For each maintenance-forever item, name the structural fix that would end it (restoring the dental contact; evaluation of the web space and footwear) and discuss it with a clinician. Where the cause can be removed, removal beats lifelong maintenance.
6. **Teach mechanisms, not rules.** For children (and yourself), explain the contingency ("food sits here because these teeth don't touch"). That builds tracking rather than pliance, which the rule-governed behavior literature suggests is less fragile.

## Caveats

- Core belief findings (Meyer 1985; Halm 2006) come from specific groups (hypertension clinic patients; inner-city adults hospitalized for asthma) and rely on self-reported adherence.
- Several quantified "felt better" figures come from psychiatry and antibiotics, not skin or teeth. The antibiotic figure is a conference abstract, and stopping antibiotics early is not always wrong.
- The 2026 proactive-tacrolimus figures are observational. Proactive AD therapy evidence mainly concerns moderate-to-severe eczema.
- Food-impaction studies show small effect sizes, and interdental-cleaning evidence is low-certainty.
- The reinforcement framing, the cue-loss framing, the proposed name, and the McLuhan reading are this report's synthesis. They are consistent with established principles but not tested as such.
