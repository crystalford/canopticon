# The Documentary Voice: A Specification for Investigative/Forensic Production Type

This report extracts operational guidelines for a "documentary" production type to sit alongside (and orthogonal to) the existing "video essay" type. The defining axis to encode: the video essay narrator is *thinking out loud* about an idea in the present; the documentary narrator is *walking the listener through evidence* about something that happened or is happening. Both can be argumentative, but the documentary earns its argument by surfacing what wasn't visible before, not by reasoning out loud. The emphasis throughout is on the forensic/investigative subtype the operator wants to use as the working default.

---

## 1. ANALYSIS OF MAJOR DOCUMENTARY VOICES

### Errol Morris — the interview as forensic instrument

Morris is the cleanest reference for "documentary as epistemology." His self-description of *The Thin Blue Line* is foundational: "two movies grafted together. On one simple level: did he do it, or didn't he? And on another level, *The Thin Blue Line* properly considered is an essay on false history. A whole group of people, literally everyone, believed a version of the world that was entirely wrong." That sentence alone defines what the operator means by "forensic" — the documentary's job is not chronology, it's the disassembly of an accepted account.

Operational features to encode from Morris:

- **Interview as evidence, not commentary.** The Interrotron device exists so subjects look directly at the camera while talking; Morris treats the interview as the primary forensic instrument, not as a way to gather opinion. Interviews are extracted, contradictions are surfaced, and the narrator's role is to mark where accounts do not align.
- **Contradiction as structure.** *The Thin Blue Line* is built around incompatible eyewitness accounts (the famous floating-milkshake reenactment exists to show *how* their accounts diverge, not what "really happened"). The documentary doesn't resolve contradictions by fiat; it lays them down beside each other.
- **Withholding to build participation.** Morris talks about creating "the illusion that they are speaking directly to" the audience, an "inclusive experience" where the viewer is positioned as a co-investigator. The narrator does not announce the conclusion; the narrator stages the discovery.
- **Worked as a private detective.** Morris sees "truth as a quest"; his quoted methodology: "I don't believe truth is conveyed by style and presentation… if you obey a set of documentary conventions truth magically pops out — well, that's not the way it works." Encode the implication: documentary register is not a set of rhetorical flourishes; it is the discipline of attribution and verification.
- **Philosophical first-semester questions.** Morris's collaborator quote: "I was not interested in a story unless it contained a first-semester philosophy question." For the system: the documentary spine should always answer "what is the question this piece is investigating?" — and that question should be specific and answerable, not a thematic mood.

### Adam Curtis — the archive as prosecutorial montage

Curtis is the reference for the *historical-investigative* documentary that asserts a thesis through assembled archive rather than through interviews. He explicitly calls himself a journalist who happens to use documentary as the medium. His mode is closer to the operator's brief than Morris's in one important respect: Curtis is openly arguing, but he argues by *showing the lineage of an idea or system over decades*, not by reasoning through a position in real time.

Encode from Curtis:

- **The "this is the story of how we got here" frame.** *HyperNormalisation* opens by promising "the epic story of how we got to this strange place." That frame — "here is how the present became the present, through a chain of events most people don't see connected" — is an excellent default cold-open shape for forensic documentary on systemic topics.
- **Counter-narrative posture.** Curtis introduces "new facts and data to create a counter-narrative to established media narratives." The documentary register is implicitly oppositional: it is correcting an accepted story.
- **Past-tense narration with present-tense stakes.** Curtis's voiceover is almost entirely past-tense: "In 1975, a man named… believed that…" The stakes (why this matters now) are stated explicitly at the start and the end, not threaded throughout. This is the exact split the operator's brief describes.
- **The named protagonist of an idea.** Curtis personifies abstractions: a theorist, a banker, an engineer becomes the carrier of a concept. The system should encode this as a pattern: when introducing a system or trend, name the human who actually did the thing.
- **Caveat:** critics note Curtis can drift into "diverting collage" and "rambling bar-room anecdote." The system should avoid the failure mode where the through-line gets lost in tangents.

### Werner Herzog — first-person philosophical narration

Herzog is the reference for *authored* documentary narration where the narrator is openly present, opinionated, and reflective. He records his own voiceover, writes scripts in real time while editing, and explicitly says: "If I left myself, as a person, out of it — and I only had my voice — it was kind of effective. And it's not just the voice. It's what I'm saying." His narration is "philosophical, poetic, probing, personal and irreverent."

For the documentary type the operator wants, Herzog should be a *bounded* reference, not a default. His mode is performative-poetic; the operator wants forensic. But two Herzog patterns are essential:

- **Permission to interpret what we are looking at.** In *Grizzly Man*, watching Treadwell's footage, Herzog says, "I see no kinship, no understanding, no mercy. I see only the overwhelming indifference of nature." The narrator interprets the evidence rather than just presenting it. Documentary register allows this *as long as the interpretation is plainly marked as the narrator's.* Herzog never confuses his reading with the record.
- **Comfort with absence and the unknowable.** Herzog often closes by stating what cannot be known, which is itself a form of forensic honesty.

### Frontline (PBS) — the institutional investigative register

Frontline is the closest existing template for the operator's "default documentary" register. Its self-description is precise: "investigative journalism that questions, explains and changes our world." The institutional voice (long associated with Will Lyman) is calm, declarative, attribution-heavy, third-person, and structured around interview-as-evidence intercut with archival material.

Frontline's craft features:

- **Reportorial sourcing in the spoken voice.** "Court records show…" "According to documents obtained by Frontline…" "Three former employees told us…" These constructions translate the apparatus of print investigative journalism into something a narrator can actually say without sounding like a footnote.
- **Calm under stakes.** A reviewer's description — "deep, dispassionate, yet somewhat sexy voice… resonant gravitas" — captures the register: the *content* is high-stakes; the *voice* refuses to escalate to match. Tonal restraint is itself a credibility signal.
- **Story-but-not-spin.** "Frontline lets you make up your own mind by presenting testimony of the people involved. It's a program whose narration isn't filled with conclusions worded to sound like fact." This is the central register difference from a video essay: the essay narrator concludes; the documentary narrator stages.
- **Anatomy structure.** Frontline pieces typically open with a precise question or development, lay out the actors involved, walk through evidence chronologically *or* thematically (depending on the case), present competing accounts, and close with consequences — what changed, what is still unresolved, what is at stake going forward.

### Defunctland (Kevin Perjurer) — the YouTube long-form investigative documentary

Defunctland is the most operationally relevant reference for a *YouTube-native, written-and-narrated* investigative documentary. *Disney Channel's Theme: A History Mystery* (2022) is a 92-minute investigation into who composed a four-note jingle. That subject sounds trivial; the piece works because the structure is properly forensic.

What Perjurer encodes:

- **A single, narrow, answerable question as the spine.** "Who wrote this?" Not "what does this tell us about Disney" — that's the meaning that emerges. The investigation itself is bounded and concrete.
- **Showing the search.** The film follows Perjurer's own process: googling, message-boarding, contacting the boutique sonic-branding industry, getting referred down chains of contacts. He "trusts that the excitement of following his meandering and meticulous research is enough to hold the interest of the viewer."
- **The accidental second documentary inside the first.** Perjurer's investigation surfaces the meta-question of who gets credit for commercial work, ending in "a moving tribute to an unsung hero." The structural lesson: a forensic documentary often produces a *second*, larger meaning by the time it has finished answering its narrow question. Encode this — the close should not just answer the question but reveal the larger thing the answer turned out to be about.
- **Restraint vs. video-essay flippancy.** Reviewers explicitly note: "Perjurer largely refrains from the jocular tone and flippant asides that characterize a lot of video essays on YouTube." This is exactly the register split the operator wants.
- **Deadpan delivery.** TV Tropes describes Perjurer's narration as "utterly deadpan at all times, which creates quite the contrast when paired with the whimsical language he often uses." Tonal flatness lets evidence do the lifting.
- **Other Defunctland forensic pieces** — *Disney's FastPass: A Complicated History* (2021), the Jim Henson DefunctTV series, the Disney Animatronics two-parter — share a structure: open with the artifact/system, trace its origin, walk through the decision points, document the consequences, close on what it reveals about the institution.

### LemmiNo — the atmospheric mystery investigation

David Wångstedt's LemmiNo channel (e.g. *The Vanishing of Flight 370*, *The Search for D.B. Cooper*) demonstrates a stripped-down forensic register: calm voiceover, custom timeline graphics illustrating evidence, archival material, and an explicit refusal to overclaim. He takes unsolved historical mysteries and walks the listener through the actual case file. The takeaway for the system: when investigating an unresolved case, the documentary close is not "here's my theory"; it is "here is what the evidence does and does not support."

### Internet Historian / Wendigoon — the storytelling-first end of the spectrum

Internet Historian and Wendigoon mark the *limit* of the documentary register before it slides into entertainment-narrative. Internet Historian's "Man in Cave" (about Floyd Collins) tells a true historical event in a cinematic, character-driven way. Wendigoon's true crime and conspiracy iceberg pieces lean on dramatic delivery and atmospheric voice. Both are watchable, both are popular, but both should be *bounded references* — useful for pacing and hook construction but not as the default register for investigation, because they tend toward narrative-as-entertainment rather than evidence-as-argument. Internet Historian was also publicly criticized for plagiarism and fabricated detail in 2023, which is a cautionary marker: when register slides toward entertainment, accuracy discipline tends to slide with it.

### Dan Olson / Folding Ideas — *Line Goes Up* and the financial-investigative register

*Line Goes Up – The Problem with NFTs* (2 hours 18 minutes) is the most influential recent example of *investigative-financial* YouTube documentary. Its features:

- **Argument built on a wall of citation.** Olson's "solemn yet sardonic delivery — plus his stream of screenshotted citations — positioned him as an authority for the uninitiated." The citations are *shown on screen*, not narrated, which is the right answer to the named-source problem in voice (see §5).
- **Historical-systemic frame.** Olson opens with the 2008 financial crisis to contextualize Bitcoin's origin, then traces the technology forward. The investigation is a chain of "and then…" moves, each grounded in a documented event.
- **Acknowledgement of uncertainty and counterargument.** Critics note Olson "doesn't really present any solutions" and didn't "truly consider the other side"; this is a real weakness, and the system should encode acknowledging the strongest opposing view as part of the close.
- **Length is justified by structure, not padded.** The piece has thirteen named segments. Each segment has a concrete claim and supporting evidence. The system should encode visible internal structure (segment titles, section breaks) as a way to make long-form forensic work navigable.

### *Serial* / *S-Town* / *This American Life* — audio documentary as prose-register reference

Sarah Koenig's *Serial* is the most precise reference for *spoken* investigative register, because it is audio-only and so every choice is a writing choice. From Koenig's own description and the show's reception:

- **"An anatomy of an investigation, and an anatomy of a trial and conviction… break it down into its pieces to see how the parts came to be a whole."** This is the operator's brief in one sentence. The forensic documentary's structural mode is *anatomy*, not chronology.
- **Show the wrong turns.** Koenig's rule: "If it's interesting to us, we're going to assume it's interesting to you. As long as we're responsible, not throwing stuff out there that's totally half-cocked, and as long as we can corroborate what we're doing." Failed leads, abandoned theories, and reversed assumptions are *kept in*. This is what makes the listener a co-investigator rather than a passive recipient. (Note the ethical critique below: showing the search also implicates the audience, and *Serial* has been criticized for trading the Lee family's pain for cliffhangers.)
- **Spare narration; raw tape carries weight.** "Koenig's narration is spare and courtroom-clean; when possible, raw tape carries weight — phone calls, interviews, courtroom audio." The narrator's job in spoken voice is to *frame and connect* primary material, not to over-narrate it.
- **Episodes operate like affidavits.** "Each opens on a precise question, builds through corroboration or conflict, and closes where leverage shifts." Encode this as a default per-section template.
- **"Ethical disclosure of method."** Listeners hear "not only what the record says, but how journalists verify, revise, or abandon a lead." The documentary shows the apparatus; the video essay generally does not.

### Radiolab — the explanatory-investigative hybrid

Radiolab's mode (multiple narrator voices, a guide who is openly curious, a layered "we found this, then we found this, then this changed everything") is useful as a sub-mode for documentaries on technical or scientific subjects, where the investigation is *into how something works* rather than *into what someone did*. The pattern: a question, a partial answer, a complication that overturns the partial answer, a deeper answer.

### 30 for 30 — sports documentary as accessible forensic narrative

30 for 30's contribution is *character-driven institutional investigation* — the typical episode treats a single event or career as a window into a larger system (the league, the era, the sport's economics). The voice is generally observational-third-person, and the structure usually moves from "the event everyone thinks they remember" backward to "what was actually going on around it." For the system: when a topic is well-known on the surface, this *what-was-actually-going-on-around-it* angle is the canonical documentary frame.

### True crime serial documentaries — *The Jinx*, *Wild Wild Country*, *Tiger King*, *The Staircase*, *Making a Murderer*

The academic literature treats this as a now-codified genre. The shared structural pattern is the three-act thriller adaptation: hook with the crime/mystery, a long second act of "convoluted second act peppered with shady characters and red herrings," and a climax that often *deliberately resists closure*. Stella Bruzzi notes that "resistance to closure" is a defining feature of this subgenre.

These are *useful as pacing references but problematic as the default register*, for reasons covered in §8. *The Jinx*'s "real-time interviews with Robert Durst, transforming an otherwise retrospective story into an active investigation" is a good model for how to make a closed historical record feel like an ongoing investigation; *Making a Murderer* and *The Staircase* are warnings about how easily this register can be steered by the filmmaker's pre-existing belief about who is innocent.

### Bellingcat — open-source-intelligence investigative storytelling

Bellingcat is the reference for the *evidence-foregrounded* end of the spectrum. Their public guides on "how to tell stories" for open-source researchers explicitly translate investigative method into narrative — "open source research begins by pulling on a thread… keeping the narrative as clear, informative, and as readable as possible is the only way to make sure your work makes an impact." Their reports are notable for *transparency about method*: they describe "how they found out the story and which techniques they used." For a voice profile: when handling primary-source-heavy material (satellite imagery, public records, leaked documents), the narrator should briefly state the method by which the evidence was obtained, not just the conclusion drawn from it.

## 2. STRUCTURAL PATTERNS — THE DOCUMENTARY SKELETON

A documentary structure differs from a video essay structure in five concrete ways:

1. **The cold open is concrete, not conceptual.** A video essay can open with a question, an idea, a frame. A documentary opens with an artifact, a moment, a person, a record, an event. Something *happened* (or is happening), and that something is shown or named in the first 60 seconds. Examples: Defunctland's *Disney Channel's Theme* opens with the four notes themselves; *Serial* opens with a teenage boy's phone call; *HyperNormalisation* opens with a flashlight in a dark forest and the line "We live in a time of great uncertainty and confusion."
2. **Stakes are stated explicitly, then earned.** Curtis-style: "This film is the epic story of how we got to this strange place. It explains not only why these chaotic events are happening — but also why we, and our politicians, cannot understand them." That explicit stake-statement is permitted (and often required) in documentary register because the listener needs a contract — *here is what I am promising you we will figure out together*. A video essay typically does not need this contract because the contract is "I'm thinking through this with you."
3. **Subjects are named and characterized.** Documentary introduces real people, institutions, or places as *characters*, complete with relevant biographical context. Video essays often refer to thinkers or examples without giving them this kind of weight. The system should encode: when a person is named in a documentary, give them a one-sentence epithet that situates them ("Fred Leuchter, an execution-equipment engineer from Massachusetts," "Alexei Yurchak, a Soviet-born anthropologist at Berkeley").
4. **Evidence is layered and weighted, not enumerated.** Lists of facts work in print and fail in voice (see §5). Documentary structure introduces one piece of evidence, lets it sit, draws an implication from it, then introduces the next piece — each new piece of evidence either *confirms the implication* (which strengthens the case) or *complicates it* (which becomes the next turn). This is the affidavit pattern: each section opens on a question, builds through corroboration or conflict, closes where the leverage shifts.
5. **The synthesis/close is consequential, not concluding.** A video essay close typically lands the argument. A documentary close typically does one of three things: (a) names what the investigation has changed about how we should see the topic, (b) names what is still unresolved and why that matters, or (c) reveals the larger meaning the narrow question turned out to be about. *Disney Channel's Theme* ends not on "we found him" but on a meditation on artistic legacy and unseen creators; *Line Goes Up* ends on a structural critique of where this technology leads if unchecked; *Serial* Season 1 famously refuses to "solve" the case and instead delivers a meditation on reasonable doubt.

### Default Documentary Skeleton (for the system spec)

Encode the following as the default scaffold, scalable to length:

1. **COLD OPEN (≈5–10% of runtime).** A concrete artifact, scene, or moment that *contains* the question. End the cold open by stating the question explicitly — "And this is what we wanted to find out." or equivalent.
2. **STAKES & FRAME (≈5%).** Why this matters. What the accepted version of this story is, and where we have reason to think the accepted version is wrong or incomplete. This is the contract with the listener.
3. **CHARACTER & GROUND (≈10–15%).** The people, place, institution, or system involved. Background only as much as the investigation will require — no more.
4. **EVIDENCE / ANATOMY (≈50–60%).** The body. Build in 3–7 named segments, each of which functions like an affidavit: opens on a sub-question, presents primary evidence, surfaces what that evidence implies, and ends on a turn (a confirmation that strengthens the case, or a complication that bends the next segment). At least one segment should explicitly stage *the moment our reading changed* — the discovered contradiction, the document that reframed everything, the interview that broke the official story. This is the forensic equivalent of the Act II midpoint reversal.
5. **COMPETING ACCOUNTS (≈5–10%).** Explicitly handle the strongest version of the opposing or complicating reading. Do not bury it. The narrator names what the other view holds and why a reasonable person could hold it, then states what the evidence does or does not support against that view.
6. **WHAT IS STILL UNKNOWN (≈5%).** Forensic discipline requires this. The narrator names the questions the investigation could not answer and why — what records are sealed, what witnesses are dead, what has not been independently verified.
7. **SYNTHESIS / CONSEQUENCE (≈10%).** The close. Not "and so we have proven X" (essay-close) but "and here is what this means about [the larger system / the people involved / what should now be done / what we should now understand differently]." If the investigation is unresolved, the close is a precise statement of the unresolved state and what would resolve it.

This skeleton is deliberately modular: a 12-minute YouTube documentary uses all seven sections compressed; a 90-minute long-form doubles the body and breaks the EVIDENCE section into named acts.

## 3. PROSE REGISTER — DOCUMENTARY VS. VIDEO ESSAY

### Tense

The default documentary tense is **past with present-marked stakes**. Events under investigation are narrated in simple past ("In March 2017, the company filed for bankruptcy"). The narrator's framing of why this matters is in present ("This bankruptcy is the door we're going to walk through"). Within scenes, *historical present* can be used to dramatize a specific moment ("It is 4 a.m. The phone rings. Adams picks up.") but should be used sparingly and signaled clearly.

This is different from broadcast TV news, which uses present tense as default ("A community is mourning tonight…") for immediacy. Documentary-investigative register prefers past-tense factual narration *because past tense carries the implicit claim of having-been-investigated*. The tense is part of the credibility apparatus.

It is also different from video essay, which is overwhelmingly present-tense ("So this is what I think is going on…") because the video essay is a present-tense act of thinking.

### Person

Documentary defaults to **third person**, with first-person plural ("we") permitted for the investigative apparatus ("we obtained," "we found," "we asked"). First-person singular ("I") is permitted but should be marked — used when the narrator is genuinely a participant in the investigation (Defunctland on-screen, Koenig in *Serial*) or when the narrator is openly interpreting (Herzog). The system default should be third-person/first-plural; first-person singular is an opt-in mode.

Video essay, by contrast, is overwhelmingly first-person singular and present.

### The "according to" register — how attribution works in voice

This is the most important micro-craft point. In print, citations are footnotes or parentheticals. In voice, they have to be sentence-shaped. The documentary register has a small set of recurring attribution moves that the system should encode as natural-sounding sentence templates:

- "According to [source], …"
- "[Source] told us that…"
- "Court records show…"
- "In a [year] interview with [outlet], [person] said…"
- "Internal emails — later released in discovery — describe…"
- "What [person] would later admit was that…"
- "Two former employees, who asked not to be named, described…"
- "By [person]'s own account…"
- "There is no public record of…"

These constructions perform a specific job: they tell the listener *where the next claim came from* without breaking the rhythm. Crucially, the broadcast-journalism rule applies: **attribution comes before the claim, not after**. In print: "'The investigation is ongoing,' said Police Chief Jane Smith." In documentary voice: "Police Chief Jane Smith says the investigation is ongoing." The listener cannot re-read; they need to know who is speaking before they hear the claim.

### Handling uncertainty

Where a video essay can argue past uncertainty ("I think this is basically what's happening"), documentary qualifies. The system should encode a small register of uncertainty-marking phrases:

- "What the records confirm is X. What they don't tell us is Y."
- "We cannot say with certainty that…, but…"
- "The available evidence is consistent with… but does not prove…"
- "[Person] denies this. The documents tell a different story."
- "On this point, the accounts diverge."

### Dramatic irony — narrator knows where this ends, listener does not

This is the documentary's structural advantage over the essay. The narrator has finished the investigation; the listener is being walked through it. This permits *strategic withholding*: planting a fact early that will become significant later, framing a person's quoted denial in a way that the eventual evidence will contradict, naming a date as a marker that the listener will encounter again. Sarah Koenig's line — "I've got this thing in my head that I'll catch him in a lie" — is a good example: she is signaling to the listener that what follows is going to test that hypothesis. The system should encode this as a permitted move: the narrator may foreshadow what is coming, but only by setting up structural expectations, not by spoiling the answer.

### Pacing techniques

Three techniques are characteristic of documentary register and can be encoded as template patterns:

- **Scene → reflection.** A specific moment is rendered, then the narrator steps back to say what it meant. ("Inside the warehouse that night, three things were happening at once. [scene.] What no one in the building knew was…")
- **Evidence → implication.** A piece of evidence is named, then its consequence is drawn out. ("The wire transfer cleared at 11:47 p.m. By midnight, the account had been emptied. The company would file for bankruptcy in nine days.")
- **Then → now.** The historical moment is set against the present. ("In 1981, this was an obscure regulation buried in an FCC ruling. In 2024, it would be the legal hook for the largest media merger in American history.")

These three patterns are the connective tissue of forensic documentary prose. Encode them as available transitions between sections.

### Narrator's relationship to subject

Four stances are available, and the system should make this an explicit parameter:

- **Analytical** (Frontline default; Morris) — neither sympathetic nor hostile to subjects; interested in what happened.
- **Sympathetic** (Herzog on Treadwell; Defunctland on Lasarenko) — the narrator is openly moved by or invested in a subject without abandoning the investigation.
- **Skeptical** (Olson on NFT promoters; Curtis on power) — the narrator is openly unconvinced by the subject's account and is testing it.
- **Advocate** (Koenig leaning toward Adnan Syed's innocence; some Bellingcat work on war crimes) — the narrator is making a case. This is the closest documentary register comes to video-essay register, and is the most ethically demanding because it must *still* engage seriously with counter-evidence.

The system default for the operator's "forensic exploration" use case should be **analytical with skeptical undertones**: presume nothing, test everything, name what cannot be confirmed.

## 4. INVESTIGATIVE / FORENSIC TECHNIQUES

### Building a case across an entire piece

The forensic documentary's central craft challenge is *cumulative argument*. No single piece of evidence carries the case; the case is built by laying down evidence in an order such that each new piece raises the cost of the alternative explanation. The system should encode this as: the body's segments are ordered not by chronology but by *evidentiary weight and surprise* — open with the publicly available, build through the not-widely-known, peak on the genuinely new, then close with the structural implication.

### The role of physical evidence, documents, records, timelines

The documentary register has a tactile relationship to evidence. The narrator names *what kind of thing* the evidence is:

- "This is a memo, dated March 14, 2009, from the company's general counsel to the CEO."
- "The flight data recorder, recovered three weeks later from the seabed…"
- "A timeline assembled from court filings, billing records, and one survivor's own diary…"

This is the opposite of the video essay's conceptual mode. The system should encode a strong preference for *naming the specific document, dating it, and stating its provenance* whenever a substantive claim depends on it.

### Interviews as evidence vs. as commentary

A documentary uses interviews two ways. As **evidence**, the interview produces a fact, contradiction, or admission ("the foreman testified under oath that he had never seen the report"). As **commentary**, the interview produces a perspective ("a labor historian who has studied the period describes the era as…"). The system should encode this distinction: interview-as-evidence material should be deployed during the body/anatomy section to advance the case; interview-as-commentary material is appropriate during the frame, the competing-accounts section, and the synthesis.

### Narrator positioning

The system should support two positions and let the operator pick:

- **Embedded investigator.** "We started looking into this because…" "Our reporting began…" "When we asked the company about…" — the narrator is one of the people who did the work. (Frontline, Bellingcat, Defunctland)
- **Detached chronicler.** "Investigators eventually concluded…" "Court records show…" "What follows is what the evidence supports…" — the narrator is a synthesizer of an investigation that has already happened. (Curtis, much of 30 for 30, historical documentary)

The embedded mode is closer to the operator's brief ("forensic exploration of actual topics — investigating subjects in depth"). The detached mode is appropriate when the system is reporting on an investigation that already exists in the public record.

### Competing accounts

Forensic discipline requires *steelmanning the alternative* before dismissing it. The pattern: name the alternative reading, name who holds it, name why a reasonable person could hold it, then state precisely what the evidence does or does not support against it. Morris's *Thin Blue Line* is the canonical example — he gives the prosecution's version full voice before disassembling it.

### Absence as structural material

What is missing is itself evidence. Sealed records, dead witnesses, destroyed documents, refused interviews, unreturned calls — naming these in voice is a forensic move. The narrator says "We asked the company for comment. They did not respond." This is information; it should be in the documentary, not hidden. Bellingcat's transparency about method is the best contemporary model for this.

### Ethics in spoken voice

The investigative documentary makes accusations. The register has to manage this responsibly:

- **Distinguish fact, allegation, and inference.** "Smith was charged with…" is fact. "Smith allegedly…" is allegation. "The pattern suggests Smith may have…" is inference. The narrator's language must match.
- **Right of reply.** Whenever a person is being accused or critically characterized, the narrator should state whether they were asked to respond and what they said (or that they declined).
- **Burden of proof.** "Innocent until proven" applies to the spoken record. If a person was acquitted, charged-but-not-convicted, or never charged, the narration must reflect that clearly.
- **Avoid trial-by-narration.** Watch the *Making a Murderer* failure mode: presenting selected evidence in support of a thesis without making the selection visible.

### Handling the unresolved

When the investigation has not finished, the documentary close has a specific shape: state precisely what is known, state precisely what is not known, state what would resolve it, state what is at stake in its remaining unresolved. *Serial* Season 1 is the canonical model. The system should encode this as a permitted close-form distinct from the conclusive close.

## 5. THE SPOKEN-VS-WRITTEN PROBLEM IN DOCUMENTARY

This is where the operator's system has the most leverage, because the script is being read aloud.

### The wall-of-evidence problem

In print, an investigative piece can spend a paragraph listing twelve discrete findings. In voice, a list of twelve facts is unhearable past about three. The solution is *grouping and cascading*: the narrator names the *category* of evidence, gives one or two specific examples, and indicates the rest exists. "We obtained more than four hundred internal emails. The pattern across them is consistent: in March, executives were privately describing the product as unsafe; in April, the marketing team launched a campaign calling it the safest on the market." Two specific instances, the rest implied. The system should encode a hard limit (probably three) on consecutive enumerated facts in spoken voice.

### The named-source problem

In print: "(Smith, 2019, p. 47)" or "according to a 2019 report by the Ford Foundation." In voice, this sentence-rhythm is dead. The solution is the broadcast-journalism rule: *attribute first, claim second, and use the simplest version of the source that still does the job*. "A 2019 Ford Foundation study found…" is sayable. Where on-screen text can carry the precise citation, the script should keep the spoken attribution lean and let the visual carry the apparatus. Olson's *Line Goes Up* is the model: spoken attribution is short; on-screen citation is dense.

### The chronology trap

A strict chronological retelling of a long-running event is the most common failure mode for spoken documentary. The early parts have low stakes; by the time the listener arrives at the high-stakes part, attention has drifted. The solution, demonstrated by *Serial*, *The Jinx*, and most Frontline pieces, is *anatomy structure*: open in medias res with the moment of highest stakes (the body, the verdict, the collapse), then move through the contributing factors thematically. Chronology becomes a *segment-level* concern, not a piece-level one.

### Dense evidentiary material in voice

Three techniques work:

1. **The narrator names the shape of the evidence before the contents.** "What the spreadsheet shows is a pattern. From 2014 through 2017, every month, the same three accounts were credited with the same suspicious round numbers." The listener now has a frame for the data.
2. **A representative case stands in for the data set.** Instead of summarizing twenty fraud cases, the narrator walks through one in detail and says "this same pattern appears nineteen more times in the records."
3. **Visual / textual material carries the density; voice carries the meaning.** The script tells the editor what is on screen ("the spreadsheet, with three columns highlighted"), and the narration speaks the *implication* of what is on screen, not its contents.

## 6. PRACTITIONER ADVICE — RECURRING THEMES

Across Morris, Curtis, Herzog, Koenig, Perjurer, Olson, and Frontline producers, a small set of practitioner principles recur:

- **Cut where the leverage is.** Koenig: "If it's interesting to us, we're going to assume it's interesting to you." But the converse rule is implicit in every successful piece — if it isn't pulling its weight, it goes, no matter how much research went into getting it.
- **Show the search.** Koenig, Perjurer, Olson, and Bellingcat all explicitly include the *process* of investigation in the final product. The dead ends and reversals are not bugs; they are how the listener becomes a co-investigator and how the conclusion earns its weight.
- **Write to the edit, not from the script.** Herzog: "I write [scripts] in real time while editing the reams of footage I gather." Most documentary practitioners describe writing *against* the existing material rather than scripting first. For an AI script-generation system, the analogous discipline is: the script is shaped by what evidence is actually available, not what would be rhetorically convenient.
- **Read it aloud.** Universally cited rule. If the narrator stumbles over it, it is wrong. Sentences over twenty words rarely survive.
- **Trust the audience.** *Serial*, *Line Goes Up*, *HyperNormalisation*, and Defunctland's long-form are all evidence that audiences will follow long, dense, demanding investigative work *if the structure is honest about what is happening at each step.* The failure mode is condescension and over-summary.
- **Don't conclude past your evidence.** Morris: "I don't believe truth is conveyed by style and presentation… if you obey a set of documentary conventions truth magically pops out — well, that's not the way it works."

## 7. ACADEMIC / CRITICAL FRAME

### Bill Nichols's six modes

Nichols's modes (poetic, expository, observational, participatory, reflexive, performative) are the standard taxonomy. For the operator's "forensic-investigative" type, the relevant register is a hybrid:

- **Expository** as the spine — voiceover narration, argumentative logic, evidence subordinated to the spoken thread. This is what the operator wants by default.
- **Participatory** as a sub-mode — the Morris/Koenig/Perjurer move where the investigator is a visible actor. Available when the operator wants embedded-investigator framing.
- **Reflexive** in small doses — the system should permit moments where the narrator acknowledges the construction of the documentary itself ("we don't know how to verify this," "we asked, and they wouldn't say").

The operator should *avoid* defaulting to **observational** (no narrator, fly-on-the-wall — incompatible with the operator's "operator reads aloud" use case), **poetic** (mood-driven, non-argumentative — closer to art film than investigation), and unbounded **performative** (filmmaker's subjectivity foregrounded — slides toward video essay register).

### History of documentary narration

The arc of documentary narration runs roughly: **Voice-of-God** (1930s–60s authoritative omniscient narrator, often male, often unattributed — *The Plow That Broke the Plains*, *Why We Fight*) → **observational/direct cinema reaction** (1960s–70s, narration treated as suspect — *High School*, the Maysles, Wiseman) → **participatory/embedded narrator** (1970s onward, the filmmaker is in the film — Morris, Broomfield, Theroux) → **first-person essay film** (Herzog, Marker, eventually Curtis) → **YouTube-era hybrid** (Defunctland, Olson, LemmiNo — solo authored, narrator-as-investigator, mid-budget, often released free to platform).

The relevant lesson for the system: the pure Voice-of-God register is dated and now reads as either institutional (Frontline, BBC nature documentary) or parodic. The credible contemporary investigative narrator is *named, situated, and accountable* — the listener understands who is speaking, why they are looking into this, and what their relationship to the material is.

### Documentary vs. journalism vs. essay film vs. true crime

These categories overlap heavily. The operationally useful distinction:

- **Documentary** uses the discipline of journalism (sourcing, attribution, right-of-reply, evidentiary standard) but with the structural and prose ambitions of literature.
- **Journalism** in pure form is news; it does not require structural ambition.
- **Essay film** uses documentary techniques but its argumentative structure is closer to the personal essay; truth-claims are subordinated to the author's reasoning.
- **True crime** is documentary applied to crime, with all the standard documentary craft and a specific set of pitfalls (§8).

For the operator's spec: the documentary type is *the discipline of journalism delivered with structural and prose ambitions*. That definition handles every relevant case.

### YouTube documentary as subgenre

The contemporary YouTube long-form documentary (Defunctland, Folding Ideas, LemmiNo, parts of Wendigoon and Internet Historian, much of the video essay community when it shades investigative) is now a recognized form. Its features:

- Solo or near-solo authored.
- 30 minutes to 3 hours.
- Heavy use of on-screen citation and visual evidence.
- Single-narrator, recorded-at-desk voice.
- Released free to platform; supported by Patreon/sponsorship rather than commission.
- Frequently treats subjects beneath the threshold of legacy documentary attention (a four-note jingle, a fan-made conspiracy, a defunct theme-park attraction) — and uses them to investigate larger systems.

The system should treat YouTube long-form as the *native medium* for the documentary type, not as a degraded version of legacy documentary.

## 8. NEGATIVE EXAMPLES — WHAT TO FORBID

The system should explicitly avoid the following failure modes, all of which are well-documented in critical literature on the form:

1. **True crime that exploits rather than investigates.** *Serial* itself has been criticized for "reporting of an investigation [Koenig] had not finished, along with her tendency to withhold information for the sake of cliffhangers," which "provoked listeners to conduct their own research at the cost of [the victim's] privacy." The pattern: a real victim's death becomes a puzzle to be solved by the audience for entertainment; family members are implicated without consent. The forbid-rule: in cases involving real victims, *the victim is the moral center, not the puzzle*.
2. **Mystery box structure that promises more than it delivers.** The hook-then-stall-then-anticlimax pattern (some Netflix true crime, some YouTube conspiracy content). Forbid the structural move where every section ends on a "but wait" that does not actually deliver. The "but wait" must be earned by an actual turn in the evidence.
3. **Sensationalist narration that overrides the evidence.** When the narrator's emotional escalation is doing work that the evidence cannot do. The Frontline register — calm voice on high-stakes material — is the corrective.
4. **The History Channel "ancient aliens" register.** Speculation in the syntax of fact. Sentences like "Could it be that…?" and "Some experts believe…" used to sneak unsupported claims past the listener. Forbid in totality. Documentary register requires that speculation be marked *as* speculation and that named experts be actually named.
5. **Wikipedia-summary documentaries.** Pieces that recite well-known facts in chronological order without any investigative move, any new evidence, any contradiction surfaced. The diagnostic: nothing in the documentary would be unfamiliar to someone who had read the relevant Wikipedia article. The system should require that every documentary either (a) surface evidence not in the public summary, (b) connect public facts in a way the public summary does not, or (c) reframe the accepted account of a topic — and ideally all three.
6. **Over-padded documentary running the same fact across too many beats.** Often a problem with streaming-platform true crime expanded to fit episode counts. Forbid: a single fact, once established, may not be re-established in subsequent segments without new evidence attached.
7. **Trial-by-narration.** Presenting selected evidence in support of a thesis without making the selection visible. *Making a Murderer*'s lawsuits and the academic critique of *Serial*'s favorable framing of Adnan Syed are the canonical cases. The corrective is the §4 competing-accounts requirement.
8. **The "smartest person in the room" essay slide.** When the documentary narrator drops the apparatus of attribution and starts arguing personally — the moment the piece slides from documentary to essay register. The forbid-rule: if the narrator is making a substantive claim about *what happened* or *what is true*, attribution is required; if the narrator is making a claim about *what this means*, that must be marked as interpretation.
9. **The faked search.** When the documentary stages a "process of investigation" that is theatrical, as if it is happening in real time, when in fact everything has been resolved before filming. Defunctland's *Disney Channel's Theme* is borderline on this and works because Perjurer is honest about the process; bad versions are dishonest about it.

## 9. THE DOCUMENTARY-VIDEO-ESSAY DISTINCTION

The operator's intuition is correct and is the cleanest organizing axis. Stated formally:

| | Video Essay | Documentary |
|---|---|---|
| Driving question | "What do I think about X?" | "What actually happened with X, and what does that mean?" |
| Default tense | Present | Past, with present-marked stakes |
| Default person | First-person singular | Third-person / first-person plural |
| Authority comes from | Reasoning quality | Sourcing, attribution, evidence |
| Subjects | Often abstract / textual | Real people, places, events, records |
| Narrator's mode | Thinking out loud | Walking listener through evidence |
| Structural spine | Argument | Anatomy / case |
| Close form | The argument lands | The case is stated; consequence is named |
| Failure mode | Pretentious / unsupported | Padded / sensational / Wikipedia-summary |

**Signals that push a topic toward documentary form:**

- There is a record, a body of evidence, or a documented chain of events to investigate.
- There is an accepted public account that the piece intends to test, complicate, or correct.
- There are real people, institutions, or places whose actions are central.
- The piece's value is in surfacing what was not previously visible (new evidence, new connections, suppressed material).
- The piece will make accusations, attribute responsibility, or name actors.

**Signals that push a topic toward video essay form:**

- The subject is conceptual, textual, aesthetic, or thematic.
- The piece's value is in reframing or interpreting publicly available material that everyone already has.
- The narrator is openly working out a position rather than reporting a finding.
- The piece does not turn on disputed facts.

The same topic can be done either way. *The economics of streaming* can be a video essay (an interpretive frame on widely-known facts) or a documentary (an investigation of a specific company's specific practices, sourced and attributed). The choice depends on what kind of work the piece will do.

## 10. CONCRETE TAKEAWAYS — OPERATIONAL SPEC

### Patterns to encode (preferred)

1. **Past-tense default with present-tense stake-marking.** "In 2017, the company filed for bankruptcy. What that bankruptcy revealed is what we're going to look at."
2. **Third-person / first-plural default.** "We obtained…" "The records show…" "Investigators found…"
3. **Attribution before claim.** "According to the inspector general's report, …" not "…, according to the inspector general's report."
4. **Single-question spine.** Every documentary opens by stating a specific, answerable investigative question (or a precise mystery to anatomize). Not "what does X mean for our culture" — that is essay register.
5. **The affidavit segment.** Each body section opens on a sub-question, presents primary evidence, draws an implication, ends on a turn. (Confirmation that strengthens the case, or a complication that bends the next segment.)
6. **Named subjects with one-sentence epithets.** Every named person gets a one-sentence situating clause the first time they appear.
7. **Specific evidence, named.** "A memo dated…" "Court filings from the bankruptcy proceeding show…" "Security camera footage, obtained through a public records request, captures…"
8. **The three connective patterns.** Scene → reflection. Evidence → implication. Then → now.
9. **Steelman the alternative.** Before the close, name the strongest competing reading, name who holds it, name what evidence does or does not support it.
10. **What is not known.** The close names the unresolved. Forensic discipline.
11. **The consequence close.** End on what the investigation reveals about the larger system, the people involved, what should change, or what remains unanswered. Not on rhetorical flourish.
12. **Sentence rhythm: short, declarative, mostly under twenty words.** Alternate short and long. Active voice. Contractions where natural. Read it aloud.
13. **Cite tightly in voice; cite densely on screen.** Spoken attribution is short ("a 2019 Ford Foundation study"); on-screen text carries precise citation.
14. **Withholding only by structure.** The narrator may set up that something is coming; the narrator may not falsely promise something the evidence will not deliver.
15. **Disclose method.** When the investigation involved obtaining specific records, contacting specific people, or refusing to publish on the basis of unverified material, say so briefly.

### Patterns to forbid (actively)

1. **First-person singular present-tense argumentation as default voice.** That is the video essay register.
2. **"Could it be that…" / "Some experts believe…" / unattributed speculation.**
3. **Lists of more than three consecutive enumerated facts in spoken voice.**
4. **Mystery-box stalling that does not pay off.**
5. **Trial-by-narration.** Selected evidence presented in support of a thesis without acknowledging the selection.
6. **Wikipedia-summary structure.** Recitation of well-known facts in chronological order without an investigative move.
7. **Strict chronology at the piece level.** Anatomy structure, not chronology.
8. **Sensationalist tone-escalation.** The voice does not match the gravity of the content; the voice stays calm and lets the content carry the gravity.
9. **Fabricated process.** Theatrical "live investigation" framing when the work is actually finished.
10. **Accusation without right-of-reply disclosure.** If a person is being critically characterized, the script must state whether they were asked to respond.
11. **Conflating fact, allegation, and inference in single sentences.**
12. **Reusing the same fact across multiple segments without new evidence attached.**
13. **Rhetorical close.** The documentary close is consequential, not flourishing.
14. **"Voice of God" omniscience.** No unattributed authoritative pronouncements about contested matters; the narrator is named, situated, and accountable.

### Sentence rhythm and connective tissue

Documentary register's characteristic rhythm:

- Opens sections with a *declarative orienting sentence*. ("There were two phone calls that night.")
- Builds through *short factual sentences*, often with concrete dates, names, places.
- Punctuates with *one longer interpretive sentence* per beat that tells the listener what the facts add up to.
- Closes sections with a *turn sentence* — a one-line statement of where the leverage now is. ("And that was the lie that, six months later, would unravel everything.")
- Uses *attribution clauses front-loaded*. "What the inspector general would later find was…"
- Uses *temporal anchors* — specific dates, durations, ages, times of day — as connective tissue. "Eleven days later." "By the spring of 2019." "Three hours before the call."

### Default structural skeleton (recap)

1. Cold open — concrete artifact/scene that contains the question (5–10%)
2. Stakes & frame — why this matters; what the accepted account is (5%)
3. Character & ground — who/where/what (10–15%)
4. Evidence/anatomy — 3–7 affidavit segments, including a midpoint reframing (50–60%)
5. Competing accounts — steelman the alternative (5–10%)
6. What is still unknown (5%)
7. Synthesis/consequence (10%)

### Handling evidence in spoken voice

- Name *what kind of thing* the evidence is before the contents.
- Use a representative case to stand in for a data set.
- Group; cascade; never enumerate beyond three.
- Let on-screen text carry citation density.
- State provenance: how the evidence was obtained.
- When evidence is partial or contested, say so as you introduce it.

### Handling the investigative through-line in spoken voice

- State the question explicitly at the open.
- Restate the question at the midpoint, after the reframing turn.
- Reframe the question at the close in light of what the investigation found.
- Allow brief navigational sentences ("So that's where the public record stops. Here's what we found that wasn't in it.") — these are not video-essay self-narration; they are pieces of the apparatus and they are permitted.

### Closing a documentary (different from closing a video essay)

A video essay closes by landing the argument. A documentary closes by *naming what now changes, in light of what was found*. Three valid close-forms, in order of preference for the operator's forensic use case:

- **The reframing close.** "We started looking for X. What we found was that the story isn't really about X at all — it's about Y." (Defunctland *Disney Channel's Theme*; *Serial*; *Line Goes Up*.)
- **The consequence close.** "And so here is what this investigation forces us to reckon with." (Frontline standard.)
- **The unresolved close.** "Here is what we now know. Here is what we still cannot answer. Here is what would resolve it. Here is what is at stake while it remains unresolved." (*Serial* Season 1; many Bellingcat reports.)

Avoid the rhetorical-flourish close, the sentimental close, and the call-to-action close — those are essay/op-ed register, not documentary.

### The functional test for "this prose is documentary register, not essay register"

Three diagnostics:

1. **The attribution test.** Strip the script of attribution clauses ("according to," "we obtained," "records show," named source clauses). If the script still reads coherently as a piece of writing, it is essay register. Documentary register *depends* on its attribution apparatus and falls apart without it.
2. **The tense test.** Highlight every claim about events under investigation. If they are predominantly in past tense, with present-tense reserved for stakes-framing and the narrator's interpretive moves, it is documentary register. If they are predominantly in present-tense first-person ("so what's happening here is…"), it is essay register.
3. **The "what was actually going on" test.** Can you state, in one sentence, what previously-unseen thing this piece surfaces? If yes — a specific finding, a documented contradiction, a chain of events the public account misses — it is documentary. If the answer is "an interesting way of thinking about X," it is essay.

A piece that fails all three tests is a video essay. A piece that passes all three is forensic documentary in the register the operator wants. A piece that passes one or two is a hybrid and should be flagged for the operator to choose which type to commit to before the script is finalized.

This specification is intended to be implementation-ready. The voice profile is: third-person, past-tense default with present-tense stake-marking, attribution-front-loaded, calm under stakes, evidence-foregrounded, contradiction-tolerant, uncertainty-naming, consequence-closing. The structural skeleton is the seven-section affidavit-cascade above. The forbid-list is enforceable as a set of rules against specific sentence shapes and rhetorical moves. The functional test (attribution / tense / "what was actually going on") gives the system a way to validate output against the type definition and to reject scripts that have drifted into video essay register.
