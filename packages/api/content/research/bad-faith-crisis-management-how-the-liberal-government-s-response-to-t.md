# Bad-Faith Crisis Management: How the Liberal Government's Response to Trump's "51st State" Remarks and the 2025 Tariff War Signalled Unreliability

## TL;DR
- The Liberal government (Trudeau, then Carney, with Joly at Foreign Affairs) responded to a U.S. negotiating signal — Trump's "51st state" taunts and tariff threats — with performative nationalism ("elbows up," "Canada is not for sale") and domestically-aimed outrage rather than sustained, direct engagement, then repeatedly reversed course under pressure (DST rescinded hours before collection; counter-tariffs walked back; an apology for the Reagan ad), producing a pattern critics across the spectrum called "capitulation."
- The contradiction is documented: Ottawa publicly dismissed Trump's fentanyl rationale as a "totally false" pretext while privately taking it seriously enough to spend C$1.3 billion, appoint a "Fentanyl Czar," and list cartels as terror entities — and Trudeau admitted on a hot mic that annexation was "a real thing," contradicting his public "snowball's chance in hell" line.
- The strategy did not work on its own terms: through mid-2026 CUSMA talks remained stalled, U.S. tariffs on steel, aluminum, autos and lumber stayed in place despite every Canadian concession, and U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer (Fox Business, June 9, 2026, per Global News) again named Canada's retaliatory tariffs as a "problem" — the latest irritant he cited for why talks with Canada lagged those with Mexico — evidence the escalatory posture cost credibility without securing relief.

## Key Findings

**1. The U.S. signal was consistent and explicitly transactional.** From December 2024 Trump tied the "51st state" idea directly to the tariff/trade-deficit grievance ("They would save massively on taxes and military protection… 51st State!"). The Feb. 1, 2025 executive order (EO 14193) framed the tariffs around a concrete, stated demand: fentanyl and border enforcement. Whether serious or theatre, the U.S. ask was legible and repeated.

**2. The Liberal response was primarily rhetorical and domestically oriented.** Trudeau's "snowball's chance in hell," Carney's campaign "elbows up"/"Never 51" video with Mike Myers, and Joly's "we will never be a colony" all functioned as nationalist rallying cries that coincided with — and arguably drove — the Liberals' electoral recovery, rather than as engagement with the stated U.S. grievances.

**3. The fentanyl contradiction is the clearest evasion.** Ottawa simultaneously argued the fentanyl rationale was "totally false"/"unjustified" (because of Canada's tiny share — of 21,889 lbs of fentanyl seized by U.S. border authorities in FY2024, only about 43 lbs, roughly 0.2%, came from the northern border versus 21,148 lbs, about 96.6%, at the Mexican border, per CNN's Feb. 3, 2025 fact check of federal CBP data; Canada.ca's own border page states "Less than 1% of fentanyl seized in the US comes from Canada") AND undertook major substantive measures premised on the concern being real (C$1.3B border plan, Fentanyl Czar, cartel terror-listing). It could not both be a "bogus excuse" and worth a billion-dollar response.

**4. Trudeau's private position contradicted his public one.** On a hot mic (Feb. 7, 2025) Trudeau said annexation "is a real thing" driven by critical minerals — directly at odds with his public framing of the 51st-state talk as a mere "distraction" and impossibility.

**5. The DST reversal is the signature climbdown.** Champagne insisted on June 19, 2025 the tax "is in force and it's going to be applied"; eleven days later, hours before first collection and after Trump suspended talks, Ottawa rescinded it. The White House called it a "cave."

**6. The U.S. side was more rhetorically coherent and direct.** Trump's demands were blunt and repeated; the U.S. consistently linked tariff relief to specific asks and treated Canadian concessions as confirmation its pressure worked, while declining to reciprocate.

**7. The crisis response damaged negotiating credibility.** Trade talks stalled; USTR Greer publicly pointed to Canada's retaliatory tariffs as an obstacle; Canada made multiple concessions and "received nothing in return."

## Details

### Timeline of the U.S. signal (Dec 2024 – Mar 2025)
- **November 2024 (Mar-a-Lago dinner):** Trump first floated merging the countries and called Trudeau "Governor."
- **Dec. 18, 2024:** On Truth Social — "Many Canadians want Canada to become the 51st State. They would save massively on taxes and military protection. I think it is a great idea. 51st State!"
- **Jan. 2025:** At Davos and in North Carolina, Trump repeated that Canada could avoid tariffs by becoming a state.
- **Feb. 1, 2025:** Executive Order 14193 declared a national emergency over "the failure of Canada to do more" on fentanyl, imposing 25% tariffs (10% on energy) under IEEPA. Paused Feb. 3 for 30 days.
- **Pre-Super Bowl (Feb. 2025):** Trump told Bret Baier the annexation threat was "a real thing."
- **Mar. 4, 2025:** Tariffs imposed. Trudeau called the legal pretext "totally false."
- **Mar. 12–13, 2025:** 25% steel/aluminum tariffs took effect; Trump repeated the 51st-state line as Hoekstra testified.

The stated U.S. rationale was framed as fentanyl/border security in the EO, but Trump publicly emphasized the trade deficit — citing an inflated "$200 billion a year" subsidy figure. The actual 2024 U.S. goods trade deficit with Canada was roughly US$45 billion (about -0.2% of U.S. GDP), per TD Economics ("Setting the Record Straight on Canada-U.S. Trade"); USTR data later showed US$46.4 billion for 2025, and the USTR's office had placed the 2024 figure at $63.3 billion. Trump openly linked statehood to tariff avoidance — i.e., presented as both threat and "offer."

### The Liberal messaging and tone
Trudeau initially framed the 51st-state talk as a deliberate *distraction*: "I know that as a successful negotiator, he likes to keep people a little off balance. The 51st state, that's not going to happen… It's just a non-starter" (MSNBC, Jan. 2025); and to CNN's Jake Tapper, Trump was "getting people to be somewhat distracted… to take away from the conversation around 25% tariffs." His most-quoted line: "There isn't a snowball's chance in hell that Canada would become a part of the United States."

Joly escalated the rhetorical register. To BBC Newsnight (~Mar. 4, 2025) she said she took the line "very seriously": "This is not a joke anymore. There's a reason why Canadians, when they go out on a hockey game, are booing the American national anthem… We're insulted. We're mad. We're angry." On CNN (Mar. 6, 2025) she framed the tariffs as an annexation strategy: "President Trump wants to put us into a state where we are much more weakened economically in order eventually to annex us." At the G7 foreign ministers' meeting in Charlevoix (Mar. 13–14, 2025) she told Rubio "Canada's sovereignty is not up to debate, period," recounting: "I said to them, 'This is not a joke.' Canadians are anxious… you are here in a sovereign country, and so therefore we don't expect this to be even discussed, or clearly not laughed at." To PBS: "we will never be a colony, period."

Rubio's response (State Dept. transcript, Mar. 14, 2025) was notably more matter-of-fact: the 51st-state issue "was not a topic, it was not on our agenda… The tariffs were." He recounted the origin — that at Mar-a-Lago Trudeau said Canada couldn't survive tariffs as a nation-state, "at which point the President said, well, then you should become a state."

### The documented contradiction on fentanyl
Trudeau's Mar. 3–4 statement called the tariffs "unjustified" and noted fentanyl seizures from Canada had "dropped 97 per cent… to a near-zero low of 0.03 pounds." Yet the same statement enumerated a major substantive response premised on the concern: a C$1.3-billion border plan ("new choppers, boots on the ground"), a Fentanyl Czar, cartel terror-listings, a Joint Operational Intelligence Cell, and a Canada-U.S. Joint Strike Force. The Fentanyl Czar — Kevin Brosseau, appointed Feb. 11, 2025 — set a goal of getting cross-border fentanyl "to zero," later telling CBC News, "Canada is on it. We're doing our part to be a good neighbour," and noting he speaks "every working day" to the U.S. Office of National Drug Control Policy. Canada thus treated as a genuine, billion-dollar problem the very rationale it publicly branded a "bogus excuse" (Joly, BBC). U.S.-side seizure data (19.5 kg at the northern border in 2024 vs. 9,570 kg at the southern) supported Canada's "tiny share" claim — but did not resolve the messaging contradiction of dismissing the pretext while funding a response to it. (Canada.ca later reported about 430 kg, or 948 lb, of fentanyl intercepted through combined Canadian border operations in 2025, and that illegal crossings from Canada to the U.S. were down 99% in January 2026 from June 2024 peaks.)

### Trudeau's hot-mic admission
On Feb. 7, 2025, at a closed-door Canada-U.S. Economic Summit accidentally carried on a loudspeaker, Trudeau said Trump's annexation talk "is a real thing," driven by critical minerals: "Mr. Trump has it in mind that the easiest way to do it is absorbing our country and it is a real thing." Alberta Federation of Labour president Gil McGowan confirmed Trudeau assessed "what he really wants is to either dominate Canada or take it outright." This privately-held assessment contradicted the public "distraction"/"non-starter" framing.

### The "Buy Canadian" campaign and counter-tariffs
On Feb. 1, 2025 Trudeau announced 25% retaliatory tariffs on C$155B of U.S. goods (C$30B immediately, C$125B in 21 days) and urged Canadians to "buy Canadian products and vacation at home." Framing was both defensive-retaliatory ("We didn't ask for this but we will not back down") and addressed to Americans ("Tariffs against Canada will put your jobs at risk"). Carney, on becoming leader (Mar. 9) and PM (Mar. 14), kept the tariffs "until the Americans show us respect" and declared the "old relationship… is over" (Mar. 27, 2025), vowing to "fundamentally reimagine" the economy. The rhetoric escalated tensions and was explicitly nationalist — a posture that helped win the Apr. 28 election but committed Ottawa to "dollar-for-dollar" lines it later abandoned.

### The reversals
- **DST (June 2025):** Champagne, June 19: the DST "is in force and it's going to be applied." Trump suspended all trade talks June 27 over the tax. June 29–30: Canada rescinded it "in anticipation of a mutually beneficial comprehensive trade arrangement," hours before the first (retroactive, ~US$2B) payment was due. White House: "Canada caved." Carney defended it as supporting "a resumption of negotiations."
- **Counter-tariffs (Aug. 2025):** After Trump raised tariffs to 35% (Aug. 1), Carney did not retaliate; on Aug. 22 he removed counter-tariffs on CUSMA-compliant goods, keeping only steel/aluminum/auto tariffs. Carney: "Canada currently has the best trade deal with the United States." Critics (Unifor's Lana Payne: "Walking back counter-tariffs isn't an olive branch. It only enables more U.S. aggression"; Poilievre: "his elbows have mysteriously gone missing") called it capitulation.
- **Reagan ad (Oct.–Nov. 2025):** After Ontario's anti-tariff ad using Reagan's words aired during the World Series, Trump terminated talks (Oct. 23) and added 10% tariffs (Oct. 25). Carney apologized to Trump (reported Nov. 1). Tariffs stayed.

### The U.S. side's behaviour vs. Canada's
The U.S. position was blunt and consistent: relief was conditioned on specific asks, and concessions were treated as wins, not reciprocated. Press secretary Leavitt: "Prime Minister Carney and Canada caved." USTR Greer repeatedly named Canada's retaliatory tariffs (and provincial alcohol bans) as obstacles — on Fox Business (June 9, 2026, per Global News) he called them an ongoing "problem," the latest in a series of irritants he cited for why talks with Canada were slower than with Mexico, with counter-tariffs still in place on over 300 U.S. steel, aluminum and auto products. He said Canada was in a "different spot" where it was "hard to see where that ends." Trump openly mused about letting CUSMA "expire." The asymmetry: every documented major reversal in the bilateral was Canadian.

### CUSMA stalling
By the 2026 review, talks had stalled. Canada conceded the DST and most counter-tariffs and, by Ottawa's own account, "received nothing in return"; U.S. sectoral (Section 232) tariffs on steel, aluminum, autos and lumber remained. Greer told Congress the U.S. wouldn't "rubber stamp" renewal and pointed to "problems." Carney (Apr. 2026) said talks would "take some time" and that the U.S. "doesn't dictate the terms," while LeBlanc pushed a "comprehensive" approach the U.S. rejected (Washington insists sectoral tariffs are "separate"). At the G20 (late 2025) Carney dismissed the question of when he last spoke to Trump about tariffs — "Who cares? I mean, it's a detail" — a remark critics read as detachment given that 75.9% of Canada's total exports went to the U.S. in 2024 (Statistics Canada, *The Daily*, Feb. 5, 2025), with 85.7% of Canadian exporting enterprises selling to the U.S.

### Performative vs. substantive
Substantive measures existed (the border plan, Czar, Strong Borders Act/Bill C-2) but were framed defensively and undercut by the simultaneous "false pretext" messaging. The 51st-state comment was never treated as a proposal to engage; it was immediately and uniformly dismissed/decried. Conversely, the genuinely substantive lever — sustained quiet negotiation — was repeatedly subordinated to public posturing (the election campaign, the "old relationship is over" speech), then abandoned in a series of climbdowns.

### Carney specifically
Carney campaigned harder-line than Trudeau ("elbows up," tariffs until "respect"/"credible, reliable commitments"), then governed markedly softer: the May 6 Oval Office meeting was "friendly"; he called Trump a "transformative president"; he rescinded the DST, declined to retaliate at 35% and 50% steel/aluminum, removed counter-tariffs, and apologized over the Reagan ad. Analysts (The Conversation: "Elbows down? Why Mark Carney seems to keep caving"; The Tyee: "it seems Canada has no elbows at all") documented the rhetoric-reality gap. His reversals were arguably more pragmatic than Trudeau's posture — but the campaign-to-governance whiplash is itself the reliability problem: the public commitments ("dollar-for-dollar," hold the line "until respect") were not kept.

## Recommendations
For an evidence-first treatment of the reliability question, structure the case around the **gap between stated posture and actual behaviour**, using these load-bearing, documentable contrasts:
1. **Lead with the fentanyl contradiction** (publicly "totally false"/"bogus" vs. a C$1.3B response and a Czar tasked with getting it "to zero") — the single cleanest example of saying one thing and doing another.
2. **Use the Champagne DST quotes as a paired before/after** (June 19 "going ahead" vs. June 29–30 rescission) — an 11-day, on-the-record reversal under explicit pressure.
3. **Document the hot-mic vs. podium contradiction** (Feb. 7 "it is a real thing" vs. "snowball's chance"/"distraction").
4. **Track the climbdown sequence** (DST → 35% non-retaliation → Aug. 22 counter-tariff removal → Reagan-ad apology) as a pattern, not isolated events, and pair each with the matching "hold the line" commitment it broke.
5. **Quote the critics from across the spectrum** (Unifor/Payne from the left; Poilievre from the right; The Conversation/Tyee/Independent Institute analysts) to show the "capitulation" read is not partisan.
6. **Benchmark against outcomes**: tariffs remained or rose after every concession; CUSMA stalled; Greer named Canadian retaliation as the problem. The thesis strengthens if framed as *posture that neither de-escalated nor delivered*.

What would change this assessment: evidence that the "elbows up" posture extracted a concrete, durable U.S. concession (it did not, through mid-2026); or evidence that the substantive border measures were presented as good-faith engagement rather than alongside "false pretext" messaging.

## Caveats
- **Sourcing and framing:** Several characterizations ("caved," "capitulation," "no elbows at all") are from partisan or opinion sources (White House, Poilievre, opinion columns) and are presented as *attributed characterizations*, not established fact. The factual spine (dates, EO text, official statements, reversal timing) rests on primary/official sources (pm.gc.ca, canada.ca, White House, State Dept., CRS) and wire/major outlets.
- **The U.S. case had its own weaknesses:** Canadian fentanyl seizures were genuinely a tiny share (~0.2% of U.S. seizures in FY2024); the U.S. trade-deficit figure Trump cited ($200B) was inflated roughly four-to-fivefold versus the ~US$45–46B actual goods deficit; and U.S. courts (the Court of International Trade, and later the Supreme Court, per reporting) found the IEEPA tariff use unlawful. A reliability critique of Ottawa is not an endorsement of the U.S. rationale.
- **Counterargument:** Defenders argue Carney's pragmatism reflected Canada's structural weakness (75.9% of exports to the U.S.) and that no posture would have moved an unpredictable counterpart — i.e., the reversals were realism, not bad faith. This reading should be presented, not assumed away.
- **One wording caveat:** Joly's widely-circulated "This is not something we laugh at" (Newsweek) appears to conflate her BBC and G7 remarks; the BBC verbatim Newsnight line continues with the hockey-anthem sentence. The BBC wording is treated here as authoritative.
- **Date conventions:** Some downstream events (CUSMA review specifics, Greer's June 2026 comments) fall in 2026 and are evolving; figures cited are as reported through mid-2026.
