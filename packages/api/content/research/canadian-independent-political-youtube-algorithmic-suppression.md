## Canadian Independent Political YouTube and the "Algorithmic Suppression" Complaint: A Research Brief

**Scope.** This brief addresses claims circulating among Canadian independent political YouTubers — among them Jasmin Laine (@JasminLaine_), Wyatt Claypool (@wyatt_claypool, "The National Telegraph"), and Derek Smith (@unacceptfringe) — that YouTube is suppressing their reach, often citing unusually high like-to-view ratios (e.g., 13K likes on 72K views; 5K likes on 22K views) as evidence. A specific claim attributed to Laine ties the alleged suppression to a "2026 algorithm tranche of CRTC regulations" capping reach at roughly 70–95K views. The brief separates documented facts from inferential or analytical claims and flags confidence levels where evidence is thin.

## 1. CRTC / Bill C-11 Regulatory Timeline and the "2026 Algorithm Tranche" Claim

**Documented facts (high confidence):**

- Bill C-11 (the Online Streaming Act) received Royal Assent on April 27, 2023. It amends the Broadcasting Act to bring online undertakings — including streaming services and, in limited cases, social media services — under CRTC jurisdiction.
- Section 9.1(8) of the Act expressly prohibits the CRTC from making an order requiring the "use of a specific computer algorithm or source code."
- Section 4.2(2) permits the CRTC to make regulations covering certain commercial programs uploaded to social media services, but the Governor-in-Council's binding Policy Direction issued November 9, 2023 (Order Issuing Directions to the CRTC — Sustainable and Equitable Broadcasting Regulatory Framework) instructed the CRTC to exclude social media users and user-generated content from regulation, and to prioritize outcome-based regulations that "minimize the need for broadcasting undertakings to make changes to their computer algorithms."
- The CRTC's regulatory plan, updated May 6, 2024, targets late 2025 for implementation of the new framework, with further consultations continuing into 2026. Consultations on diversity, equity and inclusion are scheduled December 2025–March 2026.
- To date, the CRTC's principal Bill C-11 decisions implement: (i) a registration regime for online undertakings with more than CA$10 million in Canadian revenue; (ii) a 5% revenue contribution (Broadcasting Regulatory Policy 2024-121) on foreign streamers with >CA$25 million in Canadian revenue that are not affiliated with a Canadian broadcaster, with payments earmarked for the Canada Media Fund, the Independent Local News Fund, FACTOR/Musicaction, Black Screen Office, BPOC and accessibility funds; (iii) Broadcasting Regulatory Policy CRTC 2025-299 (November 18, 2025) modernizing the definition of "Canadian program" for the audio-visual sector, including a 20% Canadian copyright-ownership requirement and a revised points system, plus AI-use restrictions; and (iv) Broadcasting Regulatory Policy CRTC 2025-344, setting described-video / audio-description obligations for online streaming services (with comments on draft orders due February 3, 2026).
- The base-contribution decision is currently being challenged in Federal Court by the Motion Picture Association-Canada (Netflix, Paramount, Disney, Warner Bros. Discovery, etc.) and separately by Apple, Amazon and Spotify (the music-streamers' challenge focuses on the local-news component).
- No CRTC order or regulation in force as of May 2026 mandates any algorithmic outcome on YouTube, requires YouTube to prioritize Canadian creators or legacy broadcasters in its recommendation system, or restricts the reach of independent Canadian YouTubers. The CRTC's discoverability proceedings remain at the consultation/policy-design stage; they are part of the as-yet-unfinished Phase 3 implementation work, not a deployed "algorithm tranche."
- Quebec's Bill 109 (Act respecting the discoverability of French-language cultural content) — a parallel provincial regime — completed clause-by-clause review in late 2025 but is widely expected to face constitutional challenge if enacted; it is not federal CRTC law and does not target English-language Canadian political content.

**The specific Laine claim — verification:**

- The phrase "2026 algorithm tranche of CRTC regulations" does not correspond to any identifiable CRTC decision, regulatory policy, notice of consultation, or order in the public record reviewed for this brief.
- The CRTC's own published timeline does schedule further C-11 consultations through 2026, but no algorithm-targeting regulation has been issued, drafted for comment, or scheduled for enforcement in 2026 that would limit YouTube view counts for independent Canadian commentators.
- The Bill C-11 statutory framework, as constrained by Section 9.1(8) and the November 2023 Policy Direction, currently prohibits the type of regulatory instrument that would explain a uniform 70–95K view cap.

**Inferential assessment (medium-to-high confidence):** As an objective matter, no CRTC instrument presently exists that could plausibly produce a per-video view ceiling on a Canadian political commentary channel. Critics of C-11 (notably Michael Geist of the University of Ottawa, OpenMedia, and YouTube Canada itself in its 2022–23 advocacy) have long argued that outcome-based discoverability rules — if and when issued — could *indirectly* pressure platforms to manipulate recommendations and could *theoretically* depress the reach of Canadian creators globally (because algorithmic up-ranking inside Canada penalizes content that under-performs with newly exposed audiences, dragging down its broader recommendation profile). That risk, however, remains prospective and contingent on future CRTC rule-making; it has not been operationalized. **Confidence that no current CRTC instrument is causing the alleged reach cap: high.** **Confidence that future discoverability rules could plausibly affect Canadian creators: medium (this is the consensus of critics across the political spectrum, but it is a forecast, not an observed effect).**

## 2. Platform Geographic Siloing and Cross-Border Discovery

**Documented facts:**

- YouTube has long permitted (and in some categories required) country-level geo-blocking via Content ID and upload policies, primarily driven by licensing/copyright considerations. This system pre-dates Bill C-11 and is creator- or rights-holder-controlled rather than platform-imposed for political commentary.
- YouTube's published election policy (most recently codified in its 2024 elections-coverage statements) explicitly states that its recommendation system "prominently surfaces high-quality content" — i.e., it raises "authoritative" sources — in search, the homepage, and the "watch next" panel for election-related queries. This treatment is applied globally and on a per-country basis to the user's locale.
- YouTube's "raise authoritative voices" policy was introduced in 2017 and expanded in 2019 and 2020. It explicitly prioritizes legacy/mainstream outlets (the company has cited CNN, Fox News, the Guardian as examples) for news and breaking-news queries, with the stated rationale of reducing misinformation.
- Meta's response to Canada's Online News Act (Bill C-18) is a documented case of platform-level Canadian content siloing: since August 2023, Facebook and Instagram have blocked news links for Canadian users. Google reached a separate $100 million/year agreement and was exempted from individual bargaining via a CRTC decision. There is no equivalent documented siloing by YouTube in response to Canadian legislation.
- YouTube's response to Bill C-11, articulated by Jeanette Patell (head of government affairs and public policy, YouTube Canada) in 2022–23, was to warn that CRTC discoverability requirements *could* hurt Canadian creators' global reach because forced exposure to uninterested Canadian audiences would lower click-through and engagement signals, which would in turn reduce global recommendation weight. This is a documented platform-stated concern, not documented platform behaviour.
- In early 2025, multiple news outlets (Stockholm Center for Freedom; Nordic Monitor) reported that Google/YouTube algorithm changes coincided with significant traffic drops at independent Turkish news outlets after a January 2025 update. This is documented in third-party reporting but evidence is largely outlet-attributed; Google has not confirmed the changes were country-targeted.

**Inferential assessment (medium confidence):** There is no public evidence that YouTube has implemented a Canada-specific geo-siloing measure that would systematically suppress cross-border discovery of Canadian political commentary. The "authoritative sources" prioritization is a global policy that predates the current cycle and has been documented to disadvantage independent commentary in many jurisdictions, not only Canada. **Confidence that no Canada-specific geo-restriction has been publicly disclosed: high. Confidence that the global "authoritative sources" weighting structurally disadvantages independent political commentary: medium-high — this is the platform's own publicly stated design intent.**

## 3. Canadian YouTube Market Size and the Realistic Ceiling for Political Commentary

**Documented facts:**

- According to DataReportal's *Digital 2025: Canada* report (based on Google's own advertising-reach data), YouTube's ad-reach audience in Canada was 31.7 million users in early 2025 — roughly 79.4% of Canada's total population and about 83.4% of the total Canadian internet user base. (Ad-reach is not identical to monthly active users but is the most commonly cited proxy.)
- Comscore data cited by Google Canada places YouTube's reach at "98% of Canadians across all audiences" on a less restrictive definition.
- Oxford Economics' 2022 YouTube Canada Impact Report attributed CA$2.0 billion in GDP contribution and ~35,000 FTE jobs to YouTube's Canadian creative ecosystem (a figure citable but not independent of YouTube).
- 71% of all political content viewership on live-streaming platforms in 2024 (per Stream Hatchet) was tied to U.S. political events; only 29% concerned non-U.S. politics. Rumble's political viewership was 94% U.S.-focused.
- Canada's federal voting population in the April 28, 2025 election was approximately 28 million eligible electors; turnout was 69.5% (~19.5 million voters).
- 2025 polling and Pew-style data on the share of Canadians who regularly consume political YouTube specifically is not publicly published at the same granularity as the U.S. Pew News Influencers studies (which found that English-language U.S. political-news influencers on YouTube command audiences in the tens of millions cumulatively).

**Inferential assessment (medium confidence):** A reasonable estimate of the *active English-language Canadian political-YouTube audience* — i.e., people who regularly seek out political commentary in English on YouTube as opposed to news-clip viewers — is in the low single-digit millions at most. Canada's English-speaking population is ~26 million; of that, perhaps a quarter to a third use YouTube for any kind of news/political content with some regularity (extrapolating from U.S. Pew data showing about a third of U.S. adults regularly get news from YouTube). The "regular political-commentary" subset is substantially smaller — likely well under 2 million for any given month, and a much smaller "frequent viewer" core. A single Canadian political commentary video routinely reaching 70,000–95,000 Canadian views is therefore consistent with reaching a meaningful slice (3–10%) of the active addressable audience, which is typical *strong* performance for that niche, not a sign of suppression. **Confidence: medium** — the upper bound is reasonably well-anchored in YouTube ad-reach data; the active-political-viewer subset is an inference from comparable U.S. consumption data because no equivalent Canadian Pew-style study has been published.

**Comparator:** Top Canadian political YouTube channels (e.g., True North, Rebel News, Juno News) typically post videos that pull in the tens of thousands to low hundreds of thousands of views; only viral or rage-bait crossover content regularly clears 500K. By contrast, top U.S. independent political YouTubers (Brian Tyler Cohen, MeidasTouch, Megyn Kelly, Tim Pool, Ben Shapiro) routinely generate millions of views per video, drawing on an addressable English-speaking U.S. audience roughly 10x the Canadian one and with substantial international spillover.

## 4. Canadian vs. U.S. Independent Political YouTube Comparison

**Documented facts:**

- A Chaotic Era / Pixability analysis of 200 partisan U.S. political YouTube channels (each >100,000 subscribers) found that the top tier of U.S. political channels added 22.9 million combined subscribers in Q1 2025 and 18.1 million in Q2 2025 — a noticeable slowdown attributed to post-Trump-inauguration interest fading. Right-leaning channels outpaced left-leaning channels in Q2 2025 net adds (9.4 million vs. 8.6 million). Pixability separately tracked a 25.6% increase in left-leaning channel views and a 10.08% decline in right-leaning channel views comparing January–February 2025 vs. March–April 2025.
- Streams Charts (April 2025 report) documented a 56% year-over-year increase in political-livestream watch time across YouTube/Twitch/Kick in Q1 2025 vs. Q1 2024 (3.4 billion vs. 2.61 billion hours), with YouTube accounting for ~98% of measured watch time. Their Q1 2025 figure was a slight decrease from Q4 2024 — consistent with a post-U.S.-election contraction.
- Liberal Media Subscriber Decline: Multiple commentary outlets reported (December 2024 onward) that left-leaning U.S. political YouTube and TikTok creators saw subscriber and view declines after the November 2024 election. This is sourced primarily to opinion outlets rather than peer-reviewed measurement.
- Documented complaints of "algorithmic suppression" from U.S. independent political creators across the political spectrum have been recurrent since at least 2019, particularly intensifying after YouTube's January 2019 "borderline content" recommendation changes and the 2020 election-integrity policy. The complaint is platform-wide, not Canada-specific.

**Inferential assessment (medium-high confidence):**

- U.S. independent political channels enjoy a viewership ceiling roughly an order of magnitude above Canadian ones, attributable to (a) population scale, (b) the global salience of U.S. politics (which draws non-U.S. viewers to U.S. creators), and (c) a deeper sponsorship / monetization ecosystem.
- Canadian channels rarely benefit from substantial cross-border spillover into the U.S. market in the way U.S. creators benefit from Canadian audiences. This is not a CRTC effect — it is a demand-side artifact: U.S. viewers are not generally seeking out commentary on Canadian politics.
- "Algorithmic suppression" is a complaint shared across U.S. and Canadian independent creators post-2024, post-2025. Its ubiquity argues strongly against a CRTC-specific cause for the Canadian version of the complaint. **Confidence: medium-high.**

## 5. Post-Election Attention Cycle and the Canadian 2025 Timeline

**Documented facts:**

- The 2025 Canadian federal election was held April 28, 2025. Voter turnout was 69.5% — the highest since 1993. The Liberals (under Mark Carney) won a minority government with the highest vote share since 1984.
- YouTube's own published election data for the U.S. 2024 cycle reported >45 million U.S. viewers of election-related content on November 5, 2024 alone, with top videos (Joe Rogan's Trump and Vance interviews; Jubilee's Ben Shapiro segment) drawing tens of millions of views in election week. This is consistent with documented spike-and-contract patterns around election cycles globally.
- Stream Hatchet data documented political-livestream viewership tripling on Twitch from ~5 million hours in January 2024 to ~15 million in July/August 2024 during the U.S. campaign — followed by a fall-off.
- Pixability/Chaotic Era documented the Q1-to-Q2 2025 subscriber-growth slowdown for U.S. political channels (above), explicitly attributing it to the fading post-inauguration news cycle.

**Inferential assessment (high confidence):** A pronounced post-election viewership and engagement contraction is the documented norm for political YouTube. For Canadian creators who built their audience during the March–April 2025 election campaign — the most consequential, hard-fought Canadian campaign in over a decade, dominated by U.S. tariff and annexation threats — a six- to twelve-month contraction beginning in May 2025 is the *expected* trajectory in the absence of any regulatory action. By May 2026 (the date of writing), Canadian political YouTubers are 12+ months past peak election attention and should be expected to be at a structural trough relative to their April 2025 peaks. **Confidence: high** that a substantial part of any observed view-count compression for Canadian political channels in late 2025 / early 2026 is attributable to the normal post-election attention cycle, regardless of any algorithmic or regulatory factor.

## 6. Like-to-View Ratio Analysis: What an 18% Ratio Actually Indicates

**Documented facts on benchmarks (multiple sources triangulated):**

- HypeAuditor / SociaVault / UpGrowth / Creator Hero / WizStudio / LiveReacting / Marketing Charts engagement-rate calculators (drawing on aggregate samples ranging from ~100,000 to 350,000 channels) converge on the following YouTube like-to-view ratio benchmarks for long-form content:
  - Platform-wide average: ~1–4% (varies by source and methodology; the most commonly cited figure is 3.75–4%).
  - "Strong" engagement: 5–7%.
  - "Exceptional" / top-decile: above 10%.
  - SociaVault's 2026 analysis of 75,000 channels found nano channels (1K–10K subs) averaged 5.23% engagement per view; macro channels (100K–500K) averaged 2.12%.
  - Niche-specific: gaming ~5.47%, education and tech-review channels in the 3–4% range, music as low as 1.87–2.28%.
- A like-to-view ratio of ~18% (13K/72K = 18.1%; 5K/22K = 22.7%) sits well above the conventionally "exceptional" 10%+ threshold.
- Industry guidance (SoNuker, Creator Hero) notes that consistently sustaining a >15% like-to-view ratio on a channel with more than 10,000 subscribers is rare and, when sustained, typically indicates either (a) a highly loyal, ideologically aligned core audience where most viewers are already subscribers/regulars, or (b) artificial inflation. There is no public evidence of the latter in the Laine/Claypool/Smith cases.

**Inferential assessment (high confidence):** A like-to-view ratio of 15–22% is not, in itself, evidence of algorithmic suppression. It is — under the prevailing analytical framework used by audit tools, marketing analysts and the platform itself — a *direct indicator* of an audience composed predominantly of returning, ideologically aligned subscribers rather than newly recommended/discovered viewers. The two interpretations are analytically distinct but not mutually exclusive:

- *Interpretation A (audience composition):* A high like ratio reflects that nearly every viewer is a fan. The channel is "preaching to the choir." This is the standard marketing-analytics reading and does not require any algorithmic intervention to produce.
- *Interpretation B (suppression):* The high ratio reflects an artificial ceiling: the platform is exposing the content only to existing subscribers (who like it at very high rates) but not surfacing it to new audiences (who would consume but not engage at the same rate). Under this interpretation, the missing low-engagement viewers are the "tell."

These two interpretations cannot be distinguished from the like-to-view ratio alone. Distinguishing them would require examining the creator's YouTube Studio analytics — specifically the "Subscribers vs. Non-Subscribers" reach breakdown, the impressions-to-views click-through rate from "Browse features" and "Suggested videos," and the impressions volume on Home/Suggested over time. None of these analytics are publicly visible from a video page; they are accessible only to the creator. Public observers, including the affected creators in their own public posts, have not (as of this writing) published those internal analytics to substantiate the suppression interpretation.

**Confidence:** High that the cited like-to-view ratios are insufficient evidence to establish suppression on their own; they are equally consistent with a small, loyal, audience-saturated base — which is the expected profile for a Canadian political commentator with a strong subscriber-to-view ratio post-election.

## 7. Legacy / "Authoritative" Source Prioritization on YouTube

**Documented facts:**

- YouTube formally announced the "raise authoritative voices" recommendation policy in 2017 and expanded it through 2018–2020. Per YouTube's own blog posts (notably the January 2019 "Continuing our work to improve recommendations on YouTube" announcement and the 2020 elections-support post), the platform commits to surfacing "authoritative" news sources — explicitly named examples have included CNN, Fox News, and The Guardian — at the top of search results, in "Up Next" panels, and on the homepage for news and breaking-news queries. YouTube stated in 2020 that consumption from authoritative news partners' channels had grown 60% year-over-year as a result.
- YouTube does not publicly disclose its full list of "authoritative" sources or the criteria used to designate them.
- YouTube's January 2019 recommendation change targeted "borderline content and content that could misinform users in harmful ways" — content not violating policies but considered low-quality. YouTube reported that the change reduced views of such content from recommendations by more than 50%.
- A CBC News investigation (October 2019) that analyzed 20,000+ YouTube recommendations on Canadian-politics search terms ahead of the 2019 federal election found only 2.1% of "Up Next" recommendations were in any way related to the topic searched, suggesting the algorithm was actively *steering away* from political content toward viral/popular non-political content. Karen Yeung (University of Birmingham) characterized the lack of relevant political recommendations as a form of de facto censorship.
- A 2024 NBC News-reported academic study of YouTube's recommendation system on built-from-scratch sock-puppet accounts found a tendency to recommend right-leaning and Christian content even to ideologically neutral profiles. A 2022 Brookings study found YouTube's recommendation algorithm pulled users "slightly to the right of the political spectrum" on average. A 2025 PNAS Nexus analysis found asymmetric moderation — the algorithm steered users *away from far-right content* more aggressively than away from far-left content. Research conclusions are genuinely contested.
- YouTube's January 2025 algorithm refinement and the July 15, 2025 "inauthentic content" enforcement wave (targeting reused/templated/AI-generated content) tightened monetization and visibility for channels using mass-produced formats.

**Inferential assessment:**

- **High confidence:** YouTube has, by its own admission, structurally prioritized "authoritative" (i.e., legacy and major-mainstream) news sources in news/political surfacing since 2017. This is a global, platform-wide policy, not a Canada-specific or CRTC-driven policy.
- **Medium confidence:** This prioritization measurably disadvantages independent commentary in algorithmic news/political surfacing, particularly on search and Up-Next. The disadvantage is not the same as "suppression" of subscriber-driven reach but does compress the discovery funnel for new viewers.
- **Low-to-medium confidence:** Whether the "authoritative" weighting has tightened in 2024–2026 specifically (beyond its 2017–2020 baseline) is unclear from public sources. Some creators and outside observers have alleged tightening; YouTube has not announced one.

## Synthesis: What the Evidence Supports and What It Does Not

**On the specific Laine "2026 algorithm tranche of CRTC regulations" claim:** No such CRTC instrument exists in the public record as of May 2026. The CRTC's 2026 work is principally consultative (Phase 3 implementation of C-11, including discoverability rules whose final form has not yet been adopted, and a December 2025–March 2026 consultation on diversity, equity and inclusion). The statutory framework forbids the CRTC from ordering a specific algorithm (Section 9.1(8)), and the binding November 2023 Policy Direction excludes social media users and user-generated content and instructs the regulator to minimize algorithmic changes. The CRTC's enforcement actions to date target money flows (5% revenue contributions from large foreign streamers) and definitional matters (what qualifies as Cancon, with the November 18, 2025 audio-visual decision), not the recommendation behaviour of YouTube's algorithm toward Canadian political commentators.

**On the like-to-view ratio cited as evidence:** Ratios of 15–22% are extraordinary by industry benchmarks but are not, on their own, dispositive evidence of suppression. They are equally — and more conventionally — interpreted as the signature of a small, highly loyal, subscriber-dominated audience that is approaching saturation of its addressable core. Distinguishing the two interpretations would require channel-level YouTube Studio analytics (Subscribers vs. Non-Subscribers reach; impressions click-through-rate; traffic source breakdowns) that have not been made public by the creators making the suppression claim.

**On the apparent 70K–95K view ceiling:** Three documented dynamics, jointly, are sufficient to account for this pattern without invoking CRTC action:

1. The realistic addressable English-language Canadian political-commentary YouTube audience is in the low millions of monthly users, with a much smaller (likely six-figure) "frequent viewer" core. A video reliably reaching 70K–95K views is reaching a meaningful share of the available regular audience.
2. The April 28, 2025 Canadian federal election drove a peak attention cycle; the documented post-election contraction pattern (mirrored in U.S. data from late 2024 and Q1–Q2 2025) predicts substantial year-over-year compression for Canadian political channels in the May 2025–April 2026 window.
3. YouTube's longstanding (2017-onward) "authoritative voices" prioritization structurally limits the discovery funnel for independent political commentary in favour of legacy news sources — a platform-wide, not Canada-specific, dynamic.

**On the broader complaint pattern:** Independent political YouTubers across the U.S. (left and right) have reported equivalent "suppression" complaints throughout 2024 and 2025. That cross-jurisdictional ubiquity argues against a CRTC-specific cause.

**Confidence summary:**

| Claim | Confidence |
|---|---|
| No current CRTC algorithm regulation exists | High |
| Specific Laine "2026 algorithm tranche" does not correspond to any identifiable CRTC instrument | High |
| Future CRTC discoverability rules *could* affect Canadian creators (forecast) | Medium |
| Cited like-to-view ratios do not, alone, prove suppression | High |
| Post-election cycle accounts for substantial view compression in May 2025–April 2026 | High |
| YouTube's "authoritative voices" weighting structurally disadvantages independent commentary globally | Medium-high |
| Canada-specific platform-level geo-siloing of YouTube content is documented | Low (no public evidence of YouTube doing this in Canada) |

**Caveats and limits of this brief:**

- Public observers do not have access to creator-level YouTube Studio analytics. The brief therefore cannot definitively rule out abnormal traffic-source compositions on the cited videos.
- YouTube does not publicly disclose its ranking algorithm, its authoritative-source list, or country-level recommendation weightings, so any platform-side analysis is necessarily inferential.
- The CRTC's Phase 3 implementation work remains active through 2026. Future decisions — particularly on discoverability — could change the regulatory picture. This brief addresses the situation as of May 2026.
- Several sources cited in the research (e.g., True North, Freezenet, OpenMedia, YouTube's own blog, partisan engagement-rate calculator vendors) have identifiable editorial positions on Bill C-11 and should be treated as advocacy-inflected rather than disinterested.

- Meta's response to Canada's Online News Act (Bill C-18) is a documented case of platform-level Canadian content siloing: since August 2023, Facebook and Instagram have blocked news links for Canadian users. Google reached a separate $100 million/year agreement and was exempted from individual bargaining via a CRTC decision. There is no equivalent documented siloing by YouTube in response to Canadian legislation.
- YouTube's response to Bill C-11, articulated by Jeanette Patell (head of government affairs and public policy, YouTube Canada) in 2022–23, was to warn that CRTC discoverability requirements *could* hurt Canadian creators' global reach because forced exposure to uninterested Canadian audiences would lower click-through and engagement signals, which would in turn reduce global recommendation weight. This is a documented platform-stated concern, not documented platform behaviour.
- In early 2025, multiple news outlets (Stockholm Center for Freedom; Nordic Monitor) reported that Google/YouTube algorithm changes coincided with significant traffic drops at independent Turkish news outlets after a January 2025 update. This is documented in third-party reporting but evidence is largely outlet-attributed; Google has not confirmed the changes were country-targeted.

**Inferential assessment (medium confidence):** There is no public evidence that YouTube has implemented a Canada-specific geo-siloing measure that would systematically suppress cross-border discovery of Canadian political commentary. The "authoritative sources" prioritization is a global policy that predates the current cycle and has been documented to disadvantage independent commentary in many jurisdictions, not only Canada. **Confidence that no Canada-specific geo-restriction has been publicly disclosed: high. Confidence that the global "authoritative sources" weighting structurally disadvantages independent political commentary: medium-high — this is the platform's own publicly stated design intent.**

## 3. Canadian YouTube Market Size and the Realistic Ceiling for Political Commentary

**Documented facts:**

- According to DataReportal's *Digital 2025: Canada* report (based on Google's own advertising-reach data), YouTube's ad-reach audience in Canada was 31.7 million users in early 2025 — roughly 79.4% of Canada's total population and about 83.4% of the total Canadian internet user base. (Ad-reach is not identical to monthly active users but is the most commonly cited proxy.)
- Comscore data cited by Google Canada places YouTube's reach at "98% of Canadians across all audiences" on a less restrictive definition.
- Oxford Economics' 2022 YouTube Canada Impact Report attributed CA$2.0 billion in GDP contribution and ~35,000 FTE jobs to YouTube's Canadian creative ecosystem (a figure citable but not independent of YouTube).
- 71% of all political content viewership on live-streaming platforms in 2024 (per Stream Hatchet) was tied to U.S. political events; only 29% concerned non-U.S. politics. Rumble's political viewership was 94% U.S.-focused.
- Canada's federal voting population in the April 28, 2025 election was approximately 28 million eligible electors; turnout was 69.5% (~19.5 million voters).
- 2025 polling and Pew-style data on the share of Canadians who regularly consume political YouTube specifically is not publicly published at the same granularity as the U.S. Pew News Influencers studies (which found that English-language U.S. political-news influencers on YouTube command audiences in the tens of millions cumulatively).

**Inferential assessment (medium confidence):** A reasonable estimate of the *active English-language Canadian political-YouTube audience* — i.e., people who regularly seek out political commentary in English on YouTube as opposed to news-clip viewers — is in the low single-digit millions at most. Canada's English-speaking population is ~26 million; of that, perhaps a quarter to a third use YouTube for any kind of news/political content with some regularity (extrapolating from U.S. Pew data showing about a third of U.S. adults regularly get news from YouTube). The "regular political-commentary" subset is substantially smaller — likely well under 2 million for any given month, and a much smaller "frequent viewer" core. A single Canadian political commentary video routinely reaching 70,000–95,000 Canadian views is therefore consistent with reaching a meaningful slice (3–10%) of the active addressable audience, which is typical *strong* performance for that niche, not a sign of suppression. **Confidence: medium** — the upper bound is reasonably well-anchored in YouTube ad-reach data; the active-political-viewer subset is an inference from comparable U.S. consumption data because no equivalent Canadian Pew-style study has been published.

**Comparator:** Top Canadian political YouTube channels (e.g., True North, Rebel News, Juno News) typically post videos that pull in the tens of thousands to low hundreds of thousands of views; only viral or rage-bait crossover content regularly clears 500K. By contrast, top U.S. independent political YouTubers (Brian Tyler Cohen, MeidasTouch, Megyn Kelly, Tim Pool, Ben Shapiro) routinely generate millions of views per video, drawing on an addressable English-speaking U.S. audience roughly 10x the Canadian one and with substantial international spillover.

## 4. Canadian vs. U.S. Independent Political YouTube Comparison

**Documented facts:**

- A Chaotic Era / Pixability analysis of 200 partisan U.S. political YouTube channels (each >100,000 subscribers) found that the top tier of U.S. political channels added 22.9 million combined subscribers in Q1 2025 and 18.1 million in Q2 2025 — a noticeable slowdown attributed to post-Trump-inauguration interest fading. Right-leaning channels outpaced left-leaning channels in Q2 2025 net adds (9.4 million vs. 8.6 million). Pixability separately tracked a 25.6% increase in left-leaning channel views and a 10.08% decline in right-leaning channel views comparing January–February 2025 vs. March–April 2025.
- Streams Charts (April 2025 report) documented a 56% year-over-year increase in political-livestream watch time across YouTube/Twitch/Kick in Q1 2025 vs. Q1 2024 (3.4 billion vs. 2.61 billion hours), with YouTube accounting for ~98% of measured watch time. Their Q1 2025 figure was a slight decrease from Q4 2024 — consistent with a post-U.S.-election contraction.
- Liberal Media Subscriber Decline: Multiple commentary outlets reported (December 2024 onward) that left-leaning U.S. political YouTube and TikTok creators saw subscriber and view declines after the November 2024 election. This is sourced primarily to opinion outlets rather than peer-reviewed measurement.
- Documented complaints of "algorithmic suppression" from U.S. independent political creators across the political spectrum have been recurrent since at least 2019, particularly intensifying after YouTube's January 2019 "borderline content" recommendation changes and the 2020 election-integrity policy. The complaint is platform-wide, not Canada-specific.

**Inferential assessment (medium-high confidence):**

- U.S. independent political channels enjoy a viewership ceiling roughly an order of magnitude above Canadian ones, attributable to (a) population scale, (b) the global salience of U.S. politics (which draws non-U.S. viewers to U.S. creators), and (c) a deeper sponsorship / monetization ecosystem.
- Canadian channels rarely benefit from substantial cross-border spillover into the U.S. market in the way U.S. creators benefit from Canadian audiences. This is not a CRTC effect — it is a demand-side artifact: U.S. viewers are not generally seeking out commentary on Canadian politics.
- "Algorithmic suppression" is a complaint shared across U.S. and Canadian independent creators post-2024, post-2025. Its ubiquity argues strongly against a CRTC-specific cause for the Canadian version of the complaint. **Confidence: medium-high.**

## 5. Post-Election Attention Cycle and the Canadian 2025 Timeline

**Documented facts:**

- The 2025 Canadian federal election was held April 28, 2025. Voter turnout was 69.5% — the highest since 1993. The Liberals (under Mark Carney) won a minority government with the highest vote share since 1984.
- YouTube's own published election data for the U.S. 2024 cycle reported >45 million U.S. viewers of election-related content on November 5, 2024 alone, with top videos (Joe Rogan's Trump and Vance interviews; Jubilee's Ben Shapiro segment) drawing tens of millions of views in election week. This is consistent with documented spike-and-contract patterns around election cycles globally.
- Stream Hatchet data documented political-livestream viewership tripling on Twitch from ~5 million hours in January 2024 to ~15 million in July/August 2024 during the U.S. campaign — followed by a fall-off.
- Pixability/Chaotic Era documented the Q1-to-Q2 2025 subscriber-growth slowdown for U.S. political channels (above), explicitly attributing it to the fading post-inauguration news cycle.

**Inferential assessment (high confidence):** A pronounced post-election viewership and engagement contraction is the documented norm for political YouTube. For Canadian creators who built their audience during the March–April 2025 election campaign — the most consequential, hard-fought Canadian campaign in over a decade, dominated by U.S. tariff and annexation threats — a six- to twelve-month contraction beginning in May 2025 is the *expected* trajectory in the absence of any regulatory action. By May 2026 (the date of writing), Canadian political YouTubers are 12+ months past peak election attention and should be expected to be at a structural trough relative to their April 2025 peaks. **Confidence: high** that a substantial part of any observed view-count compression for Canadian political channels in late 2025 / early 2026 is attributable to the normal post-election attention cycle, regardless of any algorithmic or regulatory factor.

## 6. Like-to-View Ratio Analysis: What an 18% Ratio Actually Indicates

**Documented facts on benchmarks (multiple sources triangulated):**

- HypeAuditor / SociaVault / UpGrowth / Creator Hero / WizStudio / LiveReacting / Marketing Charts engagement-rate calculators (drawing on aggregate samples ranging from ~100,000 to 350,000 channels) converge on the following YouTube like-to-view ratio benchmarks for long-form content:
  - Platform-wide average: ~1–4% (varies by source and methodology; the most commonly cited figure is 3.75–4%).
  - "Strong" engagement: 5–7%.
  - "Exceptional" / top-decile: above 10%.
  - SociaVault's 2026 analysis of 75,000 channels found nano channels (1K–10K subs) averaged 5.23% engagement per view; macro channels (100K–500K) averaged 2.12%.
  - Niche-specific: gaming ~5.47%, education and tech-review channels in the 3–4% range, music as low as 1.87–2.28%.
- A like-to-view ratio of ~18% (13K/72K = 18.1%; 5K/22K = 22.7%) sits well above the conventionally "exceptional" 10%+ threshold.
- Industry guidance (SoNuker, Creator Hero) notes that consistently sustaining a >15% like-to-view ratio on a channel with more than 10,000 subscribers is rare and, when sustained, typically indicates either (a) a highly loyal, ideologically aligned core audience where most viewers are already subscribers/regulars, or (b) artificial inflation. There is no public evidence of the latter in the Laine/Claypool/Smith cases.

**Inferential assessment (high confidence):** A like-to-view ratio of 15–22% is not, in itself, evidence of algorithmic suppression. It is — under the prevailing analytical framework used by audit tools, marketing analysts and the platform itself — a *direct indicator* of an audience composed predominantly of returning, ideologically aligned subscribers rather than newly recommended/discovered viewers. The two interpretations are analytically distinct but not mutually exclusive:

- *Interpretation A (audience composition):* A high like ratio reflects that nearly every viewer is a fan. The channel is "preaching to the choir." This is the standard marketing-analytics reading and does not require any algorithmic intervention to produce.
- *Interpretation B (suppression):* The high ratio reflects an artificial ceiling: the platform is exposing the content only to existing subscribers (who like it at very high rates) but not surfacing it to new audiences (who would consume but not engage at the same rate). Under this interpretation, the missing low-engagement viewers are the "tell."

These two interpretations cannot be distinguished from the like-to-view ratio alone. Distinguishing them would require examining the creator's YouTube Studio analytics — specifically the "Subscribers vs. Non-Subscribers" reach breakdown, the impressions-to-views click-through rate from "Browse features" and "Suggested videos," and the impressions volume on Home/Suggested over time. None of these analytics are publicly visible from a video page; they are accessible only to the creator. Public observers, including the affected creators in their own public posts, have not (as of this writing) published those internal analytics to substantiate the suppression interpretation.

**Confidence:** High that the cited like-to-view ratios are insufficient evidence to establish suppression on their own; they are equally consistent with a small, loyal, audience-saturated base — which is the expected profile for a Canadian political commentator with a strong subscriber-to-view ratio post-election.

## 7. Legacy / "Authoritative" Source Prioritization on YouTube

**Documented facts:**

- YouTube formally announced the "raise authoritative voices" recommendation policy in 2017 and expanded it through 2018–2020. Per YouTube's own blog posts (notably the January 2019 "Continuing our work to improve recommendations on YouTube" announcement and the 2020 elections-support post), the platform commits to surfacing "authoritative" news sources — explicitly named examples have included CNN, Fox News, and The Guardian — at the top of search results, in "Up Next" panels, and on the homepage for news and breaking-news queries. YouTube stated in 2020 that consumption from authoritative news partners' channels had grown 60% year-over-year as a result.
- YouTube does not publicly disclose its full list of "authoritative" sources or the criteria used to designate them.
- YouTube's January 2019 recommendation change targeted "borderline content and content that could misinform users in harmful ways" — content not violating policies but considered low-quality. YouTube reported that the change reduced views of such content from recommendations by more than 50%.
- A CBC News investigation (October 2019) that analyzed 20,000+ YouTube recommendations on Canadian-politics search terms ahead of the 2019 federal election found only 2.1% of "Up Next" recommendations were in any way related to the topic searched, suggesting the algorithm was actively *steering away* from political content toward viral/popular non-political content. Karen Yeung (University of Birmingham) characterized the lack of relevant political recommendations as a form of de facto censorship.
- A 2024 NBC News-reported academic study of YouTube's recommendation system on built-from-scratch sock-puppet accounts found a tendency to recommend right-leaning and Christian content even to ideologically neutral profiles. A 2022 Brookings study found YouTube's recommendation algorithm pulled users "slightly to the right of the political spectrum" on average. A 2025 PNAS Nexus analysis found asymmetric moderation — the algorithm steered users *away from far-right content* more aggressively than away from far-left content. Research conclusions are genuinely contested.
- YouTube's January 2025 algorithm refinement and the July 15, 2025 "inauthentic content" enforcement wave (targeting reused/templated/AI-generated content) tightened monetization and visibility for channels using mass-produced formats.

**Inferential assessment:**

- **High confidence:** YouTube has, by its own admission, structurally prioritized "authoritative" (i.e., legacy and major-mainstream) news sources in news/political surfacing since 2017. This is a global, platform-wide policy, not a Canada-specific or CRTC-driven policy.
- **Medium confidence:** This prioritization measurably disadvantages independent commentary in algorithmic news/political surfacing, particularly on search and Up-Next. The disadvantage is not the same as "suppression" of subscriber-driven reach but does compress the discovery funnel for new viewers.
- **Low-to-medium confidence:** Whether the "authoritative" weighting has tightened in 2024–2026 specifically (beyond its 2017–2020 baseline) is unclear from public sources. Some creators and outside observers have alleged tightening; YouTube has not announced one.

## Synthesis: What the Evidence Supports and What It Does Not

**On the specific Laine "2026 algorithm tranche of CRTC regulations" claim:** No such CRTC instrument exists in the public record as of May 2026. The CRTC's 2026 work is principally consultative (Phase 3 implementation of C-11, including discoverability rules whose final form has not yet been adopted, and a December 2025–March 2026 consultation on diversity, equity and inclusion). The statutory framework forbids the CRTC from ordering a specific algorithm (Section 9.1(8)), and the binding November 2023 Policy Direction excludes social media users and user-generated content and instructs the regulator to minimize algorithmic changes. The CRTC's enforcement actions to date target money flows (5% revenue contributions from large foreign streamers) and definitional matters (what qualifies as Cancon, with the November 18, 2025 audio-visual decision), not the recommendation behaviour of YouTube's algorithm toward Canadian political commentators.

**On the like-to-view ratio cited as evidence:** Ratios of 15–22% are extraordinary by industry benchmarks but are not, on their own, dispositive evidence of suppression. They are equally — and more conventionally — interpreted as the signature of a small, highly loyal, subscriber-dominated audience that is approaching saturation of its addressable core. Distinguishing the two interpretations would require channel-level YouTube Studio analytics (Subscribers vs. Non-Subscribers reach; impressions click-through-rate; traffic source breakdowns) that have not been made public by the creators making the suppression claim.

**On the apparent 70K–95K view ceiling:** Three documented dynamics, jointly, are sufficient to account for this pattern without invoking CRTC action:

1. The realistic addressable English-language Canadian political-commentary YouTube audience is in the low millions of monthly users, with a much smaller (likely six-figure) "frequent viewer" core. A video reliably reaching 70K–95K views is reaching a meaningful share of the available regular audience.
2. The April 28, 2025 Canadian federal election drove a peak attention cycle; the documented post-election contraction pattern (mirrored in U.S. data from late 2024 and Q1–Q2 2025) predicts substantial year-over-year compression for Canadian political channels in the May 2025–April 2026 window.
3. YouTube's longstanding (2017-onward) "authoritative voices" prioritization structurally limits the discovery funnel for independent political commentary in favour of legacy news sources — a platform-wide, not Canada-specific, dynamic.

**On the broader complaint pattern:** Independent political YouTubers across the U.S. (left and right) have reported equivalent "suppression" complaints throughout 2024 and 2025. That cross-jurisdictional ubiquity argues against a CRTC-specific cause.

**Confidence summary:**

| Claim | Confidence |
|---|---|
| No current CRTC algorithm regulation exists | High |
| Specific Laine "2026 algorithm tranche" does not correspond to any identifiable CRTC instrument | High |
| Future CRTC discoverability rules *could* affect Canadian creators (forecast) | Medium |
| Cited like-to-view ratios do not, alone, prove suppression | High |
| Post-election cycle accounts for substantial view compression in May 2025–April 2026 | High |
| YouTube's "authoritative voices" weighting structurally disadvantages independent commentary globally | Medium-high |
| Canada-specific platform-level geo-siloing of YouTube content is documented | Low (no public evidence of YouTube doing this in Canada) |

**Caveats and limits of this brief:**

- Public observers do not have access to creator-level YouTube Studio analytics. The brief therefore cannot definitively rule out abnormal traffic-source compositions on the cited videos.
- YouTube does not publicly disclose its ranking algorithm, its authoritative-source list, or country-level recommendation weightings, so any platform-side analysis is necessarily inferential.
- The CRTC's Phase 3 implementation work remains active through 2026. Future decisions — particularly on discoverability — could change the regulatory picture. This brief addresses the situation as of May 2026.
- Several sources cited in the research (e.g., True North, Freezenet, OpenMedia, YouTube's own blog, partisan engagement-rate calculator vendors) have identifiable editorial positions on Bill C-11 and should be treated as advocacy-inflected rather than disinterested.
