Someone has driven to a friend's house a dozen times with a phone giving directions. One day the phone dies on the way, and they find they do not know the route. They have been there often, but they have never found their way there. London taxi trainees are the opposite case: those who learned the city well enough to plan any route themselves gained grey matter in the posterior hippocampus, and those who did not qualify did not [12]. In one case a device produces the route; in the other the driver does.

Cognitive ectothermy is the name for the first case, made general. It is drawn from the working paper *Why Temperature*, which reads McLuhan's hot and cool as a theory of regulation.

## Definition

**Cognitive ectothermy** is relying on the environment to supply understanding, as an ectotherm relies on outside heat. Answers, routes and explanations arrive from outside, and when the supply stops, so does the person's ability to produce them.

**Cognitive endothermy** is the trained capacity to produce one's own predictions, recall and explanations.

Unlike the animal kinds, these are not fixed. People move between them according to how they spend their time.

Two terms carry the rest. A medium is **hot**, for a given task and receiver, when it supplies most of the output in the part of the task being learned before the receiver has predicted it. It is **cool** when the receiver produces much of that output, or predicts it before the answer arrives. A **cool excursion** is a brief, deliberate period of producing before receiving, within a mostly warm environment.

(Biologists use "endothermic brain" for a different idea, about how warm-bloodedness shaped animal cognition. The terms here describe a person's relation to information.)

## Prior work

Each part of this theory has been studied under another name. What has not been done is to join them, or to answer why McLuhan used temperature at all.

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hot and cool media [15] | Media theory | Hot media are "well filled with data"; cool media ask the audience to complete them | Why the distinction is one of temperature; any mechanism |
| Active learning [3] | Education | Students learn more when they do more | Why order matters; tools outside the classroom |
| Pretesting effect [5] | Cognitive psychology | Guessing before reading helps, even when the guess is wrong | The link to media in general |
| Productive failure [4] | Learning sciences | Solving first, then instruction, beats the reverse | When the receiver is a beginner |
| Expertise reversal [7] | Instructional design | What helps novices hinders experts | The parallel with a body needing fuel |
| Hot/cool systems [10] | Self-control | A fast "go" system and a reflective "know" system | Media as what shifts the balance |
| Cognitive offloading | Cognitive psychology | People hand memory and calculation to tools | What happens to the capacity over time, and how to rebuild it |
| Cold acclimation [8] | Physiology | Regular cold raises the body's own heat production | Any use outside the body |

**What this theory adds.** It answers why McLuhan's distinction is one of temperature: temperature has long been the language for regulation, and a medium loads the regulation of the person using it. It names one mechanism, prediction before the answer, that links the learning findings. It gives two measures that apply to any medium, AI included. And it makes the receiver's capacity, not only their state, the thing at stake.

## Mechanism

A body in the cold produces its own heat; a body warmed from outside stops producing it. The same arrangement holds for a mind and its predictions.

In the predictive account of perception, the brain runs on predictions and learns when incoming signals correct them. A high-definition signal settles the matter at once. A low-definition one leaves gaps that the receiver fills from their own predictions, which is what McLuhan called completion [15].

So the difference between hot and cool comes down to order. A hot medium answers before the receiver has predicted, so there is nothing of the receiver's own for the answer to correct, and little changes. A cool medium leaves the receiver to predict first, and the answer then corrects something the receiver made. That correction is what is learned.

Repeated over months, the order becomes a habit of the environment. Search, feeds, explainers and chatbots supply an answer before most questions are fully formed. In that climate the receiver rarely has to predict, recall or explain, so those capacities are rarely trained, which is ectothermy.

A medium can also heat a receiver by raising arousal. When supply and arousal are both high, the reflective system has the least room. Stress shifts control to the fast, reflexive system [10], and content that arrives finished and arousing travels furthest: each moral-emotional word raised a message's spread by about 20% [11].

## Evidence

The strongest support is for the order effect (P3). Across 225 studies, active learning raised exam performance by 0.47 standard deviations over lecturing [3]. Across 53 studies and 166 comparisons, solving a problem before instruction beat instruction first (Hedges' g = 0.36) [4]. In five experiments, students who attempted questions before reading a passage, getting only 5–22% right, later outscored students who spent the time reading, for example 75% against 56% [5]. In a physics course, students learned more from active sessions than from a polished lecture while rating the lecture higher [6].

The relation (P1) shows most directly with AI. In a field experiment with nearly a thousand high-school students, the same model was set up to give answers or to give hints. With answers, students did worse than controls once the tool was removed; with hints, they did not [2].

The boundary (P4) is well measured. Worked solutions help novices and hinder experienced learners [7], and in the productive-failure data the youngest children did better with instruction first [4]. A body produces heat in the cold only if it has fuel; a learner with nothing to predict with is left cold by a cool medium.

The capacity claim (P6) rests on an analogy. Ten days of cold exposure raised people's heat production without shivering, and the cold came to feel more comfortable [8]; the authors of a review of indoor climate recommend regular excursions outside the comfort zone [9]. GPS users stopped more and drew worse route maps than map users [13], and people remembered less of objects they photographed than of objects they only looked at [14].

The paper scored thirteen paired comparisons from the learning research, with a second, blind coder. Nine fit, three showed no reliable difference (calculators, laptop against handwritten notes, and the Google effect, which failed to replicate), and one reversed (worked examples for novices). The full table is in the paper.

## Measures

Two quantities make the theory checkable for any exchange, for a given task and receiver.

1. **Completion share.** How much of the output, in the part of the task being learned, does the receiver produce?
2. **Order.** Does the receiver predict or attempt before the answer arrives, or after?

A third applies to the medium: **arousal**, its effect on the receiver's overall gain. High supply and high arousal at once is **overheating**.

**Worked example: a chatbot and compound interest.** The same model, topic and time can be hot or cool.

| Exchange | What the person produces | Order | Temperature |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| They type "Explain compound interest" and read the answer. | The question only | Answer first | Hot |
| They write: "I think it means interest on interest, so $100 at 10% becomes $121 after two years. Is that right, and where does my picture break?" | Their own model, an example and a calculation | Prediction first | Cool |
| They read the plain answer, close the window, and explain it to a friend that evening. | The explanation, later, in their own words | Answer first, production after | Cooled after the fact |

## Protocol

1. **Predict before you ask.** Write down a guess before searching or prompting.
2. **Recall before you look something up.** Try to retrieve it first, even for a few seconds.
3. **Explain it back.** After watching or reading an explanation, put it in your own words, aloud or on paper.
4. **Start hot when new, then cool by degrees.** A beginner learns more from worked examples; remove the worked steps one at a time as skill grows. The instructional research calls this fading.
5. **Cool an overheated feed before reacting.** Delay, step back, and attend to the abstract point rather than the arousing detail [10].
6. **Build tools cool.** A tool can ask for the user's guess first, give hints before answers, and ask for the answer explained back [2].

Regularity matters more than intensity, as with cold exposure [8].

## A four-question check

For any tool you rely on for a task you want to keep:

1. Is this the part of the task you want to get better at, or keep?
2. Does the tool supply the output before you have tried?
3. Could you do the task if the tool were gone tomorrow?
4. Do you know enough to make a reasonable first guess?

A yes to the first two and a no to the third means the tool is keeping you warm in a skill you want. If the answer to the fourth is yes, add a cool excursion: guess first, then check. If it is no, stay hot for now and cool as you learn. This check is a working tool built from the measures above; it has not been validated.

**Worked example: the dead phone.** The route is a skill the driver wants (1: yes). The phone gives each turn before the driver thinks of it (2: yes). Without it, they are lost (3: no). They have driven the route a dozen times (4: yes). So: before each trip, say the route aloud, then let the phone confirm it.

## Scope

The theory applies when a person wants to keep or build the capacity a medium supplies. It does not apply when the output is not something the person needs to produce: no one needs to compute a square root by hand to use one. The calculator result fits this limit, since most of the studies introduced calculators after arithmetic had been learned.

It does not say hot media are bad. For a beginner, a hot medium is often the right start, and for a task the person will never need to do unaided, supply is the point.

The physiology is used as a model of regulation, not as a claim that information has temperature.

## Counter-evidence

Three of the thirteen comparisons show no reliable difference. Calculators did students no harm in two meta-analyses; typed and handwritten notes did not differ on replication; and the "Google effect" on memory failed to replicate. One comparison reverses for novices, which the theory treats as its boundary rather than a fit.

The scoring is a count of directions, not a meta-analysis, and the blind coder was a separate AI model working from descriptions. Several supporting studies are small (15 children in one), and one is a preprint that has drawn a published critique.

The capacity argument has no direct test. Cold acclimation is measured in bodies over days; no study follows people taking information "excursions" over weeks and measures what they can produce afterwards.

The arousal half joins separate findings. The link from hot media to the fast, reflexive system has not been tested as one claim.

Finally, the reading of McLuhan is a reading. He may have taken hot and cool from the slang of his day without thinking about bodies. The claim is that the metaphor carries a physiology that fits his definitions, not that he intended it.
