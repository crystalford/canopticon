A morning routine runs without thought: the kettle, the coffee, the cream, a supplement stirred in. What it rests on does not. The cream is bought once a week or so, in a shop, on the way to somewhere else, with money that has to be there that week. None of that is a habit, and none of it is tracked. When one piece lapses, the morning does not lose one step. The coffee goes, and whatever was mixed into it, and the order of everything that came after.

This theory names the weak point in routines like that one: the things a habit rests on that are not habits themselves.

## Definition

**The restock gap** is the difference in reliability between a habit and the things it rests on. A habit runs automatically; its **dependencies** do not. A dependency is anything the habit needs and does not provide for itself: a supply, the money that buys it, a service or subscription, equipment that must be charged or replaced, another person's part. Each is kept up by an **upkeep task**: buying, paying, renewing, refilling, asking. A **carrier habit** is one that other behaviours have been tied to, as when a supplement is taken in the morning coffee. The **failure reminder** is the moment a dependency lapses, which for most upkeep tasks is the only reminder they have.

The theory's central claim is that a routine is only as reliable as the least automatic thing it rests on, that this is usually an upkeep task, and that the upkeep task's reminder arrives at the moment the routine needs it. The name comes from the commonest case, a supply that has to be restocked.

**What this theory adds.** It identifies a frequency mismatch built into habit chains: the conditions that make a daily behaviour automatic are the conditions its supply tasks can never meet. It explains why those tasks are put off, why their failure arrives at the worst moment (their reminder is the failure), and why one failure stops more than one step (chunking and carriers). And it borrows its remedy from operations research, where the same problem was solved for hospital supplies.

## Mechanism

A behaviour becomes automatic through repetition in a stable setting. In a study of people repeating a chosen behaviour daily in the same context, automaticity rose along a curve over weeks to months [1]. Behaviours done less often, or in shifting contexts, keep needing thought to guide them [2].

A daily habit meets those conditions. Its supply tasks do not. Cream is bought weekly, floss replaced monthly, a filter changed every few months, each at a different time and often in a different place. So the habit runs automatically while the tasks that keep it supplied stay deliberate, and deliberate tasks compete for attention with everything else. A task with an immediate cost and a delayed payoff is the kind people put off [4] [16]; a trip to the shop for one item fits that description exactly.

The supply task's reminder makes this worse. Without some other signal, the prompt to restock is running out, and running out happens at the moment of use. The reminder and the failure are the same event.

The failure then spreads. A practised sequence is stored and run as a unit, with brain activity marking its start and end [6], and people perform learned sequences as chunks [7]. When one element of the context a habit depends on goes missing, the habit is disrupted [3] [8]. A missing supply interrupts the stored sequence at that step, and the steps after it have lost their place. Behaviours tied to the habit fail with it: a supplement taken in the coffee is skipped when the coffee is. Tying a behaviour to an existing habit makes it stronger [11], and it also makes it depend on the carrier.

Finally, the chain is hard to see. People doing habitual behaviours think about other things [2] and tend to explain their habits as intended [12]. A person whose morning falls apart is likely to blame the morning, or themselves, and less likely to trace it to the cream.

## Evidence

Automaticity needs frequent, context-stable repetition (P1). In 82 people repeating a behaviour daily in a fixed context, automaticity followed an asymptotic curve, reaching 95% of its maximum in 18 to 254 days; missing a single opportunity did not materially affect it [1]. In hourly diaries, behaviours performed almost daily in stable contexts went with unrelated thoughts and less stress, while less frequent behaviours needed thought [2].

Supply tasks fall outside those conditions (P2). This follows from P1; no study has measured the automaticity of supply tasks as such.

Deliberate tasks with an immediate cost get put off (P3). A meta-analysis of 691 correlations found task aversiveness and task delay among the strongest and most consistent predictors of procrastination [4]. Models of present bias show that people who are naive about their own future self-control put off activities with immediate costs [16].

Depletion is the default reminder, and an earlier one works (P4). Hospitals keep supplies at the point of use with two bins: when the first empties it is sent for refilling while the second is used, so the restocking signal comes before the stock is gone [5].

Practised sequences are stored as units (P5). As rats learned a maze routine, activity in the sensorimotor striatum came to emphasise the beginning and end of the automated procedure, and stayed that way for weeks [6]. People learning a ten-step button sequence spontaneously grouped it into chunks, and performance on a shuffled sequence was quicker and more accurate when the chunks were preserved [7].

A missing element disrupts the habit (P6). Students' exercise, newspaper-reading and television habits survived a transfer to a new university only when aspects of the performance context stayed the same [3]. A recent review states that environmental changes that disrupt cue exposure discontinue habitual action, though the disruption may be short-lived [8].

Preparation drives the behaviour (P7). In 181 adults recruited from gyms, habit in the preparatory phase predicted exercise over six weeks while habit in the performance phase did not [9]. In a randomised trial with 94 new gym members, a habit-building intervention raised activity, and preparatory habit was among the factors that carried the effect on self-reported activity [10]. Instigation habit, the habit of starting, likewise predicted exercise frequency where execution habit did not [14].

Carriers strengthen and bind (P8). People told to floss after brushing formed stronger habits and flossed more at eight months than those told to floss before [11]. The binding half follows from cue-dependence; it has not been measured.

The chain is hard to see (P9). People's thoughts during habitual behaviour were unrelated to the behaviour [2], and people tend to infer from the frequency of a behaviour that they must have intended it [12].

A routine follows its supply (P10). Among food stamp recipients, caloric intake declined by 10 to 15 percent over the 30 days after benefits arrived [17]. The author reads the decline as impatience, consuming the supply early, which is the same present bias that puts off upkeep tasks (P3).

Tracking helps (P11). Across 138 randomised studies, interventions that got people to monitor their progress raised goal attainment by 0.40 of a standard deviation, and the effect was larger when the information was physically recorded [18].

What fails most often in households (P12) has not been studied.

## Measures

Four quantities make the theory checkable for any routine.

1. **Supply interval.** How often each supply task recurs, against how often the habit it serves recurs. The larger the ratio, the wider the gap.
2. **Reminder type.** Whether each supply task is prompted by running out, by a level set in advance, or by a fixed occasion.
3. **Dependents.** How many steps and tied behaviours stop when the supply is missing.
4. **Failure trace.** When a routine fails, whether the person traces it to the supply, and how long the failure lasts.

The theory expects routines to fail most often where the supply interval is long, the reminder is running out, and the dependents are many.

## Protocol

1. **List what the routine rests on.** Walk through it once and name every supply, payment, service and person it needs.
2. **Write the list down and check it on a fixed day.** Monitoring helps goals hold, most of all when it is recorded [18].
3. **Find the carriers.** Note which habits have other behaviours tied to them, and what those behaviours would need if the carrier stopped.
4. **Move the reminder ahead of failure.** Keep a second unit in reserve and buy when you open it, or set a level at which you restock, as the two-bin system does [5].
5. **Tie the upkeep task to a fixed occasion.** Put the item on a standing list, or buy it on a trip that already happens every week.
6. **Give each carrier a fallback.** Decide in advance what the tied behaviour does if the carrier is missing, so one failure stops one thing.

## A four-question check

For any routine that has failed, or that you rely on:

1. Does it rest on something restocked, paid for or renewed less often than the routine runs?
2. Is that restock prompted only by running out?
3. Do other behaviours depend on the routine?
4. When it last failed, did you trace the failure to the supply?

A yes to the first question means the theory applies. Yes to the second or third, or no to the fourth, adds to the risk. This check is a working tool built from the measures above; it has not been validated.

**Worked example: morning coffee.** Cream is bought weekly while coffee is made daily (1: yes). The only reminder is an empty carton (2: yes). A supplement is taken in the coffee, and the coffee starts the morning's work (3: yes). The bad morning was blamed on the morning (4: no). Keeping a second carton, and buying a new one when it is opened, moves the reminder ahead of depletion.

**Worked example: flossing.** Floss is replaced every month or two, flossing happens daily (1: yes), and a new pack is bought when the old one runs out (2: yes). Nothing else depends on flossing (3: no). A spare pack in the drawer closes the gap.

**Worked example outside the home: a printer in a small office.** Printing is daily and toner is replaced every few months (1: yes), ordered when the printer says it is empty (2: yes), and invoices and labels wait on it (3: yes). Office supply services already use the remedy: order at the first low-toner warning, or keep one cartridge in reserve.

## Scope

The theory applies to routines that consume or wear out a material: food and drink, hygiene, medication taken from a supply, equipment that needs charging or replacing. It applies in the same way to money and services: a routine paid for by a subscription lapses when the subscription does, and eating follows the cycle of the money that buys food [17]. It also applies to routines that depend on a less frequent maintenance task, such as a bicycle ride that depends on inflated tyres.

It concerns the reliability of chains of habits. It says nothing about which habits are worth keeping. It also does not claim that habits are fragile in general: a single missed opportunity does not undo a habit [1], and disruption caused by a missing cue can be brief [8].

## Counter-evidence

Habits recover. A missed day does not reset automaticity [1], so a supply failure costs a bad morning more than it costs the habit, unless failures repeat often enough to break the consistency that sustains it.

Some supply tasks do become habitual. A weekly shop on the same day at the same store is a stable context repeated often enough to automate, and a person with a weekly shopping habit may close the gap without knowing it. The gap is widest where supply tasks are ad hoc, and those are common but not universal.

A disrupted chain can also be an opportunity. Interventions to change behaviour worked better among people who had recently moved house, within about three months of the move [15]. When a carrier habit fails, it is a chance to rebuild the chain on purpose.

The evidence for each part comes from different fields and settings. Habit formation was studied with chosen health behaviours, chunking with button presses and rats in mazes, preparatory habits with exercise, and the two-bin system with hospital stockrooms. The theory's claim that these parts combine in household routines, and its expectation about which routines fail most often, have not been tested.
