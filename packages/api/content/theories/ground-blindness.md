A phone dies on the way to a friend's house, and the driver finds they do not know the route. A routine is dropped because the problem it treated has gone, and the problem comes back. A student who did well with an AI tutor sits the exam without it and does worse than students who never had one. Each time, something had been doing work that nobody could see while it was there, and the work showed only when it stopped.

This theory names that condition and draws a rule from it. McLuhan called the part of an environment that goes unnoticed its ground, and the part that is noticed its figure [1]. Ground blindness is not being able to see the ground while standing on it.

## Definition

**Ground blindness** is the inability to see what a tool, routine, substance or environment contributes, or costs, while it is in steady use. The thing is judged by what it is like to have it, because what it is like to be without it is no longer on hand.

**Removal** is any period without the thing: forced, as when a device fails, or planned. **The removal effect** is what changes when it is taken away. **The presence effect** is what changes when it is added.

The theory's central claim is that the two effects are different measurements and can point in opposite directions, so neither can be read from the other.

**What this theory adds.** It joins these into one claim about people and the things they rely on. It names the finding that the presence effect and the removal effect can have opposite signs, which makes a tool impossible to judge from either alone. It shows from experiments that judgements made while a thing is present are biased in a known direction. And it turns the clinic's and the engineer's method into an everyday one, with the cost of removal counted.

Chesterton's fence is the opposite counsel and is partly right: a fence should not be torn down on a guess. This theory's answer is a gate rather than a demolition. Open it briefly, see what comes through, and close it again.

## Mechanism

Three things combine.

The first is habituation. A steady, unchanging stimulus stops drawing a response [10], and what draws no response stops being noticed. A tool used every day, a routine done every morning, a level of caffeine kept constant, all become part of how things are rather than something one is doing.

The second is adaptation. A body or a habit adjusts to what is always there. In dependence, the brain's reward set point shifts to offset the drug, so the drug comes to restore a baseline it has itself moved [16]. Caffeine works the same way on a smaller scale: in habitual users, much of what feels like a boost is the reversal of withdrawal [15], and they find themselves wanting it partly to avoid the withdrawal [20]. Skills adjust too. A skill handed to a machine goes unpractised, and "physical skills deteriorate when they are not used" [4].

The third is a judgement made at the wrong time. Asked what something is worth while having it, a person can compare it only with an absence they no longer remember accurately. The comparison that matters, with life after a period without it, has not been made. So the value is overstated and the dependence understated [11] [12].

The effects of presence and removal can therefore point in opposite directions. Presence shows the help; removal shows the help plus everything that adapted around it.

## Evidence

Removal shows what presence hides (P1). Four weeks without Facebook reduced online activity, increased time with family and friends, reduced news knowledge and polarisation, raised well-being, and reduced Facebook use after the experiment ended [11]. Stopping oral hygiene revealed gingivitis within weeks [18]; stopping a long-used moisturiser returned dryness within days [19].

The sign can flip (P2). Students who practised maths with an answer-giving AI improved their practice grades by 48%, and then scored 17% below students who never had it when the AI was taken away [13]. Habitual caffeine users perform worse without caffeine, and caffeine restores them; non-users gain little from it, apart from feeling less sleepy, and become more anxious [14]. A review concluded that only long-term withdrawal studies can reveal caffeine's net effect, and that they find little benefit from long-term use over abstinence [15].

Present judgement is biased (P4). After deactivation, people valued Facebook less than before, which suggests that what people say a service is worth while using it overstates it [11]. In a second experiment, users underestimated how much their own use was habit [12].

Automation leaves the skill unpractised (P5). Habitual GPS users navigated worse from memory, and in 13 people retested after three years, more GPS use went with a steeper decline [17].

The method exists already (P6). Clinical trials withdraw long-term treatments from patients who respond, a design suited to "long-term, non-curative therapies" [7]; single-case designs establish a treatment by withdrawing and restoring it [8]; software teams take servers down on purpose to see what fails [9].

## Measures

Four quantities make the theory checkable for any tool, routine or substance.

1. **Presence effect.** What changes when it is added, measured while it is in use.
2. **Removal effect.** What changes when it is taken away, measured after a period without it.
3. **Sign agreement.** Do the two point the same way? Where they differ, the thing is doing something its users cannot see.
4. **Valuation shift.** What a person says the thing is worth before a period without it, and after.

A fifth is the price of the test: how long withdrawal or relapse lasts, and what it costs. Caffeine withdrawal, for example, begins 12–24 hours after the last dose and lasts two to nine days [20].

## Protocol

1. **List what has become ground.** Tools, routines and substances used daily for months, whose absence you have not felt in a long time.
2. **Choose the cheapest to remove.** Prefer what is restored at once and whose absence costs little: a navigation app, an AI assistant, a feed. Do not stop a routine that holds back a standing cause; check the cause instead.
3. **Write down the expected effect first.** What you think will happen, and what the thing is worth to you.
4. **Remove it for a set period**, long enough to pass any withdrawal: a day for a navigation app, a week for caffeine.
5. **Compare.** What changed, which way, and whether what you would pay for it moved. Restore it, or not, on that evidence.

## A four-question check

For any tool, routine or substance you use every day:

1. Have you gone a full day without it in the last month?
2. Can you do the task it does for you, unaided?
3. Did using it start for a reason you could still name?
4. If it disappeared tomorrow, do you know what would happen?

A no to any of them means the thing has become ground. Two or more noes are the case for a planned removal. This check is a working tool built from the measures above; it has not been validated.

**Worked example: navigation.** The driver has not gone a day without GPS (1: no), cannot reach the friend's house unaided (2: no), started using it for unfamiliar trips (3: yes) and does not know what would happen without it (4: no). Three noes: drive the familiar routes without it for a week, and see which ones are actually known.

**Worked example: coffee.** Two cups every morning for years (1: no), work feels impossible without it (2: no), started for early shifts (3: yes), and the effect of stopping is unknown (4: no). Three noes. A week without it is the test; the first two to four days will feel worse, which is withdrawal and not the coffee's benefit [20]. What is left after a week is the comparison that counts [15].

**Worked example outside the body: a software system.** The team cannot say what fails if one service goes down (4: no). The practice is to take it down on purpose, at a time and scale chosen in advance, and watch [9].

## Scope

The theory applies to things in steady use long enough to have adapted the user or the setting around them: devices, automation, media, routines and habitual substances.

It does not recommend removing things for its own sake. Many things that become ground are good, and removal is a measurement, not a verdict. Where the removal itself would do harm, as with stopping a prescribed medicine, a routine that holds back a standing cause, or a safety system, the test is not run that way: medicine withdraws treatment only under trial conditions [7], and a maintenance routine is checked through its cause, not by stopping it.

## Counter-evidence

Removal can mislead in its own way. The first days without a substance or a tool are dominated by withdrawal, which is an effect of the removal and not a measure of the thing's value; a test cut short finds only the withdrawal. Novelty can also flatter the absence, and a person who expects to feel better without something may do so for that reason.

Not every removal reveals a hidden cost. Some tools have no adaptation around them, and a period without them shows only the loss of the help, which matches the presence effect. In the learning research, calculators show no harm to arithmetic once a skill is learned, which suggests that handing a task to a tool after the skill has formed may cost little.

Several pieces rest on small or single studies: the GPS decline on 13 people retested, the bias in present judgement on two experiments from one research group. The claim that ground blindness is general, across tools, routines, substances and media, joins findings from separate fields and has not been tested as one claim.
