My longest paper, *Narcocrasis*, runs to 5,716 words. I marked each of its 101 paragraphs by the job it does. About 2,400 words carry the argument. The rest gives background, takes side roads, repeats what was already said, or tells the reader what is coming. Read on their own, in order, the 37 paragraphs that carry it still make the whole argument.

This theory is about that part of a piece: how to find it, why readers already favour it, and what it is for.

## Definition

Every paragraph in a piece of writing does one main job. There are six:

- **Core.** It states a claim, or gives the evidence or reasoning that a claim cannot stand without.
- **Limit.** It sets the scope of a claim, states a limit, or answers an objection the reader is owed, the first time that answer is given.
- **Elaboration.** It gives background, a further example, a quotation that illustrates a point, or detail about how something works.
- **Aside.** It adds something true and interesting that the argument does not use.
- **Restatement.** It says again what an earlier paragraph already said.
- **Frame.** It introduces, previews, summarises or leads into something: an abstract, a roadmap, a section summary, a line that introduces a quotation.

The **load-bearing core** of a piece is its core and limit paragraphs. The **load share** is the fraction of its words in them. The **skeleton** is the core read in order and stated as a chain of moves, one move for each run of core paragraphs that does one thing in the argument.

The test for the core is a deletion test applied to the argument. Delete the paragraph: does the claim lose a step, a piece of evidence it needs, or a limit the reader is owed? If it does, the paragraph is load-bearing.

## Prior work

The idea that some parts of a text are central and others support them is old and well studied. What this theory adds is set out after the table.

| Rhetorical Structure Theory: nucleus and satellite [1] [2] | Linguistics | Within each relation between two spans of text, one span (the nucleus) is more central to the writer's purpose; relations include evidence, background, elaboration, restatement and summary | Applied clause by clause for coherence; evidence counts as a satellite even when an argument needs it |
| Nuclearity and importance [3] [4] | Computational linguistics | Annotators agree on nuclei; the nuclei match readers' judgements of importance and can drive summaries | A measure of a whole piece, and what the remainder is for |
| Macrostructure and macrorules [5] [7] | Discourse psychology | Readers reduce a text to its gist by deleting, generalising and constructing; the gist is what lasts | A way for a writer to see a finished piece's gist and its proportion |
| The levels effect [5] [6] | Memory research | Ideas high in a text's structure are recalled better and longer | What a writer should do with the rest |
| Summaries against full texts [8] [9] [10] | Learning research | A text cut to its main points can teach as well or better | Which parts of a given text make up its main points |
| Seductive details and coherence [11] [12] [13] | Learning research | Interesting material that does not serve the explanation lowers learning | Repetition and framing, which are relevant to the explanation |
| Signalling [14] [15] | Reading research | Headings, previews and summaries help readers of complex texts | How much of a piece they take up |
| Argument mapping [17] | Philosophy and education | An argument laid out as claims and reasons is easier to judge | Reading an existing prose piece back into its map, paragraph by paragraph |

**What this theory adds.** It moves the nucleus and satellite distinction from the clause to the paragraph, where a writer works, and gives six jobs a writer can mark by hand. It applies the deletion test to the argument, so evidence a claim needs counts as core, where Rhetorical Structure Theory would make it a satellite. It turns the marking into a measure, the load share, and shows where the rest of a piece gathers. It treats each remaining paragraph as serving a particular reader, which the signalling and skimming research supports. And it takes the core in order as the piece's skeleton, the form from which the piece, a summary, a short or a video can be rebuilt, which ties it to the [mechanism chain](/theories/the-mechanism-chain): a mechanism is held as a chain of parts and actions, and an argument as a chain of moves.

## Mechanism

A piece of writing is written for several readers at once. One reads it straight through. One reads the abstract and the conclusion. One skims. One returns months later looking for a single point. Each needs something different, and a finished piece carries all of it: the argument, the background that makes it readable, the previews and summaries that let a skimmer find their way, and the repeats that catch a reader who skipped.

Readers already treat these parts differently. They agree on which parts are central [3] [4]. Memory keeps the central ideas and lets the details go: details were forgotten about four times faster than the central propositions over three months [5]. Skimmers short of time pick up the important statements and not the minor ones [16]. A summary of the main points can teach as well as or better than the full text [8] [10], and interesting material that does not serve the explanation draws attention away from it [11] [12].

The writer, though, sees the piece as a sequence of sections, and an outline drawn from section headings follows the layout. In the narcocrasis paper, the paragraph that states the paper's own central claim sits in a 332-word section between longer ones, and an outline drawn from the headings had folded it into a neighbouring move.

Marking each paragraph by its job makes the proportions visible. The core, read in order, is the argument at its essential size. Because it is the part memory keeps, summaries keep and skimmers look for, it is also the part every other version of the piece has to carry: a summary, a short spoken version, a video. The other paragraphs can be added back for a particular reader, and each can be judged by which reader it serves.

## Evidence

Readers agree on what is central (P1). In building a large corpus annotated with rhetorical structure, three trained annotators agreed on which spans were nuclei at kappa 0.86, and on the relations between spans at 0.76 [3]. Thirteen judges rating 160 clauses from five *Scientific American* articles as very, moderately or not important gave consistent ratings for 87% of the clauses [4].

Structure finds what readers judge important (P2). Ranking those clauses by how centrally they sat in a rhetorical structure tree recovered the clauses a majority judged very important with 66% precision, against about 26% by chance; grouped into sentences, precision rose to 75 to 82% [4].

The central ideas last (P3). Kintsch and van Dijk cite earlier work in which propositions high in a text's hierarchy were recalled two to three times better than low ones. In their own recall data, the probability of reproducing a detail fell from .099 to .018 over three months, while that of a central proposition fell only from .391 to .321; summaries written at once and after three months had the same length, 75 and 72 words [5]. Meyer and McConkie found that an idea unit's position in a passage's logical hierarchy affected whether listeners recalled it [6].

Finding the core is a skill (P4). Kintsch and van Dijk set out three rules for reducing a text to its gist: deleting what nothing later depends on, generalising a list into its category, and constructing a statement the details imply [5]. Brown and Day found that students from grade five up already deleted trivial and repeated material about 90% of the time or more. Inventing a topic sentence that the text never states was rare in grade five, used on about half of suitable occasions by college students, and on 84% by experienced writing teachers [7].

The main points teach as well as the whole (P5). Students who read summaries of college textbook chapters remembered the main points better than students who read the full chapters, at 20 minutes, one week and 6 to 12 months; the summary readers also did better on inference questions and on learning related new material, and the advantage held when the textbook's main points were underlined [8]. In three experiments, a summary of how lightning forms made of five captioned pictures, about 80 words, taught as well as or better than the 600-word lesson, and adding text to the summary made it less effective [10].

Two things make a summary work (P6). Reder and Anderson later tested why. Rereading the main points at spaced intervals and leaving out the details each improved memory for the central ideas, independently [9].

Side material costs (P7). Interesting but irrelevant details in a lesson lowered recall of its main steps and problem solving [11]. A meta-analysis of 58 studies with more than 7,500 students found an overall effect of g = −0.33 [12], and an earlier one of 39 effects found medium negative effects on recall and transfer [13].

Frames help readers of complex texts (P8). A review of headings, previews, summaries and other signals found that signalled content is generally remembered better and unsignalled content is usually unaffected [14]. Headings, an overview and a summary changed what readers recalled, and how they organised it, when a text's topic structure was complex, and had no effect when it was simple [15].

Skimmers find the core themselves (P9). With under half the time needed to read a text, readers who skimmed the whole of it recognised its important statements better than readers who read half of it at normal speed (d′ 1.67 against 1.13), and no better on unimportant ones. They spent more time early in paragraphs and near the top of each page [16].

Mapping an argument helps people judge it (P10). Students taught with computer-based argument maps gained more than three times as much in critical thinking as students taught by other methods [17].

How much of a piece is load-bearing (P11), and whether a skeleton drawn from the core outdoes an outline drawn from headings (P12), rest on the two worked examples below.

## Measures

1. **Load share.** Words in core and limit paragraphs divided by all words in the body.
2. **Restatement and frame share.** Words in restatement and frame paragraphs, overall and by section.
3. **Skeleton gap.** The number of core paragraphs that sit on no move of the piece's stated outline or skeleton.
4. **Core-only reading.** Readers given only the core paragraphs, in order, and readers given the whole piece answer the same questions about its main points and the same inference questions, as in Reder and Anderson's comparisons [8].
5. **Agreement.** Two people mark the same piece; the share of words both mark as core or limit.

## Protocol

1. **Number the paragraphs and count their words.** Leave out the title, references and headings.
2. **Write the piece's claim in one sentence first.** The marking depends on it.
3. **Mark each paragraph.** Ask whether deleting it would cost the claim a step, a piece of evidence it needs, or a limit the reader is owed. If yes, mark it core or limit. If no, name its job: elaboration, aside, restatement (and the paragraph it repeats) or frame.
4. **Total the words by job and by section.**
5. **Read the core alone, in order.** If it does not hold together, a paragraph was marked wrongly, or the piece is missing a step.
6. **Write the skeleton from the core.** One move for each run of core paragraphs that does one thing. Compare it with the outline the headings give.
7. **Decide what each remaining paragraph is for.** Keep frames a complex piece needs, shorten a repeated answer to a pointer back to the paragraph that gave it, and move asides to a note or cut them.

## A four-question check

For any piece you have written or are about to rewrite:

1. Can you state its claim in one sentence?
2. Can you point to the paragraphs that carry the claim, and say what each adds?
3. For every other paragraph, can you say which reader it is for?
4. Read alone and in order, do the carrying paragraphs make the argument?

A no to the second question means the claim is not yet located in the piece. A no to the third marks a paragraph to cut. A no to the fourth means a step is missing. This check is a working tool built from the protocol above; it has not been validated.

**Worked example 1: a long paper.** [*Narcocrasis*](/papers/narcocrasis) has 101 paragraphs and 5,716 words. Marked by hand: core 34%, limits 8%, elaboration 27%, asides 4%, restatement 18%, frame 9%. Its load share is 42%: 37 paragraphs, 2,420 words. The repeats gather where a paper's conventions put them. The thesis is stated in the abstract, again with the coined names, again at the start of the third section and again in the conclusion. Eight of the ten replies to objections repeat a paragraph from the body almost one for one, 360 of that section's 482 words. The longest single paragraph, a 224-word table across five dimensions, adds no new claim, and its section carries none. The section that carries the paper's own idea, that the shifted baseline goes unseen while the drug stays vivid, is one of the shortest. Its outline, drawn from the sections, missed six load-bearing paragraphs, making four points: that central claim, the finding the paper calls its most important, the clearest statement of what the new term adds, and the contrast between its two routes to recovery.

**Worked example 2: a short essay.** [*How one word can bring back an idea*](/articles/keep-three-words-rebuild-the-rest) has 19 paragraphs and 1,339 words. Core 59%, limits 15%, elaboration 15%, restatement 7%, frame 4%: a load share of 74%. It has no abstract, roadmap, objections section or conclusion, which is where the paper's repeats gathered. Its one restatement is the numbered how-to list, which repeats the three steps given earlier and the final check. Every core paragraph sits on one of its skeleton's nine moves, and one move rests on an elaboration, the study of labels and category learning.

## Scope

The theory applies to writing that argues or explains: essays, papers, reports, briefs, lectures and the scripts of explanatory videos. Its six jobs are judged against a claim, so it needs a piece that has one.

What the finished piece keeps is the writer's choice. A paper is read by people who skim, people who read only the abstract and people who come back for one point, and frames and repeats serve them. The theory locates the part the piece rests on, and asks of every other paragraph which reader needs it.

Narrative, description and lists fit it less well. A story's events are related in time more than in support, and a descriptive passage may have no part more central than another.

## Counter-evidence

Centrality is more than one thing. Stede argues that what makes a span central in rhetorical structure comes from several independent sources, including argumentative intent, repetition, digression and syntax, so that one notion of nuclearity mixes them, and that in narrative and descriptive texts segments often cannot be ranked at all [2]. Marking paragraphs by job may hide the same mixture.

Repeats and frames do work. Part of a summary's advantage came from rereading the main points at intervals [9], which is what a restatement gives a reader. Signals helped recall and organisation in texts with complex structure, and when only some topics were signalled, the others were recalled worse [15]. A long paper stripped to its core may be harder to follow than the original, even though its argument is all there.

The summary studies used summaries written by skilled people, and short texts. Inventing a statement the text implies but never makes is a late skill [7], so a less practised reader may mark topic sentences as core and miss a claim the piece never states in one place. The learning studies mostly used science explanations and short lessons, often 6 to 12 minutes [12]; a persuasive piece may need more of its evidence in front of the reader to convince.

The agreement figures come from trained annotators working clause by clause [3] [4]. Agreement on the six jobs at the level of the paragraph has not been measured, and both worked examples were marked by one reader. A paragraph often does two jobs, and the marking records only the main one.
