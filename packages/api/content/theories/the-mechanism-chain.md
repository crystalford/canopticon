Ask someone how a toilet works and the answer usually comes out as a few words in no particular order: water pressure, tank, flush, fill. The same person uses one several times a day and could teach a child to use it. They know what it does and how to operate it. What they are missing is the order of the parts and what each one does, and they do not notice it is missing until they are asked.

This theory is about that difference, and about a way of holding a mechanism so that it can be explained.

## Definition

**Use knowledge** is knowing what a thing does and how to operate it: press the handle, the bowl empties and refills. It is learned by using the thing, and it is usually accurate. **Mechanism knowledge** is knowing how the thing produces its result: which parts act on which, in what order. A **mechanism chain** is mechanism knowledge held as an ordered list of links, one link for each part and what it does, from the starting condition to the result, with each link compressed to a short **cue word**. A **rebuild** is producing the full explanation from the cue words alone. The **break point** is the first link in a rebuild that will not come back.

The theory's claim is that use knowledge is mistaken for mechanism knowledge, that procedures escape this mistake because they are already held as ordered steps, and that a mechanism held as a chain can be rebuilt on demand, with its break point showing where understanding stops.

**What this theory adds.** It ties the fact that procedures escape the illusion to their form: a procedure is already an ordered list. It proposes holding mechanisms in that same form, as a chain of part-and-action cue words in causal order, which is also how the philosophy of science defines a mechanism and how people simulate one. And it treats the chain's first break as a measure: the point where a rebuild fails is the point where understanding stops. It also names use as the way function knowledge builds up and passes for mechanism knowledge.

## Mechanism

Using a thing teaches two kinds of knowledge, and both are accurate. One is its function: the toilet clears the bowl and refills. The other is a procedure: press the handle, wait, let go. A procedure is an ordered list of steps, and Rozenblit and Keil found that people judge their knowledge of procedures well [1]. Every use rehearses the function and the procedure again.

The mechanism is not rehearsed. Each use shows the result and hides the parts that produce it, which sit inside a tank or a slider. Yet knowing what a thing does feels like knowing how it does it, and people rate their understanding of mechanisms far above what they can explain [1] [4]. Familiarity can leave the gap in place: in drawings of a bicycle, a familiar everyday object, people often placed the chain wrongly [3]. The gap shows only when someone tries to explain the mechanism step by step, and then their rating falls [1] [2].

A mechanism has the same shape as a procedure. It is a set of parts and what each does, in order from a starting condition to a result [5]. People reason through one in that order, following the causal chain one interaction at a time [7], and events joined by causes are what memory holds best [8] [9]. A mechanism is therefore well suited to being held as a chain.

The chain works as a set of cues. Each link is compressed to a word, such as *hook* for a part and *settle* for an action, and the full explanation is rebuilt from the words. Learning the parts and their order improves understanding [10] [11] [12], and rebuilding from memory makes it last and shows whether it is there [13] [14] [15]. When a rebuild fails, it fails at a particular link, and that link is the edge of what the person understands.

## Evidence

The overestimate is real and falls when people explain (P1). In Rozenblit and Keil's studies, students rated how well they understood devices such as zippers, flush toilets and cylinder locks, wrote out how each works step by step, and rated themselves again; the ratings fell [1]. People asked to explain how a political policy would work rated their understanding lower afterwards and took more moderate positions [2].

It is specific to mechanisms (P2). The drop was large for devices and absent for procedures, and much smaller for facts and narratives [1].

Familiarity does not bring mechanism (P3). Asked to complete drawings of a bicycle, people made frequent and serious mistakes, such as drawing the chain around both wheels. Bicycle experts made fewer errors, but still made some [3].

Function passes for mechanism (P4). Rozenblit and Keil proposed that people confuse understanding of high-level function with understanding of the mechanism beneath it, and that procedures are largely spared because procedural knowledge is mostly the sequence of high-level steps [1]. Keil names the confusion of functional relations with knowledge of how subsystems work as one of two sources of the illusion [4].

A mechanism is parts and actions in order (P5). Machamer, Darden and Craver define mechanisms as "entities and activities organized such that they are productive of regular changes from start or set-up to finish or termination conditions" [5]. A framework for recognising mechanistic reasoning in students lists entities, activities, their organisation, and chaining, which is working out one stage of a mechanism from the stages before or after it [6].

People simulate a mechanism link by link (P6). Hegarty proposed that people inferring how pulley systems move simulate each pairwise interaction between components and trace a chain of causality across the system [7].

Causal chains are remembered (P7). Events in stories that had more causal connections, and events on the main causal chain, were recalled more and judged more important [8]. Causal connections, with two other measures, accounted for 31% of the variation in what readers of eight simple stories recalled [9].

Parts and order teach understanding (P8). In Mayer's review, diagrams showing a system's parts and the sequence of events, such as a car's brakes when the pedal is pressed, improved conceptual recall in all 10 tests (median 57%) and transfer problem solving in all 16 (median 64%), for learners new to the topic [10]. Teaching the names and states of the main parts before a lesson improved problem-solving transfer in 5 of 5 tests, with a median effect size of 0.85 [11] [12].

Explaining from memory teaches more (P9). Students prompted to explain each sentence of a science text to themselves outperformed students who read it twice [13]. Recalling a passage beat rereading it after two days and after a week [14]. Practising retrieval beat building concept maps on comprehension tests that required inferences [15].

Whether a mechanism held as a chain is judged as accurately as a procedure (P10), and whether the break point marks the limit of understanding (P11), have not been tested.

## Measures

1. **Rating gap.** Rated understanding of a mechanism on a 1–7 scale before and after trying to explain it, as in Rozenblit and Keil's studies [1].
2. **Links rebuilt.** The number of part-and-action links a person produces, in the correct order, against a reference chain checked with a source.
3. **Break point.** The position of the first missing or wrong link.
4. **Transfer.** Whether the person can answer questions the explanation does not state directly, such as what happens if one part fails, as in Mayer's problem-solving tests [10].

The theory expects the rating gap to shrink, and links rebuilt and transfer to rise, when a mechanism is learned as a chain and rebuilt from its cue words.

## Protocol

1. **Name the result and the starting condition.** What the thing does, and what sets it going.
2. **List the parts.** Only the ones that act.
3. **Give each part its action, in order.** From the starting condition to the result, and check the order against a reliable source.
4. **Compress each link to one cue word.** A part or an action, whichever brings the link back.
5. **Learn the parts and their actions first.** Before any detail, as pre-training does [11] [12].
6. **Rebuild from the cue words alone.** Out loud or in writing, without the source.
7. **Mark the break point and fix that link.** Reread only the link that failed, then rebuild the whole chain again on a later day.

## A four-question check

For anything you use and think you understand:

1. Can you say what it does and how to operate it?
2. Can you name the parts that make it work?
3. Can you give each part its action, in order, from start to result?
4. Can you rebuild that explanation from one word per link, without looking?

A yes to the first question and a no to any of the others is the gap this theory describes. The question where the answer turns to no shows where understanding stops. This check is a working tool built from the measures above; it has not been validated.

**Worked example: a zipper.** Each tooth has a hook on one side and a hollow on the other. The slider is a set of wedges: pulled up, its angled sides push the two rows together, and each hook settles into a hollow on the opposite row. Pulled down, a wedge at its front pries the teeth apart [18]. The chain: **hook · hollow · push · settle · pry**.

**Worked example: a flush toilet.** Pushing the handle pulls a chain that lifts the flapper. The tank's water drops into the bowl in about three seconds and fills the siphon, which pulls the bowl's contents down the drain. The flapper settles back over the opening. As the water level falls, the float falls with it and opens the fill valve; the tank and bowl refill, and the rising float shuts the valve off [19]. The chain: **lift · drop · siphon · reseat · float · refill · shut**. The everyday answer, "water pressure, tank, flush, fill", has the result and one part, and is missing the siphon, the flapper and the float.

**Worked example outside the home: a bicycle.** The pedals turn the cranks, which turn the front chainring. The chainring pulls the chain, the chain turns the sprocket on the rear wheel, and the rear wheel turns. The chain: **crank · ring · chain · sprocket · wheel**. The common drawing error, a chain running around the front wheel as well [3], is a broken link at *sprocket*.

## Scope

The theory applies to anything that works by a sequence of causes: machines and devices, processes in the body, and the working of an institution or a policy, where Fernbach and colleagues found the same overestimate [2]. A mechanism with a loop, such as the float that shuts the valve it opened, is still a chain, with the last link feeding back to an earlier one.

It does not claim that everyone needs mechanism knowledge of everything. People rely on others who understand what they do not, and that is often the efficient choice [4] [16]. The theory is about knowing where one's understanding stops, and about holding a mechanism when one does want to understand it.

## Counter-evidence

Shallow understanding usually works. Keil argues that coarse understanding is efficient, lets detail be filled in when it is needed, and leans on other people and the environment [4]. People also rate their own understanding higher when they are told that experts understand something, even when they do not have that understanding themselves [16]. The illusion therefore has a social source that holding a chain does not touch.

The procedure comparison may have other explanations. Rozenblit and Keil gave further reasons for the illusion: mistaking visible parts for knowledge held in the head, the lack of a clear end point for an explanation, and how rarely people explain anything [1]. Procedures may be spared for those reasons as much as for being ordered lists, in which case giving a mechanism the form of a list would help less than this theory expects (P10).

A chain of words can create its own illusion. Knowing the five cue words for a zipper can feel like knowing how a zipper works. Building a structure without retrieving it gains less than retrieval does [15], so the chain helps only if it is rebuilt from memory and checked against a source.

The benefits of parts-and-steps instruction were found for learners new to a topic; students who already had their own models of a system gained nothing [10]. Experts in bicycles still made errors [3]. A chain can be learned wrong, and the check against a source is part of the method for that reason.
