Someone plans to go to the gym three times a week. On Wednesday they do a yoga video at home instead, and it feels like cheating — the gym session is still "owed". By Friday they have skipped both. The usual advice is to be more specific and more disciplined: gym, Monday-Wednesday-Friday, 45 minutes. This article argues the research points the other way. Being specific helps, but only about the moment you start. Being rigid about the workout itself produces less exercise, and the effect lasts after the plan ends.

## Paying people to exercise at a fixed time worked worse than paying them to exercise any time

The clearest test paid 2,508 Google employees to go to the gym. Half were paid only for visits in the same two-hour window each day; half were paid for visits at any time. The flexible group went more. In the researchers' words, “<q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Key findings">Routine incentives generated fewer gym visits than flexible incentives, both during our intervention and after incentives were removed. Even among subgroups that were experimentally induced to exercise at similar rates during our intervention, recipients of routine incentives exhibited a larger decrease in exercise after the intervention than recipients of flexible incentives.</q>” The second sentence is the striking one: even people who exercised just as much under the rigid plan lost more of the habit once the payments stopped.

> **Objection: Surely some routines should be rigid**
>
> Some should, and the study says which: <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Key findings">routinization only wins when the best opportunity is stable day-to-day (their example: flossing before bed); in variable schedules, rigidity backfires by crowding out better out-of-window chances.</q> Flossing has one good moment every day. Exercise usually does not — and a plan that rules out every other moment rules out the days when the set one falls through.

The habit research and the gym experiment fit together once the trigger and the behaviour are separated. A stable cue helps automaticity; a restricted behaviour does not. The research's reconciliation: <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Details">stabilize the cue/trigger, but don't rigidly restrict the acceptable behavior.</q>

## Being specific helps — about the start, not the workout

The best-tested planning tool is the if-then plan: "if it is 7 a.m., then I put on my shoes". Across studies, <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Key findings">findings from 94 independent tests showed that implementation intentions had a positive effect of medium-to-large magnitude (d = .65) on goal attainment</q>. What makes them work is the trigger, which hands the decision to start over to a cue. Pinning down the workout is a different thing, and it can hurt: <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Key findings">Specificity of the cue and first action ("if it's 7am, then I put on shoes and walk out the door") helps; over-specifying the content/target ("must be gym, must be 45 min") can backfire by creating an all-or-nothing failure condition and by inviting "progress"-based licensing.</q>

Even the reason behind the goal works better left broad. <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Key findings">one experimental study found an abstract construal of exercise ("why I move") produced more physical activity a week later than a concrete construal ("the procedures of my workout").</q>

The gap these plans are trying to close is large. <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Details">medium-to-large changes in intention produce only small-to-medium changes in behavior (experimental r+ ≈ 0.08–0.18 vs. correlational 0.40–0.48)</q>. The if-then plan works by <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Details">delegation of action initiation to an environmental cue, making the response quasi-automatic and bypassing the need for in-the-moment executive control</q>, which is the capacity ADHD weakens most. The evidence in ADHD is direct, and one weight study found these plans <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Details">specifically outperformed generic tips among poorer planners</q>

## The yoga counts against the gym only if you treat it as payment

Why does the yoga feel like cheating? Research on goals finds that the same action can be read two ways, and the reading decides what happens next: <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Key findings">actions construed as progress → substitution/balancing (the substitute discharges the obligation, motivation to do the original drops); actions construed as commitment → reinforcement (similar actions build on each other).</q> Read as a payment towards a gym quota, the yoga settles the debt and the next workout gets less likely. Read as evidence of being someone who moves, it makes the next one more likely.

The nagging comes from the plan that was never let go of. <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Key findings">A repeatedly-unmet gym intention that is never formally released keeps the gym goal active and competing with the substitute, consuming regulatory resources and producing the persistent low-grade "should" signal.</q>

> **Objection: The research on "licensing" yourself has replication problems**
>
> It does, and the report says so: <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Caveats">The moral-licensing literature has replication problems.</q> But the claim here is narrower: <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Caveats">the progress-vs-commitment construal framework is better supported than the strong "one good deed licenses one bad deed" claim.</q>

The nagging has a mechanism of its own, called goal shielding: an active goal suppresses competing ones, and <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Details">suppression is strongest for alternatives sharing the same superordinate purpose (i.e., other exercise)</q>. Holding it too long has a cost: <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Details">Prolonged shielding also carries a cost: it can erode long-term commitment to the neglected goal.</q> The research offers two ways out: <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Details">subordinate both gym and yoga to one abstract identity goal so they reinforce rather than compete, or (b) formally disengage from the gym-specific target</q>

## Locking yourself in helps some people and costs others

The opposite strategy, binding the future self, has real support. Pairing workouts with a treat <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Details">raised gym visits 51% (full treatment) / 29% (encouragement only) versus control</q>, and teaching the trick <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Details">kept people 10–14% more likely to work out 17 weeks later</q>, though <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Details">effects decayed after routine-disrupting holidays</q>.

Money on the line is riskier. Real-money contracts for health <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Details">show high forfeiture (in one HIV-testing field study a majority who enrolled lost their deposit)</q>, and the rigid kind is harder on a tired brain: <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Details">irrevocable precommitment recruits more cognitive-conflict resources (ERP evidence) than revocable precommitment — a liability under ADHD executive load.</q>

## The hard part is starting, and starting runs on a different system

<q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Theme 4">Initiating movement and executing movement are neurally distinct.</q> Deciding to start is a cost-and-benefit judgement, and the brain chemistry behind it is about wanting to act, not enjoying the act: <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Theme 4">Dopamine mediates "wanting"/behavioral activation and effort exertion, not hedonic "liking" — dopamine depletion in animals collapses willingness to work for reward while leaving enjoyment intact.</q> That describes a familiar experience exactly: <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Theme 4">This maps onto the ADHD phenomenology: wanting to have exercised, even enjoying it once underway, but being unable to mobilize the start.</q>

Once started, the problem largely solves itself: <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Theme 4">Once movement starts, the profile flips: acute exercise raises dopamine and norepinephrine, producing stimulant-like effects on attention and inhibitory control</q>. ADHD medication works on the same gap; one study found that <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Key findings">methylphenidate boosts willingness to expend cognitive effort by altering the benefit-to-cost ratio of cognitive work.</q> A rigid workout rule makes the start more expensive — it has to be the gym, it has to be 45 minutes — at the exact point where starting is already the problem.

> **Objection: Most of this was not tested on people with ADHD**
>
> True. <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Caveats">ADHD-specific adherence RCTs are sparse and underpowered.</q> <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Caveats">Much of the direct experimental evidence on rigid-vs-flexible, temptation bundling, and construal comes from general (non-ADHD) populations; the ADHD mechanism (dopamine, delay/effort discounting, executive dysfunction) makes extrapolation reasonable but not certain.</q> The flexible-beats-rigid result holds for people in general; the argument that it matters more with ADHD rests on the mechanism, not on a trial.

Two features of ADHD make the start steeper. Future rewards lose value faster: <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Details">future rewards (fitness) are steeply devalued, so the distal payoff cannot motivate the proximal effort</q>. And the stimulant effect is uneven: <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Details">methylphenidate shifts the benefit-to-cost ratio of effortful work by modulating striatal dopamine, and helps most those with lower baseline dopamine synthesis capacity</q>

Exercise itself is not a perfect substitute for medication. Its effects on thinking are <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Details">often larger in ADHD than controls</q>, but <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Details">catecholamine increases after acute exercise were smaller in ADHD boys than controls in one study, so the medication analogy is imperfect</q>. The practical consequence is the same either way: <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Details">friction removal beats motivation enhancement.</q> The research's examples are mundane on purpose: <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Details">lay out clothes, pre-pack the bag, gym on the commute, "just 5 minutes/one song" starts, body doubling</q>

The research also cautions that the planning effects shrink when behaviour is measured rather than reported: <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Caveats">implementation-intention effects are smaller for objectively-measured than self-reported behavior</q>. And: <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Caveats">Individual variation is large.</q> Its own advice is to treat the answer partly as a personal experiment.

## What this explains

It explains why the strictest plans fail first: an all-or-nothing rule turns every missed session into a broken plan. It explains the guilt over a substitute workout: the old target was never retired. And it explains the most effective result in the largest exercise study yet run, which tested 54 ideas on 61,293 gym members: <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Key findings">45% of these interventions significantly increased weekly gym visits by 9% to 27%; the top-performing intervention offered microrewards for returning to the gym after a missed workout.</q> The winner paid almost nothing — <q doc="narrow-the-goal-or-broaden-the-substitutes-the-neurobiology-and-behavi" section="Key findings">That top program awarded ~125 bonus points (≈$0.09) for bouncing back after a miss, producing roughly 0.40 more weekly visits (a 27% increase) versus placebo control — a "get back on track" design, the opposite of an all-or-nothing rule.</q>

Taken together, the research describes a plan with two parts that are usually confused. The start should be fixed, cheap and automatic: a time, a cue, shoes by the door. The workout should be open, so that any movement counts and a missed day is something to come back from, not something that breaks the plan. Strictness belongs at the start; flexibility belongs everywhere after it.
