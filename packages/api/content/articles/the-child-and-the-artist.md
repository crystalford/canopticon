In the story, the Emperor walks out in new clothes that do not exist, and the court admires them. McLuhan read the story in an essay dated November 1965: "When the Emperor appeared in his new clothes, his courtiers did not see his nudity, they saw his old clothes." Then he named who could see: "Only the small child and the artist have that immediacy of approach that permits perception of the environmental." [1]

The claim sits in a passage about why people cannot see the world they live in. "When an environment is new, we perceive the old one for the first time." He credited Bertrand Russell with an image for it: "if the bath water got only half a degree warmer every hour, we would never know when to scream." And he gave the artist a job: "The artist provides us with anti-environments that enable us to see the environment. Such anti-environmental means of perception must constantly be renewed in order to be efficacious." [1]

Sixty years on, each part of that can be checked. The child and the artist both see what others miss, but the research shows they do it in different ways, and the difference matters for anyone who wants to see their own surroundings.

## The child has not yet learned what to ignore

Adults are good at attention. Told what to watch, they watch it, and they let the rest go. That skill has a cost, which a study of four- and five-year-olds made visible. Children and adults watched sets of shapes, some of which they were cued to attend to, and then judged whether anything had changed. The adults did better on the shapes they were told to watch. The children did better on the shapes nobody had pointed out. In a second task, after searching a display, the children remembered more of the features that had nothing to do with the search [2].

The authors called it a rare developmental reversal. The same pattern turns up in learning. Shown evidence for an unusual rule about cause and effect, younger learners picked it up more readily than older ones, and the researchers suggest two reasons: knowledge makes people less open to new ideas, and young minds are more exploratory [3] [4].

That is McLuhan's "immediacy of approach" in measured form. The child sees the environment because the child has not yet adapted to it. Nothing has become background, because nothing has been there long enough. It is not a skill and it does not last. The same children who noticed the uncued shapes missed some of the cued ones, and they grow into adults who filter.

## The artist has a method

It is natural to assume artists see the way children do: that years of practice have given them a fresher eye. One careful test suggests otherwise. Artists and non-artists judged the size of objects on a perspective grid and the brightness of surfaces in shadow, under instructions to ignore the surrounding scene. The artists were as fooled by the context as everyone else, and they took longer to answer. The authors concluded, following the art historian Ernst Gombrich, that artists have no special power to see past the brain's corrections. Their advantage is in what they do: once a drawing is under way, they compare it with the scene and adjust it until the two match [5].

Other studies find a real perceptual advantage, and say where it comes from. Artists outperformed non-artists on perception tasks as well as drawing tasks, and the advantage was "closely linked to the activity of drawing"; artists used analytic procedures "qualitatively similar to those of novices," only more proficiently [6]. In another study, artists made smaller size-constancy errors and were better at selecting which lines to draw, and the authors placed the advantage in visual attention rather than in early perception [7].

Eye-tracking shows the difference in how artists look. Untrained viewers spent their time on faces and objects; artists spent more of it on the structure of the picture. On a second viewing, untrained viewers settled into fewer, longer fixations, as people do with something already seen. The artists did the opposite [8].

So the artist's "immediacy" is not the child's. The child has not yet adapted. The artist adapts like everyone else, and works against it with a procedure: make something, hold it against the world, and look at where the two disagree. The drawing is a second version of the scene, and the difference between the two versions is what the artist sees.

## Why the anti-environment wears out

McLuhan's condition, that anti-environments "must constantly be renewed," is a claim about habituation, and it has been measured.

Viktor Shklovsky made the argument in 1917. "Habitualization devours works, clothes, furniture, one's wife, and the fear of war," he wrote, and art exists "to make one feel things, to make the stone stony" [9]. His word for what art does is usually translated as defamiliarization. In experiments with readers, passages that departed from ordinary language were read more slowly, rated more striking and rated as more moving, and the effect held regardless of the reader's literary training [10].

But a device works only while it is unfamiliar, and familiarity comes with use. The psychologist Colin Martindale built a theory of art history on this: habituation pushes each artist in a style to raise the impact of their work over the last, until the style can go no further and a new one begins. He tested it on Italian paintings from the fourteenth to the eighteenth century, rated by people who knew nothing of the theory, and the ratings followed the predicted course [11]. He reported the same pattern in centuries of English poetry [12].

This is the anti-environment wearing out. A painting that once made the world strange becomes part of the world; a style that once broke habits becomes one. The artist has to keep changing the instrument to keep it reading anything.

## Art as an independent reading

Ground blindness, one of the theories on this site, describes what McLuhan described: what is in steady use stops being seen, and only its absence shows what it was doing. Its remedy is a planned removal, going without the thing for long enough to feel what it does. *False Quiet* adds that every such remedy takes its reading from somewhere the environment cannot reach.

The artist offers a second way to take that reading. Removal shows the environment by taking it away. A made thing shows it by setting a second version beside it. A drawing of a street, a description of a day, a recording of a conversation played back: each is a copy close enough to compare and different enough that the comparison shows something. McLuhan's examples of new environments fit the same pattern from the other side. When railways and machines remade agrarian life, "Nature became a work of art. The Romantic movement was born." When the electric circuit surrounded the machine, "the machine itself became a work of art." [1] The old environment, held against the new one, is how the new one first becomes visible.

On this account a society's art is its standing instrument for reading an environment its members have adapted to, and the instrument is renewed because it, too, is adapted to.

## Borrowing the method

The child's way cannot be borrowed by an adult. The artist's can, because it is a procedure rather than a gift.

Make something of the thing you want to see, by your own hand: draw the route instead of following it, write down how the day went before looking at the calendar, describe what a tool does for you before switching it off. Then hold what you made against the thing, and look at where they disagree. The disagreement is the reading.

Two cautions follow from the research. First, the making matters. The artists' advantage came from comparing their own drawing with the scene [5] [6]. A picture produced for someone, by a camera or by a model from a prompt, gives them nothing of their own to compare; it is another part of the environment. Second, the method wears out. A comparison done the same way every time becomes routine, as Martindale's painters found. When it stops showing anything, change the medium: draw what you used to write, record what you used to draw.

McLuhan thought the need for this was growing. "In an age of accelerated change, the need to perceive the environment becomes urgent. Acceleration also makes such perception of the environment more possible." [1] Change is faster now than when he wrote, and the means of making a second version of anything are closer to hand than they have ever been. Whether they are used to see the environment or only to add to it depends on who does the making.

## Where this stops

The artist studies are small: nine artists in the eye-tracking study, and small groups in the others [5] [8]. They measure visual art, mostly drawing; whether writers or musicians gain the same kind of perception has been studied far less. Martindale's measures are ratings by untrained viewers, which shows that people perceive the changes but leaves room to argue about their cause. The children's advantage is real but narrow, measured in laboratory tasks with shapes. And McLuhan's complaint that psychologists had "not even referred" to our inability to perceive the environment no longer holds: habituation is among the best-studied effects in the field. What remains unstudied is his larger claim, that a culture's art is how it reads its own surroundings.
