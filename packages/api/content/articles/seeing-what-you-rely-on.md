Most people who drink coffee every morning would say it makes them sharper. The research on this is unusually clear, and it says something else. In habitual drinkers, much of what feels like a boost is the end of a small withdrawal that began overnight. A large experiment found that regular drinkers who went without caffeine were sleepier and slower by the afternoon, and that caffeine brought them back; people who rarely drank it gained little from it, apart from feeling less sleepy, and became more jittery [1]. A review of the field concluded that the only studies able to show caffeine's real effect are long-term withdrawal studies, and that these find little benefit from long-term use over going without [2].

A person cannot find this out by drinking coffee. The comparison that answers the question, life after a stretch without it, is one they have not made in years.

## What experiments find when they take things away

A few experiments have done exactly that, and their results are hard to get any other way.

In one, researchers paid people to deactivate Facebook for the four weeks before the 2018 US midterm elections. The group that went without spent less time online and more time with family and friends, knew less about the news, became less politically polarised, and reported higher well-being. They also used Facebook less after the experiment ended, and they valued it less: what they said it was worth dropped after a month without it [3]. The authors note that this suggests the usual ways of measuring a service's value overstate it.

In another, nearly a thousand high-school students had GPT-4 to help with maths practice. While they had it, their practice grades rose by 48%. When it was taken away for the exam, those who had used it to get answers scored 17% below students who never had it [4]. The effect of having the tool and the effect of losing it pointed in opposite directions. Anyone who had looked only at practice grades would have concluded the opposite of what the exam showed.

A third followed drivers' use of GPS. People with more years of GPS use had worse spatial memory when they had to navigate without it, and in a small group retested three years later, more GPS use went with a steeper decline [5].

## Why it cannot be seen from inside

Three ordinary processes hide the effect of what is always there.

The first is habituation. Responses to a steady, unchanging stimulus fade [6]. What is there every day stops drawing attention.

The second is adaptation. A body or a habit adjusts around what it relies on. With caffeine, the adjustment is a baseline that sits lower without it, so the drink comes to restore what it has itself moved; the same pattern, much stronger, is how dependence on drugs develops [7]. Skills adjust too. In a classic paper on automated control rooms, Lisanne Bainbridge pointed out that "physical skills deteriorate when they are not used", so an experienced operator who has spent years monitoring an automated system may now be an inexperienced one at the moment the system fails [8].

The third is timing. Asked what something is worth while having it, a person compares it with an absence they no longer remember. A second experiment, by two of the same researchers, found that users underestimated how much of their own use was habit [9].

None of this is new in its parts. Marshall McLuhan called the unnoticed environment a medium creates its ground, as against the figure people attend to. The philosopher Martin Heidegger observed that a hammer in use is barely noticed and becomes conspicuous only when it breaks. The sociologist Susan Leigh Star wrote that infrastructure is invisible until it fails [10]. What the experiments add is measurement, and the finding that the hidden effect can run the other way.

## The case for leaving things alone

The strongest objection is old. G. K. Chesterton described a fence across a road and a reformer who wants it removed because he sees no use for it; the wiser course, Chesterton said, is to find out why it was put there before taking it down. Many things that have become ground are there for good reasons, and removing them can do harm.

The answer is in how the removal is done. A planned removal opens the fence like a gate, for a while. It is brief, chosen for a time when it costs little, and the thing is put back if it turns out to matter. Fields that cannot afford guesswork already work this way. Drug trials test long-term treatments by withdrawing them from patients who respond and comparing them with patients who continue, a design suited to treatments that are taken indefinitely [11]. Software engineers at large companies switch off parts of a working system on purpose, at a chosen time, to find dependencies nobody knew were there [12].

There are things not to test this way. A prescribed medicine is stopped only with the prescriber. A routine that holds back a standing problem, like cleaning a gap between two teeth, is checked by asking whether the cause is still there; stopping it to see means waiting for the problem to return. And the first days without a substance or a habit mostly measure withdrawal: caffeine withdrawal, for example, typically begins 12 to 24 hours after the last dose and lasts two to nine days [13]. A test cut short finds only the withdrawal, and a test run while expecting to feel better can find that too.

## What it means

The question "is this good for me?" is usually asked about things a person already uses every day, and it is asked in the one condition where it cannot be answered. The effects that matter, and everything that has adjusted around the thing, are part of the background. The experiments above give the alternative: a set period without it, long enough to pass any withdrawal, with the expected result written down beforehand. Most people find out what they rely on when a phone dies or a service goes down. Doing it on purpose, at a time of one's choosing, is the cheaper way to find out.
