Picture a studio apartment with a bed, a desk and a dresser. The desk is also where meals are eaten. The dresser is where the mail goes, and the notes for tomorrow, and then a bag on top of the notes. A project started on the desk has to be packed away before dinner. The person living there may conclude that they are disorganised, or that something is wrong with their attention. Some of the problem may be the room.

## Space does part of the thinking

The cognitive scientist David Kirsh studied how people arrange things while they work: cooks, assemblers, packers, people doing jigsaw puzzles. He concluded that the arrangement of objects around us "is an integral part of the way we think, plan, and behave" [1]. He sorted its uses into three kinds. Arrangements simplify choice, as when a kitchen is divided into stations and each one makes only a few actions available. They simplify perception, as when a puzzler sorts pieces by colour into piles. And they save mental work, as when a short-order cook leaves a knife beside the next ingredient to mark the place in the plan [1].

Office desks work the same way. In interviews with office workers in the early 1980s, Thomas Malone found that one of the most important jobs of a desk's layout was to remind its owner of things to do, more than to help them find things [2]. Unfiled piles, sitting where they would be seen, did much of that reminding.

A home with several rooms gives this a large scale. The kitchen holds what cooking needs. A spare table can hold a half-finished project for a week. A hallway shelf holds what has to go out tomorrow. Each place keeps its materials and its reminders, and walking into it brings the right ones into view.

## One room puts everything on the same surfaces

In a single room, every activity competes for the same few surfaces. A reminder placed on the dresser is covered by the next thing that needs a place. Kirsh noted the cost of keeping too much in a workspace: the more that is kept, the more cluttered the space, and "the very opportunities that might be found go unnoticed" [1]. In a small room the clutter comes from shortage as much as from excess.

The brain pays for this directly. Objects in view compete for representation in the visual cortex, and attention has to resolve the competition. Brain imaging shows that attention has the most work to do when the scene has not already been sorted into separate, grouped objects [3]. A sorted space does some of that work for the viewer. A crowded one leaves it to attention.

How people experience their homes tracks this. In a study of 60 dual-income couples who gave recorded tours of their homes, wives who described their homes in terms of clutter and unfinished projects had flatter daily cortisol curves, a pattern linked with poorer health, and their depressed mood rose over the day; wives who described their homes as restful showed the opposite [4]. A survey of nearly 1,400 adults with clutter problems found that clutter weakened their sense of home [5].

Rooms also mark the edges of activities. Experiments from 2006 onward found that people forgot details of a task more readily after walking through a doorway into a new room, which the researchers explained as the mind closing one episode and opening another [6]. Later attempts to repeat the result have been mixed, and a 2021 series of four experiments found no reliable effect [7]. If the effect is real, a single room offers no such boundaries: work, meals and rest happen in the same place, with nothing to mark where one ends.

## It can look like a trait

When someone struggles to keep track of tasks, the usual explanation is located in the person. Sometimes that is right. ADHD is one of the most heritable psychiatric conditions, with heritability estimated at about 74% [8]. But how strongly its symptoms show depends on the setting. In interviews with 125 young adults who had been diagnosed with ADHD as children, more than half described their symptoms as depending on context; they focused better in work that was busy, interesting, or "physically demanding or hands-on" [9]. A plumber in the study said that if he could do something with his hands, he could focus on it "ten times better" [9].

The treatments for adults point the same way. In a well-known trial of cognitive behavioural therapy for adults with ADHD, the first module trained people to organise and plan with a calendar and task list system: an external structure that holds what memory would otherwise have to hold [10]. That is the job Kirsh and Malone found rooms and desks doing. Someone with fewer places to put things has less of that structure to begin with.

The pandemic gave a large-scale glimpse. During Italy's 2020 lockdown, when homes became the place where people slept, ate, worked and exercised, a survey of 8,177 university students in Milan found that living in an apartment under 60 square metres was associated with a higher risk of moderate to severe depressive symptoms, and those whose work from home had worsened were more than four times as likely to report depression [11].

## A home decides which projects can start

Space also decides what a person can take up. Philosophers of perception use the word affordance for a possibility for action that the surroundings offer, and argue that the range of affordances people perceive is wide and depends on their skills [12]. A yard affords a garden. A garage affords repairs and building. A spare room affords a long project that can be left out between sessions. In a single room most of these are unavailable, however willing the person.

The activities that need space are often good for people. A meta-analysis of 22 studies found that gardening improved a range of health outcomes, from depression and anxiety to life satisfaction and sense of community [13]. In a national survey of parents, children with ADHD showed fewer symptoms after activities in green outdoor settings than after the same activities indoors or in built outdoor settings [14]. When someone moves from a single room to a house with a yard and spare rooms, gardening, cooking, building or study can follow. The change in what they do can come from the change in what the place offers.

## Why it goes unnoticed

A home is the background to everything else, and a background is hard to see while it stays the same. Its effects show when it changes: a move to a larger place where new habits appear, or a move back to a single room where they stop. This publication's theory of [ground blindness](/theories/ground-blindness) describes the pattern: what surrounds you drops out of notice while it is there, and removing or changing it shows what it was doing.

That has a practical side. Someone in a small space can recreate some of what rooms do: a fixed surface or container for each activity, reminders kept where nothing else can be set on them, a clear end to each task. And the projects that need space can be taken to places that have it: a community garden, a workshop, a library, a shared studio.

## Where this stops

Most of the evidence here is correlational. The cortisol study had 60 couples, the clutter survey drew on people who had sought help from professional organisers, the ADHD interviews are self-reports, and the green-space study relied on parents' ratings in a nonprobability sample. The doorway effect has not held up reliably. The lockdown survey measured depressive symptoms in students, not attention. None of this shows that a small home causes ADHD. The argument is narrower: rooms and surfaces carry part of the work of organising a life, and when they are missing the work falls on attention, where it can be mistaken for a fault in the person.
