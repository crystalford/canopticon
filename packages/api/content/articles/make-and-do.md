I could never keep *poiesis* and *praxis* straight. Both are Greek, both come up in philosophy and design writing, and each arrives with paragraphs of context. What finally held was reducing them to one English word each. *Poiesis* is making. *Praxis* is doing. And the two together, make and do, are a phrase everyone already knows: *make do*.

Once the pair had snapped into that phrase, I stopped forgetting which was which. This essay is about why that works, what it costs, and how to do it on purpose.

## Two Greek words, two English handles

The distinction comes from Aristotle. In the *Nicomachean Ethics* he says that "making and acting are different," and gives the reason: "while making has an end other than itself, action cannot; for good action itself is its end" [1]. A carpenter makes a chair, and the chair is the point. Acting well, being fair or brave, is its own point; nothing is left over when it is done.

Those two sentences are the definition. *Make* and *do* are the handles. A handle does not replace the definition. It gives the definition something to hang on.

## Why a familiar word holds

The best-known experiment on this is the keyword method for learning foreign words. Learners linked each Russian word to an English word that sounded like part of it, then pictured that English word together with the Russian word's meaning. On the main test, learners using the method got 72% right, against 46% for learners left to study their own way [2].

A broader finding sits underneath it. In a series of ten experiments, words that people had processed for meaning, by deciding whether they fit a sentence or a category, were remembered better than words processed only for their sound or their typeface [3]. A familiar word brings its meaning with it, so linking a new term to one gives the new term something deep to attach to.

## Why making the link yourself matters

The handle worked partly because I found it. People remember information they generate better than the same information read from a page. The original experiments showed this across five studies and many kinds of test [4], and a meta-analysis of 86 studies later put the benefit at about 0.4 of a standard deviation, almost half a standard deviation over reading [5].

So a handle someone else supplies helps, and a handle you work out for yourself helps more. The effort of finding the word is part of what stores it.

## When the handles fuse into a phrase

*Make* and *do* would have worked as two separate handles. What made them stick was that they fuse. *Make do* is an existing English phrase, meaning "to get along or manage with the means at hand" [6]. The phrase holds the pair together, so remembering one brings back the other.

The phrase also carries an idea of its own. Making do is what making and doing look like when they happen together: building something out of what is to hand, as you go. That idea already has names. The historian Michel de Certeau gave a chapter of *The Practice of Everyday Life* the title "Making do: uses and tactics," about the ways ordinary people improvise within systems they did not design [7]. The anthropologist Claude Lévi-Strauss called the same habit of mind bricolage: working with whatever materials are at hand [8].

A good fused handle therefore does two jobs. It holds the definitions, and it points to a further idea that the definitions alone would not have suggested.

## What a handle costs

Handles have limits. In four experiments on the keyword method, learners who used it learned faster, but after a delay they had forgotten more than learners who had rehearsed by rote; the researchers suspected that the keywords' own everyday associations got in the way of recall [9]. A handle with nothing behind it is a weak memory.

A handle can also flatten. *Poiesis* is the root of *poetry*, and Aristotle's *praxis* carries a moral sense that *do* does not. Someone who keeps only the handles has a simpler idea than the one in the text.

Both costs point to the same practice: learn the handle and the definition together, and use the handle to call the definition back.

## How to make one

1. **Read the definition once in full.** The handle comes after, never instead.
2. **Find the one familiar word each term comes down to.** If it takes two words, the term may hold two ideas.
3. **Look for a pair that fuses.** Terms that come in pairs often reduce to two words that form a phrase you already know.
4. **Check what the phrase means on its own.** If its everyday meaning fits the idea, it will hold; if it pulls against it, choose another.
5. **Say the handle, then the definition, until the one brings back the other.**

## Where this stops

The research here is on vocabulary and word lists, mostly with students, and none of it tests handles for abstract concepts directly. The keyword studies used word pairs learned in a session, and their long-term results conflict. The make-and-do example is one person's experience with one pair of terms. The argument is that the method has known parts: a familiar word gives a new term meaning to attach to, finding the link yourself stores it better, and a fused phrase can hold a pair together and suggest more than either word does.
