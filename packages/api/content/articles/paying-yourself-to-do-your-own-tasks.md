A delivery app can get someone off the sofa and across a city for a modest fee, while their own short list of small jobs sits untouched. The obvious response is to build the dispatcher for yourself and put money behind the orders. The research on that idea separates two ways of doing it, and treats them very differently.

## What a delivery order bundles that a to-do list does not

The comparison is more exact than it first sounds. The research breaks a delivery order into its parts: <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="Key findings">A DoorDash order bundles at least six things: an external queue that decides what's next; one order at a time; a concrete, bounded action with an ETA; a trigger (the ping); immediate feedback; real money that a third party pays you and that you need.</q> Most of that list is design, not money: <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="Key findings">The first five are all things you can build.</q> The last is the hard part: <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="Key findings">The sixth is different in kind.</q>

## Paying people does work, while you are paying

The trials are consistent on the basic effect. A meta-analysis of 34 randomised trials found incentives moved behaviour with <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="TL;DR">the odds ratio was 1.53 up to 18 months</q>, though <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="TL;DR">effects mostly faded within about 3 months of removal</q>. A later review describes them the same way — <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="Key findings">modest, often short-lived</q>. One design detail beats the rest: framing the money as something you already hold and could lose. <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="Key findings">only the loss arm significantly beat control</q>.

The broader evidence is mixed in instructive ways. Rewards can undermine motivation, but mainly for work people already enjoy: <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="Key findings">Rewards crowd out intrinsic motivation — strong for interesting tasks, weak for boring ones (Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999).</q> Paying very little can be worse than paying nothing: <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="Key findings">Small pay can backfire — moderate (Gneezy & Rustichini 2000).</q> And honesty slips when people grade themselves: <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="Key findings">Self-verification invites small cheating — moderate to strong (Mazar, Amir & Ariely 2008; the 2018 RRR did not replicate the moral-reminder effect).</q> Pairing a chore with a treat helps for a while: <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="Key findings">Temptation bundling helps — moderate, decays (Milkman 2014).</q>

## Paying yourself a wage loses most of it

The weak version is the one where you fund a wage out of your own pocket and pay it to yourself per task. The money is not income — <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="Key findings">Mental accounting predicts people treat that money as "my money moving between pockets", not as earnings.</q> Nor does anyone check the work. The research states the loss in one line: <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="Key findings">Self-verified pennies remove all three levers that work in the trials: outside money, objective verification, meaningful amounts.</q> But it names the condition under which your own money can still carry weight: <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="Key findings">That is why the money layer needs either loss framing (you lose something you already hold) or outside verification to carry any weight.</q>

## Earning a subscription back is a different mechanism

A subscription you earn back by doing the work meets that condition. The fee has already left your pocket; doing the tasks recovers some of it, and skipping them leaves it with someone else. That is a stake, not a wage — and the research's own conclusion is that it is the strongest version of the idea: <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="Summary">The version most likely to work is a dispatcher that rewards you with loss-framed credits you earn back against your own subscription.</q> The design it sketches is deliberately modest: <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="Details">earn-back subscription credit (loss-framed, bounded, no money leaves the app)</q>.

Where it draws a line is at the step of pooling what other people lose and paying it out to whoever finishes: <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="TL;DR">Avoid user-funded forfeit pools: they collide with Google Play's real-money staking ban, Canada's payment-services rules and the Criminal Code's lottery provisions.</q> That is a legal constraint on one way of distributing the money, not evidence that the incentive fails. The research's verdict on the business is conditional rather than closed — it <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="Synthesis">probably works as a subscription dispatcher with rebate credits; likely fails as a cash-payout platform without a B2B payer.</q>

Paying out small sums in real money also runs into arithmetic. Payment processors charge per transfer, and at the scale of a few cents per task the fees swamp the payment: <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="Key findings">Paying a 5¢ commission would cost roughly 5–12× the commission.</q> A credit against a fee already paid avoids that cost entirely, which is part of why the research prefers it.

## Verification is still the hinge

Whichever way the money moves, someone has to decide a task was done. The clearest cautionary case is a commitment app that charged people for sessions they had actually completed. It <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="TL;DR">shut down in July 2017, and an FTC settlement required it to refund $948,788 to users it had charged even when they met their goals</q>. The diagnosis is not that the idea was wrong — <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="TL;DR">Its problems were verification and billing, not the core idea.</q> Who referees matters at the margin: self-refereed commitments succeeded <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="Key findings">68.7% success self-referee vs 74.2% friend-referee</q>. And the appetite for real stakes is small — <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="Key findings">32% of the commitment contracts included stakes</q>.

## What has been tried, and the part nobody has built

The neighbouring products cover pieces of the idea. Commitment apps take a stake and keep it if you fail; Beeminder was, in 2017, <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="Key findings">still tiny ($22k/mo revenue with 3.5 FTEs)</q>. A newer one reviews proof with AI and people and <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="Key findings">claims 94% completion, unaudited</q>. Calendar schedulers dispatch work too, but by time rather than place: <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="Key findings">Motion, Reclaim, Sunsama (dispatch by calendar time, not context)</q>.

What none of them does is the delivery app's core loop. The research found no product that <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="Key findings">turns brain-dumps into single, context-triggered "orders", dispatches them one at a time from a quota, requires a reason when you decline, and re-prices the work dynamically.</q> It singles out one by-product as the most valuable: <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="Key findings">Of all the features, the decline-reason data is probably the most valuable and least copied.</q> The failures it catalogues share a pattern: <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="Key findings">verification is the business; very few users opt into losing money (32% of stickK contracts; 13.7% in Halpern et al.); micro-cash is eaten by fees; "earn-money" apps carry a store-policy stigma.</q>

## For ADHD, frequency beats size

The ADHD literature supports the dispatch rhythm more than the payment. Reinforcement helps — <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="Key findings">reinforcement contingencies have a positive impact on task performance and levels of motivation for both children with AD/HD and normal controls</q> — and the responsiveness is to how often, not how much: <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="Key findings">sensitive to frequency but blind to the magnitude of penalty</q>. Many small immediate signals do work that one large delayed payout does not, which suits a queue of small credits better than a single refund at the end of the month.

## None of it has been tested directly

For a question this specific, the direct trial does not exist. <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="Key findings">No RCT tests cash micro-incentives for task completion in adults with ADHD — a genuine evidence gap.</q> The nearest academic analogue tested whether varying the payment — surge pricing for your own chores — beats a flat amount, and found it does not: <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="Key findings">no significant benefit, 0.66 success fixed vs 0.61 adaptive (P=.54); incentive size no significant effect (OR 0.98)</q>. The earn-back version has no trial at all. The research's reading of the field is that the gap is real rather than crowded: <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="Summary">So the combination is genuinely open space.</q>

> **Objection: Turning chores into paid orders is gambling with extra steps**
>
> The concern has a real basis for this group — a systematic review <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="Key findings">estimated ADHD prevalence among people experiencing gambling harm at 24% (95% CI 18–29%) vs 2.58% general</q> — though the association is not settled, since another study <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="Key findings">found no adjusted association (OR 0.6)</q>. The distinction that matters is chance. A fixed credit for a completed task is a rebate; a variable payout for an uncertain outcome is a bet. The research's instruction on that is one sentence long: <q doc="uber-for-your-own-to-do-list-feasibility-and-prior-art-review-of-a-pai" section="Key findings">Do not use lotteries.</q>

## What this explains

It explains why the delivery-app comparison feels so sharp and transfers unevenly. Five of the six things that make an order easy to accept — an external queue, one job at a time, a bounded task, a ping, immediate feedback — are structure, and structure can be copied. The sixth is money that matters. Coins moved between your own pockets do not supply it. A fee you stand to lose might, and the research's best guess is that a subscription you earn back carries the weight a self-paid wage does not. That guess is untested, and it still depends on the hinge that broke the last company to try: a credit is only a stake if claiming it takes more than a tap. The idea is not closed. It is narrowed to a version that can be tried, and the trial has not been run.
