Peter Sterling and Joseph Eyer coined the word *allostasis* in 1988 for what a later review summarises as achieving stability through change [1]. Three words are easy to keep. Whether they keep the idea is a separate question, and the answer says something about how memory holds ideas in general.

## Memory keeps the meaning

In 1967 Jacqueline Sachs played people recorded passages and tested whether they could tell a sentence they had heard from a changed version of it. Tested straight after the original, they noticed every kind of change. After 80 to 160 more syllables of the passage, their recognition of changes to the wording had fallen to near chance, while changes to the meaning were still easy to spot [2]. Sachs concluded that the exact form of a sentence is stored only for the short time it takes to understand it.

A long definition, then, is not kept as its sentences. It is kept as what it means, and the words are found again when it has to be said. A short cue can stand for that meaning: something to call it back with.

## A cue opens what was stored

Endel Tulving and Zena Pearlstone read people lists of words grouped under category names, then asked for the words back, with or without the category names as prompts. Prompted recall was always better, and people who had already recalled freely remembered further words, mostly from whole categories they had missed, once they were given the names [3]. The words had been stored all along. The cue made them reachable. Tulving and Pearlstone called the difference *available* against *accessible*.

## Only what was learned

A cue cannot open what was never stored. Shown a position from a real chess game for a few seconds, masters could set out about 93% of its roughly 25 pieces, while beginners managed about a third. With the same pieces placed at random, every player, master or beginner, recalled only about three or four [4]. The masters' memory rested on patterns learned over years, and where the board held no pattern, there was nothing for it to hold.

A cue for an idea works the same way. "Stability through change" brings back allostasis only for someone who learned what the words stand for.

## Context first

The order of learning matters too. John Bransford and Marcia Johnson read people a deliberately vague passage about sorting things into groups and doing a few at a time. One group was told beforehand that it was about washing clothes, one was told afterwards, and one was never told. Knowing the topic in advance helped people understand and recall the passage; the authors concluded that "prior knowledge of a situation does not guarantee its usefulness for comprehension": it helps when it is active as the passage is heard [5].

For an idea, that means learning the short version first and the detail after it, so the detail has somewhere to go.

## Rebuilding is the test

A cue can also hide how little is behind it. Leonid Rozenblit and Frank Keil asked people how well they understood everyday devices, then asked them to explain, step by step, how each one works. Across twelve studies, people understood far less than they had thought, and the gap was much larger for how things work than for facts, procedures or stories [6]. Knowing the three words feels like knowing allostasis. Rebuilding the idea from them, in full and without looking, is how to find out.

The rebuilding also keeps the idea. Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke had students study short prose passages and then either study them again or recall them with no feedback. Five minutes later, restudying had done better. Two days and a week later, recalling had done substantially better, although restudying had made the students more confident [7].

## What the cue leaves out

Rebuilt from "stability through change", allostasis comes out as the body adjusting to stay stable. Checked against the sources, two things are missing: one from the coiner's own later account, one from the work that built on it.

The first is timing. Sterling later described allostasis as regulation by prediction: "efficient regulation requires anticipating needs and preparing to satisfy them before they arise" [8]. The body adjusts ahead of a demand, from what it expects, where the older idea of homeostasis corrects an error after it appears.

The second is cost. Bruce McEwen and Eliot Stellar defined allostatic load as "the cost of chronic exposure to fluctuating or heightened neural or neuroendocrine response" [9]. Adjustment kept up for too long wears the body down. That cost is what connects allostasis to stress and disease, and, in the paper on [narcocrasis](/papers/narcocrasis), to addiction.

Neither is in the three words, though the three words summarise the original definition. A cue short enough to keep is shorter than the idea, so rebuilding from it fills the gaps with whatever its words suggest on their own. The source is where the gaps show.

## How to keep one

1. **Learn the idea in full once.** The definition, where it comes from, an example.
2. **Take the shortest accurate cue you can find,** from the source if it offers one.
3. **Learn the short version first,** then the detail.
4. **Rebuild the idea from the cue alone,** out loud or on paper.
5. **Check the rebuild against the source now and then,** and put back what the cue lost.

## Where this stops

The memory studies used spoken passages, word lists, chess positions, one vague paragraph and everyday devices, mostly with students, and measured memory over minutes to a week. None tested a cue for an abstract scientific idea held over months. Allostasis is one example, and its original definition is given here through a later review's summary. What the studies do establish is that memory keeps meaning and drops wording, a cue opens only what was learned, context organises what follows it, and recalling an idea both strengthens it and shows whether it was there.
