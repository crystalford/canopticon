More than 80% of people with an addiction never seek treatment [1]. The usual explanation is denial: the person knows, at some level, and will not admit it. A review by addiction neuroscientists proposed a different reading. What looks like denial, they argued, may instead reflect a breakdown in the brain networks that support insight and self-awareness, so that the person genuinely does not perceive how severe the problem has become [1].

That raises a narrower question. If something in dependence goes unseen, what is it?

## What changes in dependence?

The most influential modern model of addiction describes a shift in the brain's reward and stress systems. With repeated use, the systems that oppose a drug's effects grow stronger and stay switched on, and the brain's reward set point moves: ordinary pleasures feel flatter, and stress and unease rise between doses [2]. The model calls this an allostatic state, stability reached at a new and worse level [2].

In that state, the drug's main job changes. It no longer adds pleasure to an ordinary day so much as it relieves a bad one. Experiments on human drug choice support that shift: one review concluded that negative states "powerfully increase expected drug value acutely outweighing abstinence goals" [3]. The drug is chosen, deliberately, because it makes a bad state better.

## Why is the change so hard to see?

A baseline is what everything else is felt against. When it moves slowly, over months or years, there is no moment at which it is noticed changing; it simply becomes how things are. Perception research shows the same effect for gradual change: in a set of experiments, people looking at an image that changed slowly missed changes large enough that they would consistently have been detected had they happened all at once [9]. The drug stays vivid, because it brings relief. The flatness, the restlessness and the raised stress tone it relieves are taken for the person's temperament, or for how the world is.

The brain region most implicated in this is the insula, which represents the state of the body and is involved in conscious urges [1]. In a study of smokers who had suffered brain damage, those whose damage involved the insula were more likely to quit easily, immediately and without relapse, and without the urge to smoke coming back [4]. A computational study found that people with substance use disorders failed to adjust how much they trusted signals from inside their own body when those signals changed [5]. Both point the same way: the felt need for the drug is carried by the brain's model of the body, and that model is the part that has shifted.

This publication's paper on McLuhan and addiction gives the shifted, unnoticed baseline a name: [narcocrasis](/concepts/narcocrasis), from the Greek for numbness and a tempered mixture. In McLuhan's terms the drug is the figure everyone looks at, and the changed baseline is the ground that nobody sees. The theory of [ground blindness](/theories/ground-blindness) describes the same pattern for tools and routines in general.

## Do people recover from it?

Most do, eventually. In a national US survey, the estimated lifetime chance of remission from dependence was 84% for nicotine, 91% for alcohol, 97% for cannabis and 99% for cocaine, though half of the cases took about 26, 14, 6 and 5 years respectively to remit [6]. The figures say recovery is the usual outcome, and that for many people it takes a long time.

## Why do other people matter so much?

In rats given a choice between a drug and time with another rat, the chance of social interaction prevented drug self-administration, regardless of sex, drug type, dose or how addicted the animal had become [7]. Among people, a Cochrane review found high-quality evidence that structured programmes linking people to Alcoholics Anonymous were more effective than other established treatments, including cognitive behavioural therapy, at increasing abstinence [8].

There is a plausible reason in the model above. If the hardest thing to see is one's own baseline, the clearest view of it comes from outside: people who knew you before, who notice the change, and whose company offers a source of relief other than the drug. The paper names recovery of this kind [anacrasis](/concepts/anacrasis), from an ancient Greek word for mixing, which in one of its senses meant mixing with others.

## What it means

Calling the problem denial makes it sound like a refusal. On this evidence it is at least partly a failure of perception, of the one thing a person cannot easily step outside: the state they compare everything else to. That does not make the person less responsible for what they do, and it does not make change impossible; most people do change. It suggests that information from outside the baseline, from other people and from time away from the drug, is worth more than any argument about the drug itself.

If you are worried about your own use, a doctor or a local addiction service can help you look at it from outside.
