Someone has driven to a friend's house a dozen times with a phone giving directions. One day the phone dies on the way, and they find they do not know the route. They have been there often, but they have never found their way there.

London taxi drivers are the opposite case. To qualify, they spend three to four years learning the city well enough to plan any route themselves. A study that followed trainees found that those who qualified gained grey matter in the back of the hippocampus, a region involved in spatial memory, and that those who failed and a control group did not [1]. Same city, same task. In one case a device produces the route, and in the other the driver does.

The time spent with the information is about the same. What differs is who produces the answer, and when.

## A guess gives the answer something to correct

The clearest evidence comes from a simple experiment that sounds as if it should fail. Students were given questions about a science passage before they read it. They mostly got them wrong: their accuracy on these pretests was between 5% and 22%. Yet in all five experiments, the students who had tried to answer first did better on a later test than students who had spent the same time simply reading, for example 75% against 56% [2]. The guesses were wrong, and they still helped.

Larger bodies of research point the same way. A meta-analysis of 53 studies compared teaching a concept and then setting problems with the reverse order, setting the problem first and teaching afterwards. Problem first won, by an effect size of 0.36 [3]. Across 225 studies of university science teaching, students in classes where they worked on problems during the lesson scored about half a standard deviation higher on exams than students in lectures, and students in lectures were one and a half times as likely to fail [4].

What these have in common is order. In each case the learner produces something, a guess, an attempt, a partial solution, before the answer arrives. When it arrives, it corrects something the learner made. When the answer comes first, there is nothing of the learner's own for it to correct, and less changes.

## The same AI can do either

A field experiment with nearly a thousand high-school maths students shows that order belongs to how a tool is used. Both groups had GPT-4. One version answered whatever it was asked. The other was set up by teachers to give hints instead of answers. While they had the AI, both groups did far better on practice problems. Then the AI was taken away for the exam. Students who had used the answering version scored 17% below students who never had AI at all. Students who had used the hinting version scored about the same as those students [5].

The model, the subject and the time were the same. The difference was whether the student had to produce the next step before seeing it.

The same choice is open to anyone using a chatbot today. Typing "explain compound interest" and reading the answer is the first version. Writing "I think it means interest on interest, so $100 at 10% becomes $121 after two years; is that right, and where does my picture break?" is the second. The second takes a minute longer and leaves far more behind.

## Why it feels like learning less

If guessing first works so well, why does almost nobody do it? Part of the answer was measured directly. In a physics course, students were taught the same material in two ways, by a polished lecture and by an active session in which they worked through problems. They learned more from the active session, by about half a standard deviation. They also rated it as worse: their sense of having learned was lower by a similar amount. The authors report that students "initially take that effort to signify poorer learning" [6].

The effort of producing a guess is unpleasant, and the smoothness of a finished explanation is pleasant, so a person choosing by feel will choose the explanation. Choosing by how a lesson feels therefore favours the version that leaves less behind.

## Where guessing first does not help

The strongest objection is from people who know nothing yet. A guess needs something to guess with. Research on what is called the expertise reversal effect finds that complete beginners learn more from studying a fully worked example than from trying to solve the problem, and that the advantage reverses as they gain knowledge: what helps a novice can hold back someone more experienced [7]. In the problem-first studies, the youngest children did better when they were taught first [3].

So guessing first suits people who already have something to guess with. A body can make its own heat in the cold only if it has fuel, and a learner is similar. A beginner needs worked examples first. As they learn, the worked steps can be removed one at a time and they can be asked to predict more of each answer. Teachers call this fading.

There are also tasks where handing the work to a tool costs little. Meta-analyses of calculators in schools found no harm to arithmetic [9], and most of the studies they pooled were of children past the age at which arithmetic is first learned. The question to ask is whether the task in front of you is one you want to be able to do yourself.

## Hot and cool

Marshall McLuhan divided media into hot and cool in 1964. A hot medium, he wrote, extends one sense in "high definition", and "high definition is the state of being well filled with data"; hot media leave little for the audience to fill in, and cool media are "high in participation or completion by the audience" [8]. *Understanding Media* does not say why he used temperature, though a year later he described the senses in a thermostat's terms. One reading, set out in the working paper *Why Temperature*, is that he was describing regulation: a body warmed from outside stops making its own heat, and a mind handed a finished answer makes no prediction of its own.

Biology calls animals that take their heat from the environment ectotherms. A lizard basks, and when the sun goes in it slows down. *Cognitive ectothermy* is the same arrangement for understanding: answers, routes and explanations arrive from outside, and when the supply stops, so does the ability to produce them. The dead phone is what that looks like. Unlike a lizard, a person can change which kind they are, by how they spend their time.

## What it means

The usual argument about tools is whether they are good or bad. The evidence here suggests a different question: in this exchange, who predicts first? Search, maps, lectures and AI can each be used either way. A guess written down before asking, a route tried before checking, an explanation given back in your own words after reading: each costs a little effort, each feels slower, and each is the part that stays.
