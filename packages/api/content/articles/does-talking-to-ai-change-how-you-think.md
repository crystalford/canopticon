For most of the history of computing, people typed to machines. Now they increasingly talk to them: dictating messages, leaving voice notes, holding spoken conversations with AI assistants. It is reasonable to ask whether thinking out loud to a machine changes the thinking itself. The research on speaking, writing and explaining gives a fairly specific answer, though not the one most people expect.

## Is speaking different from typing?

Producing language in writing costs more than producing it in speech. Experiments comparing the two find that writing places heavier demands on working memory than speaking; as the title of one study puts it, "even in adults, written production is still more costly than oral production" [1]. Writing adds spelling and the mechanics of the pen or keyboard to the task, and speaking leaves more attention for what is being said.

That difference is the basis of an argument made in this publication's papers on McLuhan: that networked computing stayed typed for roughly fifty years because the technology could not yet carry the voice, and that the shift now under way returns speech as the way people put language into machines. The papers call the delay [typomora](/concepts/typomora) and the return [epanaphony](/concepts/epanaphony).

## Does saying your thoughts out loud change them?

Not by itself. A meta-analysis of 94 studies, with nearly 3,500 participants, compared people who thought aloud while solving problems with people who worked silently. Thinking aloud, simply saying what is going through one's mind, made no measurable difference to performance (r = −.03) [2]. Being asked to describe or explain one's thinking was different: it changed performance, and raised it, though it also made the work slower [2].

What changes thinking, on this evidence, is explanation: putting into words why something is so, or how the steps connect.

## Does explaining aloud work better than explaining in writing?

Explaining is one of the better-supported learning techniques. Across 64 research reports, prompting learners to explain material to themselves produced an average effect size of g = 0.55 [3].

Whether it matters if the explanation is spoken is less settled. In one experiment, students who explained a topic on video to an imagined fellow student learned more than students who restudied it, while students who wrote the explanation did not do reliably better than restudying; the direct difference between speaking and writing was not significant [4, as summarised in 6]. A later study found that explaining aloud beat explaining in writing for a difficult text but not an easy one, and traced the difference to a stronger sense of an audience: students speaking to an imagined listener gave richer explanations, with more of the concepts in them [5, as summarised in 6]. A third study, which tried to give writers the same sense of an audience by having them explain in a messenger-style chat, found no difference in learning between speaking, writing and chatting, and not even an advantage of explaining over recalling the material, though spoken explanations still contained more concepts and elaborations than chat ones [6].

The fair summary is that explaining helps, and speaking to someone tends to produce fuller explanations than typing, but the advantage of speech for learning is not yet reliable.

## What changes when the listener talks back?

An AI is not the imagined student of these experiments. It answers, and it can explain better and faster than the person talking to it. That changes who is doing the work.

A field experiment with nearly a thousand high-school maths students shows what is at stake. Students who used a version of GPT-4 that gave answers did far better on practice problems while they had it, and scored 17% below students who never had it once it was taken away for the exam; students whose version gave hints instead of answers scored about the same as those who never had it [7]. The difference was whether the student or the machine produced the next step. The same applies to a spoken conversation: a person who talks a problem through with an AI and does the explaining is doing the thing that helps, and a person who asks and listens is receiving an explanation someone else produced. This publication's essay [Guess before you look](/articles/guess-before-you-look) and the theory of [cognitive ectothermy](/theories/cognitive-ectothermy) follow that difference further.

## What it means

Talking to an AI can change how a person thinks. Speaking lowers the effort of producing language and, with a listener in mind, tends to draw out fuller explanations. Whether that ends up building understanding depends on the order of the conversation. Saying what you think first, in your own words, and then asking the AI where it is wrong, uses the medium the way the explaining studies suggest. Asking first and listening uses it the other way.

Whether a long habit of speaking to machines changes inner speech or the way people organise thoughts is still an open question; this publication keeps it at [Does talking to an AI change how you think?](/open/does-talking-to-an-ai-change-how-you-think).
