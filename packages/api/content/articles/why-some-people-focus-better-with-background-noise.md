Many people with ADHD say they work better with a fan running, rain sounds in their headphones or the murmur of a café. Interest in white, pink and brown noise "for ADHD" has grown quickly enough that researchers reviewed the evidence in 2024 [1]. The research is smaller than the trend, but it points somewhere specific: noise does help people with ADHD a little, and it does the opposite for people without it.

## Does background noise help people with ADHD focus?

A little, on the evidence so far. A 2024 meta-analysis pooled 13 randomised studies of children and young adults with ADHD or high ADHD symptoms, 335 people in all, who did laboratory attention tasks with and without white or pink noise. Noise produced a small but consistent benefit, an effect size of g = 0.25, with little variation between studies and no sign of publication bias [1]. No study of brown noise could be found [1].

The striking result came from the comparison groups. In 11 studies of people without ADHD, the same noise made performance slightly worse, g = −0.21 [1]. An earlier experiment had shown the same crossover in a single study: white noise improved memory performance for children with ADHD and lowered it for children without [2].

## Why would noise help one brain and hurt another?

The explanation most often offered is stochastic resonance. In a system with a threshold, a weak signal that falls just below it can be pushed over by a moderate amount of random noise, so the signal is detected more often; too much noise swamps it again [3]. The effect has been measured in vision, hearing and touch, and in single neurons [3].

Applied to ADHD, the idea is that the best amount of noise depends on where the brain's own activity is set. The moderate brain arousal model proposes that lower dopamine levels in ADHD mean more noise is needed to reach the peak of that curve, while a brain already near its best is pushed past it [2, 4]. An older account, optimal stimulation theory, proposed in 1983 that hyperactive children seek stimulation to bring an under-aroused system up to a working level [5]. Both say the same thing in different terms: the right amount of input depends on the starting point.

## Is fidgeting the same thing?

It appears to be. In a study of boys aged 8 to 12, observers recorded how much each child moved during working-memory tasks across four sessions. Children with ADHD did better on the tasks when they were moving more, though not as well as typically developing children; typically developing children did slightly worse when they moved more [6]. The authors read this as movement serving a function in ADHD, and cautioned against stopping it during tasks that load working memory [6].

Noise and movement are different kinds of input, and they show the same split. That is what one would expect if the difference lies in the brain's overall gain, the degree to which it amplifies all of its signals at once, and not in any one sense.

## What doesn't help?

Not every kind of background helps, and not every task. In a classroom study, video playing in the room distracted boys with ADHD more than it distracted other boys, while music helped some children and hurt others, with no consistent group effect [7]. White noise did not improve a task that measures control over eye movements, in a 2024 study of 52 children with ADHD [8]. The meta-analysis's benefit was measured on laboratory tasks, mostly short ones, and its authors note the limits of that evidence [1].

The effect is also small. A benefit of a quarter of a standard deviation is real but modest, and it does not replace treatment: in the classroom study, medication improved performance whether or not music was playing [7]. Because the same noise slightly hinders people without ADHD, a fan that helps one person in a shared room may cost the person at the next desk.

## A different starting mix of the senses

The idea that brains start from different settings has a name in this publication's papers on McLuhan and the senses: [heterocrasis](/concepts/heterocrasis), a configuration of the senses that differs before any medium acts on it. The paper that proposes it, [Heterocrasis](/papers/heterocrasis), argues that ADHD's clearest difference is in overall gain, and that external stimulation may supply some of what the nervous system does not. Background noise is one of the few places where that can be seen in a controlled study: the same input moves two groups in opposite directions.

For anyone who works better with a fan on, the studies offer a practical reading. The preference may be doing something, and the people who find noise distracting may be right too.
