People talk about a blackout as though the night is filed somewhere in the drinker's head and the right reminder would bring it back. It is not. The hours were never written down.

## The screen stays on; the save fails

A blackout is not passing out. Through the whole of it the drinker is upright and apparently present — <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Key findings">the person is awake and can talk, walk, drive, and have sex — but the brain fails to lay down new episodic memories</q>. Aaron White, the scientist whose review defined the modern account, puts the consequence plainly: <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Key findings">This creates a temporary void in the record-keeping system. Memories lost in a blackout will never come back, because the information wasn't stored in the first place.</q>

What has gone is one specific step. Old memories are intact and the working memory that holds a conversation together is mostly fine; the failure is in the handover between them — <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Key findings">the transfer of new information into durable storage</q>. So a person can follow an argument, win it, and hold none of it the next morning.

The damage starts well before a blackout. <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Details">Beginning with just one or two drinks, the ability to move new information into durable storage begins to degrade; at high, rapidly-reached BACs it can fail almost completely.</q> The research's image for it is the one that matters: <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Details">the "display screen" (working memory) keeps working while the "save-to-disk" function (hippocampal consolidation) is disabled.</q>

## Where in the brain the recording stops

Two regions carry most of the failure. The hippocampus <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Details">binds the "what, where, and when" of experience into episodic memory</q>, and alcohol scrambles its map of place; in one study, ethanol <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Details">partly reduced or abolished the location-specific firing of place cells while causing others to newly emerge</q>. It also disturbs the rhythm that coordinates recording, by <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Details">reducing the regularity with which septal neurons drive the hippocampal theta rhythm (4–12 Hz)</q>.

The front of the brain fails at the same time. In a controlled human study, high-dose alcohol <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Details">significantly suppressed LTP-like neuroplasticity in the dorsolateral PFC at 30 and 60 minutes</q>. That is the part of the brain that judges and restrains, which is why blackouts are dangerous rather than merely forgetful: <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Details">the drinker is simultaneously unable to record events and unable to judge them well.</q>

## The block is manufactured, not just a slowdown

Alcohol was long treated as a blanket dampener of the brain. The memory failure turns out to be a specific chemical event, and not the one most people assume: <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Details">the acute amnesia arises from interference with synaptic plasticity, not cell death — a reversible functional block, which is why memory returns to normal once the alcohol clears</q>. Nothing is destroyed. The writing mechanism is held shut.

Two things hold it. Ethanol partly blocks the glutamate receptors that start the strengthening of a synapse — <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Details">The amplitude of the NMDA-activated current was reduced 61 percent by 50 mM</q> — but on its own that is not enough. The second part is stranger: through the receptors it has not blocked, alcohol makes the hippocampus manufacture a sedative of its own. <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Details">neurosteroid staining was increased following 15 min administration of 60 mM but not 20 mM ethanol</q>. That home-made neurosteroid slams the brain's inhibitory system wide open, and the synapse cannot record.

The threshold in the lab mirrors the threshold in life. Alcohol <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Details">requires high concentrations (roughly 60 mM, corresponding to severe intoxication) to fully block LTP; at ~20 mM it has little effect</q>. The body's own breakdown product makes it worse: alcohol turned into acetaldehyde inside the hippocampus means that <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Details">20 mM ethanol plus acetaldehyde reproduces the LTP block that normally requires 60 mM ethanol</q>.

Because it is a chemical cascade, it can be stopped. In the laboratory the block <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Details">could be prevented by 5α-reductase inhibitors (finasteride, dutasteride) that stop neurosteroid synthesis, and by NMDA antagonists (APV)</q>, and a drug that calms the cell's stress response <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Details">can reverse ethanol's effects on LTP and learning</q>. The researcher who led the work called the glutamate receptors involved <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Details">"a double-edged sword"</q>.

## Speed decides it, not the count

This is the part that changes what a person should do about it. <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Details">Blackouts are far more strongly tied to a fast-rising BAC than to the absolute quantity consumed.</q> The brain builds a partial tolerance as the night goes on, so the danger is concentrated in the climb. In a controlled study, alcohol <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Details">impaired retrieval in word recognition only during the ascending BAC</q>.

Wearable sensors have since measured the climb directly in students. Days with <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Details">faster alcohol-rise rates (odds ratio ≈ 2.69), higher peaks (≈ 2.93), and longer rise durations (≈ 4.16) independently predicted markedly greater odds of blackout</q>. Two people can drink the same amount on the same night and only the one who drank it quickly, on an empty stomach, loses the evening.

The concentrations are known. Per the American alcohol institute, blackouts <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Details">"tend to begin at blood alcohol concentrations (BACs) of about 0.16 percent (nearly twice the legal driving limit) and higher."</q> A forensic study found <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Details">"a 50% chance of experiencing either type of blackout at a BAC of 0.22%, and a 50% chance of en bloc blackouts at a BAC of 0.32%"</q>. The rising-limb effect is old; impairment <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Details">is worse when BAC is rising than at the same BAC when falling, first described by Mellanby in 1919</q>. And sex matters: <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Details">Women reach higher and faster peak BACs per drink (lower body water, lower average weight), helping explain their elevated blackout risk.</q>

The research is careful not to stretch the point: <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Caveats">for memory specifically, encoding may be impaired on both limbs while retrieval is impaired mainly on the rising limb</q>, and the thresholds are <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Caveats">statistical associations with wide individual variation</q>, not safe limits.

## One kind can be cued back; one kind cannot

The patchy sort of blackout, where fragments return when someone tells you what happened, is the common one. In those, <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Details">hippocampal encoding is impaired but not abolished, so weak or partial traces exist and can be triggered by cues</q>. The dense kind is different in degree and absolute in effect: <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Details">consolidation is completely disrupted, so no trace was ever stored and retrieval cues are useless</q> This is why pressing someone to remember is not just unkind but pointless. There is no file to open.

The two kinds side by side:

| Feature | Fragmentary (“grayout”/“brownout”) | En bloc (complete) |
|---|---|---|
| Memory loss | Patchy, "islands" of memory with gaps | Dense, continuous gap |
| Cueing | Cues/reminders can recover memories | Memories cannot be recovered by any cue |
| Underlying deficit | Degraded/partial encoding | Consolidation fully blocked — never stored |
| Onset | Gradual, subtler | Often definite onset |
| Frequency | Much more common | Less common; associated with higher BAC and often polysubstance use |

The distinction is measurable, not just clinical folklore: <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Details">The validated Alcohol-Induced Blackout Measure (ABOM-2) confirms this two-factor structure empirically.</q>

## How common blackouts are, and who is most at risk

Blackouts are not a sign of alcoholism on their own; they are common. In a survey of college students, <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Details">"Approximately half (51%) of those who had ever consumed alcohol reported they had experienced a blackout at some point in their lives, and 40% had experienced 1 in the year before the survey."</q> A large twin study found <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Details">more than 39% of women and 52% of men reported blackouts, with genetic factors accounting for a substantial share of risk</q>.

Teenagers carry a particular risk. Alcohol suppresses the hippocampus more in adolescent animals, and adolescents are <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Details">simultaneously less sensitive to sedation/motor impairment — a dangerous combination that lets young drinkers consume more before feeling impaired while their memory systems are more easily disrupted</q>. Other drugs lower the threshold too: <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Recommendations">Mixing alcohol with benzodiazepines or sleep aids is especially hazardous.</q>

## The brain is not protecting itself

A folk theory holds that the mind shuts the recorder off to spare the drinker. The evidence runs the other way — <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Details">There is no functional benefit and no evidence of an evolved "off switch"; rather, blackout is what happens when a threshold concentration of a plasticity-disrupting drug is reached.</q> And the gap it leaves does damage of its own: <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Details">Because blackouts erase the negative consequences of a drinking episode while the early euphoria is remembered, they may bias alcohol expectancies positively and facilitate further heavy drinking — the opposite of protective.</q> The night keeps its best hour and loses the bill.

One odd effect looks like protection and is not. Alcohol taken after learning can improve memory of what came before. The research explains why: <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Details">This is a byproduct of alcohol's anterograde amnestic effect (nothing new competes with the earlier memory), not evidence of a protective purpose.</q> Repeated blackouts in young people are a warning sign in their own right; they <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Details">are also markers of, and may contribute to, attenuated hippocampal development and later cognitive problems</q>.

> **Objection: This is all rats and brain slices**
>
> Much of the chemistry is, and the research says so: <q doc="the-neurobiology-of-alcohol-blackouts-how-ethanol-silences-the-brain-s" section="Caveats">The concentrations map onto severe human intoxication, but the precise human molecular sequence is inferred, not directly measured in intact human brains.</q> What is measured in people is the behaviour and the thresholds — the preserved conversation, the absent memory, the steep climb in blood alcohol. The mechanism explains those; it is not what establishes them.

## What this explains

It explains the most dangerous thing about a blackout, which is how ordinary it looks from outside: the part of the brain that talks is working, and the part that records is not, so nobody in the room can tell. It explains why reminders fail where they usually succeed. It explains why the advice that works is about pace rather than totals — the same drinks, taken slowly and after food, may never reach the threshold at all. And it reframes what the drinker lost. Not a memory, which implies something misplaced. An hour of their life that was lived and never written.
