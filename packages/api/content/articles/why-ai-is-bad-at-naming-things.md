While writing an essay for this site about streamers who never take a day off, I asked an AI to help name the pattern: people who leave a job and rebuild the old boss inside themselves. Its first suggestion was "the portable foreman." It sounded like a name. It even had a little picture in it. It also made no sense: a foreman supervises other workers, and these streamers have none. The next round brought "conspicuous toil" and "performed hardship," which were better, and then a set of phrases built on internet templates, such as "grind cosplay." None of them felt like something a person would say.

That is a common experience. Ask a chatbot to name a new idea and you get phrases with the shape of a name: the something paradox, the something effect, two nouns joined at an angle. They are rarely wrong and rarely right. The reasons are worth spelling out, because they say something about where names come from.

## A good name is unlikely and obvious at once

A language model writes by predicting the next word. Given a description of an idea, it produces the phrases most likely to follow, which means the phrases closest to what has been written about similar ideas before. That makes it good at descriptions and weak at names.

A name that works has two properties that pull against each other. It has to be surprising enough that nobody would have produced it by describing the idea plainly. And it has to fit so well that once you hear it, it seems obvious. "Doomscrolling" is like that. Before it existed, nobody describing the habit of reading bad news on a phone late at night would have predicted that word. After it existed, it was hard to call the habit anything else.

Ease matters too. In one study, shares in companies with easy-to-say names outperformed shares with hard-to-say names in their first days of trading; a $1,000 investment in the easy names made $112 more after one day [1]. A name has to be easy to say and to remember. A model can supply easy phrases all day. What it cannot supply on demand is the improbable one that also fits.

## Tuning narrows it further

The training that turns a raw language model into a helpful assistant makes this worse. A study of that training process found that it improved how well models handled new kinds of requests and significantly reduced the variety of what they wrote [2]. The assistant learns to give the answer most people would rate well, and the answer most people would rate well is rarely the strange one.

The narrowing passes to the people using the model. In a controlled experiment, people wrote essays with a raw model, with a tuned assistant, or alone. Writing with the tuned assistant made different people's essays more similar to each other; writing with the raw model did not [3]. In another experiment, writers given story ideas by a model produced stories that judges rated as more creative and better written, and the stories were also more alike [4]. A third study found that people brainstorming with ChatGPT produced more ideas, but their ideas were less distinct from other people's, and they felt less responsible for them [5].

For naming, sameness is the whole problem. A name has to stand apart. A tool that pulls everyone's suggestions toward the same middle produces names that sound like other names.

## Names are chosen by use

Most names that last were never designed. Someone said something in the middle of a moment, and other people repeated it because it fit.

"Doom scroll" appeared in a tweet in April 2018, and "doom-scrolling" in another that October, both from ordinary users. For two years the word stayed small. In March 2020, as the pandemic closed things down, a University of Michigan professor tweeted that she was "nervously doomscrolling." Within ten days the word was in newspapers [6]. The word was already there. The moment gave thousands of people a reason to use it.

A study of new words on Reddit found the pattern behind this. The words that grew fastest and survived longest were the ones that could be used in the most kinds of sentences, as a verb, a noun, a joke, a complaint. How widely a word spread among different people mattered less than researchers had expected [7]. A name lasts when it is useful in many situations, and people find that out by using it. No one can find it out in advance by looking at a list.

A model has no moment and no crowd. It produces candidates outside any situation, and nothing in it registers whether a phrase gets repeated.

## People can still coin them on purpose

Deliberate names do sometimes work. In 2022 and 2023 the writer Cory Doctorow described, in a series of posts, how online platforms first serve their users, then shift value to business customers, then take it for themselves. He called the process enshittification. The American Dialect Society named it Word of the Year for 2023, with more than 300 people taking part in the vote [8], and Australia's Macquarie Dictionary chose it for 2024 [9].

Doctorow had what a model lacks. He named a process he had been watching for years, with steps people could recognise in their own apps. The word was rude and funny, which gave people a reason to repeat it. And he used it again and again, in post after post, until it had been tested in many sentences. The name came from inside the experience, from someone who had felt it.

## What a model is good for

None of this makes a model useless for naming. It can widen the field quickly, offering fifty candidates where a person would think of five. It can check whether a phrase is already in use, find the templates people are using now, and explain why one option fits the idea better than another. Those are real contributions.

The choice belongs to people. Say the name aloud to someone who has not heard it, and see whether they use it back. Listen to what the people inside the thing already call it; they often have a word before anyone studies them. A name is a social fact. It exists when other people use it, and only people can find out whether they will.

## Where this stops

The studies here used models from 2021 to 2023, and newer models may be tuned to vary more. The diversity findings come from essays, stories and brainstorming, not from naming itself; no study I found tests models on coining names directly. The opening story is one experience on one essay. The argument is that the pattern has causes we can name: prediction favours the likely, tuning favours the familiar, and names are chosen by use in moments a model never sees.
