William Miller and Janet C'de Baca interviewed 55 people who said a single sudden experience had changed them for good. On average the change had happened eleven years before the interview. About half described an insight, a sudden realisation, often at one of the worst points of their lives; the rest described something closer to a mystical experience. Afterwards, what they had valued most tended to matter less, and what had mattered little moved up [1]. Accounts like these are reported years afterwards, by the people they happened to, so they say little about how an idea could do this. Experiments come closer.

## One hour, three years

In 2011 Gregory Walton and Geoffrey Cohen gave first-year university students about an hour of material on belonging [2]. The students read survey results from older students: most had worried in their first year about whether they belonged, and the worry had faded with time. Each student then wrote about how their own experience fit that pattern and recorded it as a short speech for future students. A control group did the same with a survey on unrelated topics.

Over the next three years the lesson raised the grades of the Black students who took it. The gap between Black and white students' grade averages fell from 0.29 points in the control group to 0.14 in the treated group. The Black students who took the lesson also reported better health, and the share who had recently seen a doctor fell from 60% to 28% [2].

## Almost no one remembered it

At the end, only 8% of the students recalled the lesson's main content, and 14% said the study had any effect on their time at university [2]. Whatever carried the effect for three years, it was not the students thinking about the lesson.

The daily surveys from the week after show what it was. In the control group, the Black students' sense of belonging rose and fell with the day's adversity: on worse days, belonging dropped (a within-person correlation of −0.45). In the treated group that link was gone (0.01) [2]. Bad days still came. They were read differently: as the ordinary first-year difficulty the older students had described, which passes.

## Inside a loop

David Yeager and Gregory Walton, reviewing interventions like this one, put the effect down to recursive processes [3]. In a loop like this, a student who reads a bad day as proof of not belonging pulls back a little, asks fewer questions, goes to fewer study groups, and that makes the next bad day more likely. A student who reads the same day as normal keeps going, and gets the help and the grades that make the next day better. The lesson changes the reading once; the year supplies the bad days, and each one is read the new way. The effect builds because the event keeps coming round, and it outlasts the memory of the lesson because the reading has become how those days are seen.

So an idea changes a life when it changes how something that keeps happening is read.

## Where the loop holds

The surroundings decide whether the new reading survives. In a national experiment, 12,490 American ninth-graders took two online sessions of about 25 minutes each on the idea that ability can grow. Lower-achieving students' grades rose by 0.10 points on average, and the rise was larger in schools where classmates already sought out hard work [4]. Where the peers around a student read setbacks the old way, the new reading was outnumbered every day.

## Words met and words used

A word met in passing does much less. A famous experiment reported that people who unscrambled words about old age walked more slowly afterwards. When Stéphane Doyen and colleagues timed the walk with infrared sensors, the effect did not appear. It appeared only when the experimenters timing it expected people to walk slowly [5].

A word used on an experience does measurable work. Matthew Lieberman and colleagues showed 30 people faces expressing emotions, mostly fear or anger. Choosing the word for the emotion from two options reduced the amygdala's response more than choosing a fitting name for the face from two names, and it raised activity in the right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex [6]. The word was applied to what was in front of them at that moment, which is the same thing the belonging lesson did with each bad day.

## The flash

Sudden understanding does leave a mark. In one study, people watched magic tricks and tried to work out the method. Two weeks later they recalled 64.4% of the solutions that had come with an "Aha!" and 52.4% of those that had not [7]. The flash makes an idea memorable. It does not make it right. In a later study of the same kind, 37% of the wrong solutions got Aha ratings above the average for the right ones [8]. The flash is how an idea arrives. Whether it changes anything depends on what it is then used on.

## Using an idea

1. **Find what keeps happening** that the idea is about: a bad day at work, an argument that repeats, a bill that comes due, the moment a habit fails.
2. **Say how the idea reads it,** in a sentence, before the next one comes.
3. **Read the next one that way,** and the one after. The effect is in the repetitions.
4. **Look at who else reads it.** If everyone around reads it the old way, the new reading needs their company, or a place where it is shared.

Each [theory](/theories) on this site ends with a way out of a loop. Read this way, a way out is an idea of this kind: a new reading for the step in the loop that keeps recurring.

## Where this stops

The belonging study had 92 students. A later trial of an online version with 26,911 students at 22 universities raised the rate at which students finished their first year full time, mainly among groups that have historically finished at lower rates, and only where the college gave those students chances to belong [9]. The mindset study's effect was small and held only in some schools. Miller and C'de Baca's accounts are told in retrospect, and the insight studies measured recall of magic-trick solutions over two weeks. None of them followed a person for a lifetime. What they show is narrower than "ideas change lives": a new reading, applied to an event that keeps recurring, changed outcomes over years, while words met once and never used changed little.
