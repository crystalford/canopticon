Every theory on this site has a section called Protocol: a numbered list of what to do. Writing the last one, on how to find the paragraphs an argument cannot lose, the protocol turned out to be the part I used. I ran it on my own paper and it told me what the paper rested on. The theory explained why the steps work. The steps did the work.

That is an old pattern in how research reaches the world, and it suggests what this site's new section, [Methods](/methods), is for.

## Known and not done

In 2003 Elizabeth McGlynn and colleagues reviewed the medical records of a random sample of adults in twelve American cities against 439 measures of recommended care for 30 conditions and for prevention. The adults received 54.9% of the care the evidence recommended [1]. Every one of the measures was care already recommended; the gap was in doing it.

The time it takes research to reach practice is often given as seventeen years. A review of 23 studies that tried to measure such lags found them hard to compare, because they measured different things at different points [2]. The figure is uncertain; the gap it points at is well documented.

## The same knowledge as a few steps

In 2009 a team working with the World Health Organization introduced a 19-item surgical safety checklist in eight hospitals in eight cities, in high-, middle- and low-income countries. Across 3,733 patients before and 3,955 after, deaths fell from 1.5% to 0.8%, and inpatient complications from 11.0% to 7.0% [3]. Three years earlier, a project in 103 intensive care units, mostly in Michigan, had introduced five evidence-based steps for placing central lines; bloodstream infections from the lines fell by up to 66% and stayed down for the 18 months measured [4].

Neither checklist contained a new finding. Confirming the patient and the operation, giving antibiotics before the incision, washing hands, using full sterile barriers: each step was already known. What changed was the form. The knowledge was put into short steps, in order, that anyone on the team could run.

## Why steps are held better

People overrate how well they understand how things work, and find out only when they try to explain. Leonid Rozenblit and Frank Keil measured this illusion across twelve studies and found it far stronger for explanations of how things work than for procedures, facts or stories [5]. A procedure is a list of steps in order, and people judge their knowledge of one accurately. That is the form a checklist gives a finding.

The [mechanism chain](/theories/the-mechanism-chain) proposes holding a mechanism the same way, as an ordered chain of parts and actions. A method does it for an idea.

## Instruments carry theory

Philosophers of science have said something similar about instruments. Gaston Bachelard argued that scientific instruments are theories made material [6]. Davis Baird developed the point in *Thing Knowledge*, arguing that instruments can carry and express knowledge themselves, from Faraday's first electric motor to the spectrometer [7]. A thermometer holds a theory of heat that its user does not have to work out again to take a reading.

## A method

A method on this site is a theory in that form. It has the steps a person can follow, and the exact instruction to give a language model, which can follow the same steps on any piece of writing in a few minutes. The instruction is the instrument: the theory made into something that can be run without rederiving it each time. Each method also downloads as a skill for Claude, so it can be installed and used where the work is done.

## Steps travel without their reasons

The same property has a cost. In 2010 Ontario required every hospital to use a surgical safety checklist. David Urbach and colleagues compared about 109,000 operations in the three months before each hospital adopted it with about 106,000 in the three months after. Deaths went from 0.71% to 0.65%, and complications from 3.86% to 3.82%; neither change was significant [8]. The steps reached every operating room, and the effect did not follow them.

A procedure carries the knowledge that fits in its steps. Why each step matters, when to depart from it and where it does not apply all stay with the theory.

## Kept together

So every method here sits next to its theory. The method page gives the steps and the instruction; the theory page gives the evidence, graded, and the case against. Each method also ends with a section called Where it fails, drawn from its theory's counter-evidence. The steps are what people run, and the theory is how they know when to stop.
