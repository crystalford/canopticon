In the evening there is a container in the sink from lunch. Washing it would take two minutes. Leaving it until morning seems fine: there will be time, and it is the same two minutes either way. In the morning, with lunch to pack and somewhere to be, the container is in the way, and the question is why it was left. The next evening the same container is left again.

Nothing about the task changed overnight. What changed is the state of the person deciding about it.

## Deciding for someone else

In a series of experiments, Emily Pronin and colleagues asked people to make decisions for themselves now, for themselves in the future, and for another person: how much of a disgusting drink to consume for a study, how much time to spend tutoring other students during exam week. Decisions for the future self looked like decisions for a stranger, and both differed from decisions for the present self [1]. The researchers traced the difference to the present self being uniquely influenced by what it was feeling at the time. The effect appeared for real decisions and disappeared for hypothetical ones.

At night, the present self feels the cost of washing the container: getting up, running the water, the small reluctance. The morning self's situation is described, and not felt. The decision weighs a felt cost against an imagined one.

## The feeling that is missing

George Loewenstein called this a hot–cold empathy gap. People in a calm, "cold" state fail to appreciate how a "hot" state, such as hunger, fatigue or pressure, will shape what they want and do [2]. The morning rush is a hot state. From a quiet evening, it is hard to imagine how much a dirty container will matter at 7 a.m.

A second error adds to it. Across seven experiments, Gal Zauberman and John Lynch found that people expect to have more spare time in the future than they have now, and they expect this more for time than for money [3]. Tomorrow morning looks roomier from the evening than it will be when it arrives.

## The plan that quiets the evening

Unfinished tasks usually stay on the mind. E. J. Masicampo and Roy Baumeister found that an unfulfilled goal produced intrusive thoughts and interfered with unrelated work, and that making a specific plan for it removed those effects. Their account: once a plan is made, the drive to finish the goal is suspended, and it resumes at the time the plan names [4].

"I'll do it in the morning" is such a plan. It buys a quiet evening by moving the pressure to the morning, where it arrives together with every other task deferred the same way. The morning feels crowded partly because it was booked the night before.

The trade has a known shape. In two semester-long studies of students, those who procrastinated reported less stress and illness early in the term and more late in the term, were sicker overall, and got lower grades [5]. The short-term relief is real, and so is the later cost. A review of the research argues that procrastination is driven by repairing mood in the moment, with the cost passed on to the future self [6].

## Why the lesson does not stick

The morning's frustration should be enough to change the evening's decision, and it rarely is. One reason is that people misjudge their own future behaviour. In Ted O'Donoghue and Matthew Rabin's model of self-control, people who are naive about their future selves keep putting off tasks with an immediate cost, because each time they expect to do it at the next opportunity [7].

A second reason is that a feeling which has passed is hard to recall with its force. Loren Nordgren and colleagues found that people who were not fatigued underestimated how much fatigue had shaped behaviour, their own included, and could not correct for it even when told to [8]. By evening, the morning's pressure is remembered as a fact without its weight. The evening self knows the morning was annoying, and does not feel why.

People are not simply inclined to delay. In experiments on carrying buckets, people often chose to pick up the nearer bucket and carry it farther, finishing a small step early even at extra physical cost; the researchers called this pre-crastination and linked it to wanting to clear things from working memory [9]. The wish to clear small tasks is there. It is felt most strongly when the tasks are present and pressing, which at night they are not.

## A rule instead of a decision

If the problem is that each evening's decision is made without the morning's feeling, the remedy is to stop deciding each evening. Kris Kirby and Barbarose Guastello offered people a choice between a smaller reward soon and a larger one later, as the first of five identical choices to be made over several weeks. When people had to choose for all five at once, most chose the larger rewards. In the first experiment, a third of those allowed to choose each time on its own also reversed their earlier preference and chose the larger reward once they saw the choice as part of a series [10]. Seeing tonight's choice as the choice for every night changes it.

A specific if-then plan helps too. A meta-analysis of 94 tests found that plans of the form "when situation X arises, I will do Y" had a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals [11]. "When I finish dinner, I wash the container" is that kind of plan. It works because it is decided once, in advance, and then carried out by the situation instead of by fresh reasoning.

The morning's feeling can also be used where it occurs. The tasks that cause frustration on waking are the ones worth settling for good: written down that morning, while the pressure is felt, as a rule for that evening.

## Where this stops

Most of these findings come from laboratory studies with students, often about money, food or set tasks, not household chores. The plan-making study is one paper, and the visceral-drive studies concerned hunger and fatigue more than time pressure. The procrastination review measures correlations across many studies. The container in the sink is an illustration. The argument is that a common, small, repeated delay has a known structure: the decision is made in a state that lacks the feeling that would change it, the plan to do it later removes the feeling until morning, and a standing rule avoids the problem by not leaving the decision to the evening.
