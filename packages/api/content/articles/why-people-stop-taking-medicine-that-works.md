About half of the people prescribed a medicine for a long-term condition stop taking it, or take it only part of the time, within a year or two. A pooled analysis of 20 studies and 376,162 patients taking drugs that prevent heart disease found adherence, measured from prescription refills, of 57% after a median of two years, and 50% among people who had not had a heart attack [1]. Electronic pill bottles tell the same story for blood pressure: about half of the patients prescribed a blood-pressure drug had stopped taking it within one year [2].

Side effects explain less of this than most people would guess. In the heart-disease analysis, adherence barely differed between drug classes with very different side effects, and the authors took that to suggest that side effects are not the main cause [1].

## What is the most common reason people stop?

When people are asked, the most common answer is that they felt better. In World Health Organization surveys of 13 countries, feeling better was the main reason people gave for stopping an antidepressant, at 46.6% of those who stopped, far ahead of the medicine not working (18.5%) or worries such as dependence (20.0%) [3].

The same belief shows up across conditions. Among 198 adults hospitalised for asthma, 53% said they had asthma only when they had symptoms, and those who held that belief had about one-third lower odds of using their preventer inhaler when they felt well [4]. In a classic study of high blood pressure, a condition that usually has no symptoms at all, many patients believed they could tell when their pressure was up, and those who thought of the condition as something that comes and goes were more likely to drop out of treatment [5].

The researchers behind the asthma study reduced the problem to one question for clinicians to ask: "Do you think you have asthma all of the time, or only when you are having symptoms?" [4] People who answer "only when I have symptoms" treat a standing condition as a series of attacks.

## Why does feeling better lead to stopping?

For many long-term conditions, the symptom is what keeps the routine going. It reminds a person to take the dose, and the relief that follows rewards it. A medicine that works removes the symptom, so it removes both the reminder and the reward. The routine then has nothing to prompt it.

Belief follows the same path. Across 94 studies, people who believed a medicine was necessary for them were more likely to keep taking it [6]. A person who feels well has less reason to believe the medicine is necessary, because the evidence they can see, their own symptoms, now says there is nothing to treat. The usual name for the mistake is the remission-as-cure error: reading "under control while I keep doing this" as "fixed".

## Why don't people learn from the relapse?

The relapse usually arrives long after the stopping. In 31 randomised trials of people whose depression had responded to an antidepressant, 41% of those switched to a placebo relapsed, against 18% of those who continued, mostly over a year of follow-up [7].

Delay is one of the strongest obstacles to learning cause and effect. In laboratory experiments, people judge a link between an action and an outcome much less readily when the outcome is delayed, and the difficulty eases when they know how long a delay to expect [8]. Someone who stops a medicine and feels fine for weeks has, by their own experience, good evidence that stopping was harmless. When the symptom returns, the stopping is weeks in the past and the connection is easy to miss. Often it takes several rounds before the pattern is seen.

## When is stopping when you feel better the right call?

Sometimes it is. For antibiotics, a group of infectious-disease specialists and health psychologists argued in the BMJ that the long-standing advice to finish the course has little evidence behind it. They found no evidence, for any common bacterial infection, that stopping early increases the risk of resistance, and wrote that outside hospital "patients may be best advised to stop treatment when they feel better" [9]. The authors of the antidepressant surveys also caution that stopping early is not necessarily harmful, since many who stopped said they felt better [3].

What separates these cases is the cause. An infection that the body and the drug have cleared is gone. High blood pressure, persistent asthma and recurrent depression are not gone when the symptoms are; the medicine is holding them back, and the cause is still there. The useful question before stopping is whether the cause is still there, and that is a question for the prescriber, who can also say what stopping would look like and how long it would take to show.

## What helps people keep taking a medicine that works?

The strongest evidence points to habit. A meta-analysis of 771 trials of ways to improve adherence found that interventions built on habit, such as tying the dose to something done every day, did better than those that tried to change what people know or believe [10]. In a study of people with type 2 diabetes, the strength of their medication habit predicted whether they actually took it, measured with electronic pill bottles, while their beliefs about the medicine predicted it inconsistently [11].

That fits the mechanism. If the symptom is what prompts and rewards the dose, the dose stops when the symptom does. A dose tied to breakfast, to brushing teeth or to a phone alarm keeps going whether or not there is anything to feel. Knowing the timeline helps too: a person told "if you stop, it may be two or three months before you notice anything" is better placed to connect the stopping to the return [8].

## A name for the pattern

Health psychology has names for parts of this cycle: the belief that a condition is only present with symptoms, and the necessity beliefs that predict adherence. The whole loop, in which a treatment that works removes the symptom that drove it, stops, and lets an unchanged cause bring the symptom back, is what this publication calls self-extinguishing maintenance. The theory states it formally, with its evidence graded, at [Self-extinguishing maintenance](/theories/self-extinguishing-maintenance); an earlier article follows the same cycle through gum care, dry skin and other routines at [Self-extinguishing maintenance: treatments that stop because they are working](/articles/self-extinguishing-maintenance).

The practical point is simpler. For a long-term condition, feeling better while taking a medicine is what the medicine was meant to do, and it says nothing on its own about whether the condition has gone.
